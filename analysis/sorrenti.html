On Davide Sorrenti and the reality of “heroin chic”

The story of Davide Sorrenti is often told backwards. It begins with a phrase, “heroin chic,” and a young photographer who died in 1997, aged twenty. The rest of his life is then arranged around that ending: the photographs become evidence of an aesthetic, his friends become supporting characters in what may be called a cautionary tale, and his death is made to explain a fashion trend that was already in motion before he had a professional career.

The shorthand is convenient, sure, but it obscures almost everything that made Sorrenti interesting. He was a photographer, graffiti writer, skateboarder, aspiring clothing designer and an unusually observant participant in downtown New York life. He was also the son and brother of people already established in fashion, and he benefited from access that most teenagers could only have dreamed of. And he lived with beta thalassemia, a serious inherited blood disorder that shaped his everyday life, his relationships and, ultimately, the circumstances surrounding his death. His photographs sometimes approached drug use directly, but they were part of a much larger record of friends, streets, bedrooms, parties and the ordinary business of being young in New York City in the 1990s.

Sorrenti did not invent “heroin chic,” and it would be equally misleading to present him as an innocent outsider whose work had no relationship to the culture being discussed, of course. He occupied a bit of an awkward position between a youth scene and the fashion industry that was learning how to package its appearance. The tragedy is that, after his death, what was a complicated life was reduced to a useful symbol in a debate that the industry, the press and politicians were already having.

1976–1988: Naples, New York and a life organised around illness

Davide Sorrenti was born in Naples on July 9, 1976, into a family already close to the worlds of art and photography. His mother, Francesca Sorrenti, worked as a stylist and photographer; his older brother, Mario, would become an internationally recognised fashion photographer, and his sister Vanina also pursued photography. Cameras, photographs and creative work were familiar parts of the family environment. Davide grew up with an unusually direct view of how images were made, and with people around him who understood the fashion business.

His childhood was also shaped by beta thalassemia major, a hereditary blood disorder that prevents the body from producing enough healthy haemoglobin. The condition requires regular blood transfusions, and those transfusions bring their own complications: excess iron accumulates in the body and must be managed with chelation treatment. In the documentary See Know Evil, family and friends describe a routine of hospital visits, transfusions and an infusion pump used overnight. They also remember the pain, the physical limitations and the effort Davide made to keep much of this out of view.

Francesca has recalled being told in Italy that her son’s prognosis was extremely poor. In 1982, after separating from Davide’s father, she moved with her three children to New York, where he could receive specialist care. The move was an act of practical determination. The family’s creative life and eventual proximity to the fashion industry matter to this story, although the immediate reason for crossing the Atlantic was medical.

The illness did not define Davide’s personality, though it inevitably shaped the terms on which he lived. Friends interviewed in See Know Evil remember a boy who could be funny, argumentative, restless and occasionally reckless, with interests that ranged well beyond photography. He skateboarded, painted graffiti, listened to hip-hop, followed art and fashion, and had an enthusiasm for opera that could catch a lot of people by surprise. His mother has also described a melancholy streak in his work, something she understood as part of his temperament rather than a pose constructed for the camera.

The downtown years: ARGUE and S.K.E.

By his early teens, Davide had found a social world in downtown Manhattan. He adopted the graffiti tag ARGUE and became part of a loose group of friends whose shared interests included skateboarding, graffiti, music, clothes and the usual experiments in adolescent mischief. The group used the initials S.K.E., first jokingly expanded as “Some Kids Envy” and later as “See Know Evil.” Familiar, right? The name eventually became associated with the documentary about Davide and the people around him.

The world captured in the film is lively, sometimes affectionate and sometimes plainly chaotic. His friends recall all sorts of subway excursions, tagging, parties, accidents and arguments. The stories can be funny in retrospect, although they also show how little separation there was between youthful bravado and genuine danger. It is tempting to turn S.K.E. into a romantic picture of an underground family, a band of downtown outsiders living by their own rules. The film gives a less tidy impression: a group of young people who were close, yes, who could be careless with one another, and whose lives gradually moved in different directions.

Davide’s bedroom became one of the centres of this social life. It was filled with photographs, pictures torn from magazines, drawings and other fragments of visual culture. Friends remember him documenting the people around him almost constantly. And his camera was a way of keeping track of his world: a friend leaning against a wall, someone asleep in a car, a night out, a face caught without preparation, and many more. That habit would remain central to his photography. The pictures were often made among people he knew, in places where he already felt at home.

Before the label: fashion photography in the early 1990s

The visual shift later grouped under the name “heroin chic” did not exactly begin with Davide Sorrenti. During the late 1980s and early 1990s, photographers, stylists and magazine editors were already moving away from the highly produced glamour that had dominated much of the previous decade. Some of the new work favored available light, ordinary interiors, young people who looked as though they had wandered into the frame, and an intimacy that could feel closer to a personal snapshot rather than a traditional fashion picture.

Corinne Day’s photographs of Kate Moss for The Face in 1990, and her subsequent work for British Vogue, became important reference points for this change. Day’s pictures were often discussed for their apparent informality and their refusal of conventional polish. The work of Nan Goldin and Larry Clark offered other, distinct precedents for photographers interested in youth, intimacy, sex, drugs and the less flattering parts of everyday life. These artists did not share one program, and fashion photography did not simply copy them; their work nevertheless formed part of a broader visual conversation about realism, documentary photography and the appeal of an unvarnished image.

Mario Sorrenti’s work also placed the family within this changing fashion landscape. His 1993 Calvin Klein Obsession campaign with Kate Moss became a major commercial image of the period. Its stripped-back black-and-white photographs helped bring a spare, intimate visual language into mainstream advertising. Back then, Davide was still a teenager. By the time he began taking professional assignments, the industry had already shown that it could, naturally, turn apparent spontaneity and fragility into a highly marketable look.

This chronology matters, though: it does not mean Davide had no influence, or that his photographs should be detached from the culture that surrounded them. It does mean, however, that the later story in which one young photographer supposedly created an entire fashion aesthetic is historically untenable. As fashion historian Rebecca Arnold has argued in her writing on “heroin chic,” the label gathered together a much wider and more contradictory set of images and attitudes, including a strand of fashion photography that had developed before the controversy reached its peak.

1994–1995: Danücht, “Models Suck” and the camera

Davide came to photography through an environment in which cameras were readily available. His mother worked in the industry, Mario was already successful, and the family’s professional connections gave him access to people and opportunities that were not available to most aspiring photographers his age; that advantage should be acknowledged plainly. It does not, however, explain the way he used a camera, or the friendships and visual instincts that gave the work its character.

Davide was drawn to photographs that felt immediate and personal. In See Know Evil, people close to him describe his admiration for documentary photographers, including Robert Frank, and his impatience with conventional fashion production. He was interested in photographing people as they were, in their own surroundings, rather than always manufacturing an elaborate scene around them. Fashion assignments could pay for film and give him a platform, while his personal photographs remained the work he cared about most.

In 1995, Davide and friends also experimented with making clothes under the name Danücht, reportedly derived from the phrase “da new shit.” The project belonged to the same do-it-yourself world as their graffiti and photography: screen-printed shirts and hoodies, made for friends and circulated within their own scene. The group’s “Models Suck” stickers, pasted over Calvin Klein advertisements, were another joke at the expense of the fashion world whose imagery surrounded them. According to the recollections in See Know Evil, this intervention was sufficiently well placed that Vogue wondered whether the defacement might itself be a Calvin Klein publicity stunt. (It was not.)

Davide’s entry into editorial photography came through a portfolio that reflected this sensibility. He approached advertising executive David Lipman with a scrapbook of photographs, contact sheets and personal material rather than a conventional presentation, and Lipman recognised something in the pictures and helped him find work. Davide went on to shoot for magazines including Interview and i-D, and his fashion photographs often retained the looseness of his personal work.

There was a tension built into this approach, however. Davide wanted to make pictures that felt like reportage, and he disliked the machinery of glamour. Yet the photographs were still commissioned, edited, published and sold by an industry with its own commercial interests. An image can begin as a record of a particular person or friendship, but ultimately acquire a different meaning once it appears in a magazine or an advertisement. The photographer’s intention matters, but it cannot control every use to which an image is put.

1995–1996: Jaime King, friendship and the fashion machine

One of the most important people in Davide’s final years was the model Jaime King, then often credited professionally as James King. They were close, and he photographed her repeatedly. In See Know Evil, King describes a connection rooted partly in their experiences of illness and pain; she had undergone multiple operations as a child, and Davide had spent much of his life managing thalassemia. Each recognised something in the other that was difficult to explain to people who had not grown up with chronic medical problems.

“I was very sick my whole life as a child, and I had multiple, multiple surgeries. So when I met Davide, I felt like there was one other person that kind of understood what it was like for me when I was a kid growing up. He had a solitary nature that I think came with his disease. We connected on that level.”

— Jaime King, See Know Evil

His photographs of King are intimate without being especially theatrical. She appears resting, distracted, tired or simply occupied with something outside the frame. The pictures are sometimes described as romantic, but their emotional force often comes from how little they seem to demand from their subject. They belong to a larger body of work in which Davide photographed friends as people he knew, rather than as anonymous figures performing a fashion concept.

The fashion environment around them was considerably less innocent, notably. King has spoken about being introduced to drugs while she was still a very young model, and Francesca Sorrenti has described an industry in which adults sometimes enabled or tolerated drug use among teenagers so long as the young models remained available for work. Although her account is not proof that every agency or shoot operated in the same way, it does, however, point to a serious imbalance of power: minors were working in adult spaces, often far from their families, while the people responsible for their welfare could also be invested in keeping them on the job.

The accounts of Davide and King’s relationship are not entirely consistent, so there isn't a single neat explanation for it. Some friends remember Davide as believing he could help King with her drug use; King has also recalled that he could be one of the people who confronted her about it. Francesca has also said that the two did not bond over drugs and that Davide argued with King when he realised she was high. Either way, reducing their relationship to a story about two young people using drugs together would flatten the testimony of the people who knew them.

Additionally, Francesca has disputed the idea that heroin was a defining part of Davide’s life. In interviews, she has said that he tried it only a handful of times, that two experiences resulted in hospitalisation, and that he sometimes turned to drugs in an attempt to relieve physical pain. These are her recollections and should be understood as such, though it is clear that he experimented with heroin, and that drug use to an extent was present in parts of his social world.

1997: Davide’s death and the making of a public story

Davide died in February 1997, aged twenty, after travelling to Mexico and returning to New York in very poor physical condition. The exact medical circumstances have been described differently in later accounts, and the public record available through interviews does not justify reconstructing a precise toxicological sequence. Francesca has said that he had gone several weeks without a necessary transfusion, was profoundly weak, and died in the context of complications from thalassemia. She has also disputed the description of his death as a straightforward heroin overdose, saying that the medical examiner told her the opiate concentration was too low to account for his death on its own.

In a 2019 interview with Vogue Italia, Francesca recalled the anger and shock she felt when the death was immediately turned into a warning about heroin and fashion. She has described being told that the amount of opiates found in Davide’s body was “hardly enough to kill a fly.” In a separate interview with Vogue in the United States, she described his death as kidney failure related to thalassemia.

The phrase “heroin chic” became attached to the story in the immediate aftermath. Francesca has recalled that, at Davide’s wake, Interview editor Ingrid Sischy told her, “This is heroin, this isn’t chic.” Whether treated as the origin of the phrase or as one of the moments that gave it force, the remark captured the argument that was about to overtake the photography. The label was applied to a broad range of images, some of which depicted drug use directly, but many of which did not.

On May 20, 1997, The New York Times published Amy M. Spindler’s article “A Death Tarnishes Fashion’s ‘Heroin Look’”. The article helped make Davide’s death a focal point for a wider debate about fashion imagery and the industry’s responsibility. The following day, President Bill Clinton condemned the “glorification of heroin” in fashion photographs during an address to the U.S. Conference of Mayors. His remarks framed the issue as a matter of public morality, and Sorrenti’s death quickly became part of the political case against the fashion world.

“You do not need to glamorize addiction to sell clothes. The glorification of heroin is not creative, it’s destructive. It’s not beautiful; it’s ugly. And this is not about art; it’s about life and death.”

— President Bill Clinton, remarks to the U.S. Conference of Mayors, May 21, 1997

The public response made for a powerful story: a young man had died, fashion had made an aesthetic out of apparent self-destruction, and the president was telling the industry to stop. But the chronology is more complicated than the political narrative suggested. A report in The Washington Post published on May 21 noted that some fashion-industry observers believed the look had already begun to recede six to twelve months earlier, before Clinton’s speech. The shift away from the more raw, gaunt aesthetic was underway as editors and advertisers looked for something else. Either way, Clinton’s remarks amplified the controversy, albeit they did not cause the end of the trend.

Francesca Sorrenti responded by speaking publicly about the conditions young models faced and by advocating for better protections. She challenged the fashion industry’s willingness to work with underage models while failing to take responsibility for their welfare. Her criticism was directed at the adults and institutions with the power to set conditions: agencies, bookers, photographers, clients and publishers. The irony was difficult to miss. The industry had profited from images of youth and vulnerability, then found it easier to condemn a dead young photographer than to examine the very same systems that had allowed teenagers to work in unsafe environments.

After the controversy: the work, the archive and the legacy

Sorrenti's photographs, ultimately, deserve to be looked at individually. Some are tender, some funny, some restless or deliberately confrontational. A few engage directly with drug culture. Others show friends, fashion, interiors and the texture of ordinary days. The fact that Davide sometimes photographed addiction does not establish that he endorsed it, just as the presence of a drug in a photograph does not tell us everything about the person behind the camera.

One example The Destruction of the Masses: Francesca has said that Davide made the photograph before he began using heroin, working with Jaime King and Vanina on an image concerned with young people caught up in addiction. Its existence does complicate the retrospective assumption that every photograph involving drugs must have been a direct expression of Davide’s own habits. It's silly. Images can be constructed, observed, staged or critical, but their subject matter alone cannot settle the question of the photographer’s intentions.

The documentary See Know Evil, directed by Charlie Curran, helped restore some of this context through interviews with Francesca, Mario, King and members of S.K.E., alongside home movies and archival material. Ultimately, its strongest contribution is to return Davide to a network of relationships and interests that the late-1990s coverage largely ignored.

His photograph archive has also continued to emerge through books and exhibitions. ArgueSKE 1994–1997 appeared in 2019, followed by Polaroids in 2020 and My Beautyfull Lyfe in 2022. In 2025, IDEA published Davide Sorrenti: Journals, Volume 1, 1994–1995, bringing together pages from his journals, photographs, contact sheets, drawings, stickers and other fragments of his everyday visual life.

It is, admittedly, tempting to draw a straight line from Davide’s photographs to contemporary streetwear, social-media nostalgia or the visual language of any particular fashion label. The evidence for direct influence is often thin, and the images belong to a wider history of skate photography, youth culture, documentary practice and independent fashion publishing. What can be said with more confidence is that the qualities his work valued, be it their personal proximity, imperfect light, or just the feeling of being inside a real social world, remain quite attractive to photographers and audiences.

There is little to be gained from turning Davide into a saint. He had his privileges, his flaws, and his share of bad decisions; he experimented with drugs, could be difficult, and was hardly untouched by the world that later made him its cautionary tale. But he was also a young photographer with a distinct way of seeing, part of a close-knit downtown community, and a person whose life extended far beyond the circumstances of his death. The fashion industry had spent years borrowing from that world, finding ways to make its clothes, faces and attitudes commercially desirable. When the controversy came, it proved easier to make Davide's death the centre of the discussion, than to confront the adults and institutions that had helped create the conditions around him.

“Heroin chic” may remain a convenient name for that particular moment in fashion, but it tells us very little about the people who lived through it. Davide's photographs hold onto something messier and far more human: the friendships, the jokes, the intimacy, the recklessness, all the ordinary moments that rarely make it into retrospective accounts. They are a record of a life and a community, rather than an illustration of a scandal. He deserves to be remembered on those terms, too.
