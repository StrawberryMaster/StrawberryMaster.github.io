Chapter Summary

It is a good day.

Chapter Text

It was amazing that at some point, after the shouting and silence that had followed, they had both managed to fall asleep again, even though neither of them had made much of an effort to get comfortable. Pete had eventually crawled back onto his mattress, while Gary had settled on the floor beside the desk. That was, well, definitely not very comfortable. They had woken briefly when a car passed outside, exchanged no more than a sleepy glance, drifted off again, and left the rest of the early morning to pass.

When Pete finally opened his eyes for the second time, he lay on his back, shifted slightly beneath the covers, then turned his head toward the corner of the room. Gary was still asleep, so he hadn’t left on his own or anything this time. That was good! He was curled up between the wardrobe and Pete’s desk with his knees drawn toward his chest. Every so often, his upper lip twitched faintly, though whether he was dreaming or simply reacting to the noise outside was anyone’s guess. Pete assumed it was the first option. He wondered what Gary dreamed about…

He watched him for a while. It was difficult to know what to do with Gary when he wasn’t actively making things difficult. But eventually, Pete pushed the blankets aside and sat up, rubbing the sleep from his eyes. The clock on his bedside table read 7:19, which meant his father was probably already downstairs… and his mother wouldn’t be far behind. Pete glanced at Gary again before carefully stepping around him.

Of course, he had no particular desire to explain why his guest was sleeping on the floor, especially not before breakfast, and the prospect of either parent asking questions about the shouting from earlier was even less appealing. Oh, God. Did they even hear it…?

It took him a few moments of thinking about it, before he decided to just face his fate. Pete eased his bedroom door open and slipped into the hallway, descending the staircase in his bare feet. It appeared the kitchen was still dim, with the morning light only just beginning to clear the roof of the neighboring garage. His father sat at the small breakfast table in a freshly ironed cream short-sleeve shirt and dark brown slacks, with his reading glasses perched low on his nose as he scanned the community bulletin. (He seemed vaguely interested.) And his mother stood near the sink, pouring hot water into a ceramic teapot, with her hair loosely tied back and a dish towel draped over one shoulder.

She looked up when Pete appeared in the doorway, and the slight furrow that formed between her eyebrows suggested that she had already noticed something was off. After all, he was still wearing yesterday’s clothes, his hair was a mess, and the dark circles beneath his eyes were probably doing him no favors.

“Morning, Peter,” she said, setting the kettle aside. “Did you sleep all right?”

“Fine,” Pete mumbled, leaning his shoulder against the doorframe. “Just woke up early.”

His father lowered the newspaper by an inch and peered over the top of his glasses. “You know, we thought we heard something around six,” he said. “Sounded like somebody shouting upstairs or something. I don’t think the neighbors are that loud, right?”

Pete felt his stomach tighten, though he managed to keep his expression more or less unchanged. He had spent most of his childhood learning how to make himself as unobtrusive as possible in this house, and as much as he wouldn’t admit it, his ability to lie without seeming too interested in the outcome was a bit of an interesting side effect. A shame it only really worked for his parents; he shrugged.

“Oh. That was just the pipes in the bathroom,” he said. “The hot water knob on the tub was, um, sticking again. I tried to turn it and it made that awful screeching noise.”

God, how could they even believe something like that? He could sense there was no anger in the question, but he didn’t think they’d believe him. Gary’s mud-crusted sneakers were almost certainly on the rubber mat, and he certainly hadn’t bothered cleaning them. Not only that, they were difficult to miss, and Pete suspected his mother had noticed them long before he came downstairs.

“Well, remind your father to look at the gasket this weekend,” she said with a sigh, setting the ceramic pot on its cork trivet. She hesitated, glancing toward the staircase. “And your friend is still upstairs?”

“Yeah,” he admitted, looking down at his bare feet. “He was pretty tired, so I told him he could crash for a bit.”

His father folded the newspaper and set it neatly beside his coffee mug. Pete glanced over, and they looked at each other for a few seconds. He knew Gary, of course. Everyone in the neighborhood knew Gary, although not necessarily for the same reasons. He’d watched him grow from an energetic, if occasionally troublesome, neighborhood kid into one hell of a teenager whose family gossip only made things worse. He also knew Gary’s father, which was probably reason enough to approach the entire situation with a degree of caution. Not to mention the whole ‘my friend got expelled’ incident…

For a moment, Pete wondered whether his father was going to ask what had happened upstairs, or perhaps why Gary had spent the night in the first place. Instead, he picked up his coffee, blew gently across its surface, and took a sip before pushing his chair back.

“Make sure he, uh, eats something that isn’t just sugar,” he said, standing and smoothing the front of his slacks. “And tell him that if he’s going to use the back bulkhead, he needs to latch the inner storm door. I mean, God, raccoons keep getting in there. Weasels, too, I’ve seen just about everything coming out of there.”

Pete blinked, caught off guard by the apparent absence of any further questions. Was it going to be this easy? Apparently so. The tightness in his chest eased a little, although he was careful not to let his relief show too obviously.

“I will. Thanks, Dad.”

“Have a good day, Peter.” His father grabbed his cardigan from the coat tree in the hallway, slipping it on as he headed toward the front door. “I have an important meeting at ten. Behave yourselves.”

The door shut behind him a minute later, leaving Pete standing in the kitchen with his mother. (By that point, his mom was now gathering her things for her morning yoga session.) She reminded him to put the dishes in the sink, gave him a quick kiss on the cheek, and left with her tote bag slung over one shoulder.

Pete made himself breakfast after that, and it was good to make sure Gary ate something too. He toasted a couple of English muffins, spread butter across them while they were still warm, and poured two glasses of orange juice before arranging everything on a plate. By the time he carried the food upstairs, the house had been empty for nearly twenty minutes.

He nudged his bedroom door open with his hip, trying his best to balance the plate carefully in both hands. (That was working, sort of…) Gary hadn’t moved much in the time he’d been gone. He was still curled up against the base of the wardrobe, his face half-buried in the wool of the sweater he’d apparently pulled over himself at some point during the morning. One of his knees stuck out from beneath the shirt, and his hair had become an even more impressive mess while he’d been sleeping. He never thought that was possible.

Pete set the plate down on the desk, followed by the glasses, and pulled his chair out with a soft-ish scrape against the floor. He sat with his elbows resting on his knees, looking over at Gary for a moment before deciding that, well, yes, waking him up was preferable to letting the food go cold.

“They’re gone,” he said quietly. “You can wake up now.”

Gary remained still for a few seconds, and Pete briefly wondered whether he’d have to repeat himself. To his relief, the sweater shifted, and one dark eye appeared beneath its collar, blinking slowly against the light filtering through the curtains.

“Did they… say anything?” Gary asked. His voice was somewhat hoarse with sleep.

“Dad said to latch the basement door so the raccoons don’t get in.” Pete reached for one of the muffins, breaking it in half before taking a bite. “And Mom said you should eat something.”

Gary stared at him, and his brow drew together as he processed this information. He slowly pulled his arms out of the sweater and pushed himself upright, leaning against the wardrobe. The exhaustion was still evident in his face, sure, but he seemed somewhat calmer.

“Your parents are completely unhinged,” he muttered.

Pete glanced at him over the rim of his glass. “I mean, my mom’s fine. And my dad’s just a librarian.”

“That’s what makes it worse.” Gary rubbed his face with both hands before dragging himself across the floorboards on his knees. He reached up, snatched the other half of the muffin from the plate, and took a bite. “See, normal people don’t act like that. They’re like pod people.”

“Eat your damn food, man.”

“I’m eating.”

“Then stop talking.”

Gary gave him a rather unimpressed look, but he didn’t argue, and for a while, the two of them ate in relative silence. Surprisingly, the rest of the day passed in much the same way, without any particular plans or even a convincing attempt to make them. What else were they supposed to do anyway?

Considering this, they spent most of the morning drifting between the bed and the floor, occasionally putting on music or flipping through whatever magazines Pete had left lying around. Gary complained about the selection of CDs, then proceeded to listen to several of them anyway, offering his unsolicited opinions about the bands and their respective levels of artistic worth. Who could’ve thought he was a fan of Japan? (The band, not the country.) Pete mostly ignored him, although he found himself paying attention whenever Gary started talking about something that wasn’t directly related to insulting him. It was better than nothing.

One could easily think this was just an ordinary summer day. And maybe it was, regardless of what they could do or not do later on. By the afternoon, Gary had commandeered the better half of Pete’s bed, stretching out on his stomach with a fairly recent copy of an i-D magazine propped beneath his chin, while Pete sat at his desk, fiddling with his iPod and occasionally looking over his shoulder to see what Gary was reading. Every so often, Gary would make some passing remark about an article or complain that Pete’s room was unbearably boring, and Pete would tell him that he was welcome to leave whenever he liked. Curiously, Gary never took him up on it.

This sight brought a strange ache to Pete’s chest, and he would turn back to his drawings before he could dwell on it for too long. He had thought about this before, but it had been quite a while since they’d last spent any real amount of time together in a room without some sort of ulterior motive. For all the indifference Gary seemed determined to maintain, though, Pete could see how much effort it was taking him to sit still.

For example, whenever the clock in the downstairs hallway chimed the passing of another hour, Gary’s eyes would often flick toward the window, and the muscles along his jaw would tighten before he returned to whatever he was pretending to read. His shoulders remained tense even when the rest of him was sprawled comfortably across the floor, and he had a habit of running his thumb along the edge of the magazine whenever the conversation threatened to lapse into silence. Pete knew those little habits well enough to recognize that something was bothering him, although he suspected Gary would sooner swallow a handful of thumbtacks than admit it.

And obviously, he could understand why Gary was dreading it. Whatever had happened between them, Jimmy remained the person Gary had spent so long trying to defeat, and the idea of walking into a room with him and… not doing anything back would be, quite frankly, humiliating. There was also the question of what would happen if the meeting went well. It would be great, sure, but… Gary had spent so long organizing his life around opposition, or finding enemies wherever he needed them and turning every slight into another reason to keep fighting, that Pete wasn’t sure what he would do if he discovered there was nothing left to fight for.

Perhaps Gary wasn’t sure either. Pete had no intention of asking him about it, though, partly because he doubted he’d get an honest answer, and partly because he wasn’t sure he wanted to hear one. By seven in the evening, the light outside had begun to fade, and the sky beyond the curtains had a rather violet-ish color. Pete finished the last few lines of his drawing, one of a comically large giraffe, set his pencil down, and leaned back in his chair, stretching his arms above his head until his shoulders popped.

“Right, uh,” he said, glancing at the clock. “We should probably get ready.”

Gary, who had spent the better part of the previous forty-five minutes pretending to be fascinated by an article about nineteenth-century steam locomotives, let the magazine slide onto his chest and stared up at the ceiling as though Pete had just informed him that the house was on fire.

“Can’t we just say we went?” he asked, throwing an arm over his eyes. “I’ll tell you he was an asshole, which he is, you can tell me I was a prick, we can argue about it, and then we go to sleep. Saves us the commute, at least…”

“Get up, Gary. You promised me.”

“Oh, you know I was under the influence of, erm, severe emotional duress,” Gary protested, though he rolled onto his side and pushed himself up from the floor. “I’m pretty sure that invalidates any agreement made under those conditions.”

“Funny how you’re only bringing that up now.” Pete shook his head and crossed to the closet, pulling the folding doors open. Gary followed at a considerably more reluctant pace, leaning against the doorframe while he surveyed the contents. He seemed somewhat disappointed.

“You really have no taste, Pete,” he remarked. “God, it’s like a middle-aged accountant threw up in here.”

“Then find something that fits you and shut up.”

It took several minutes of rummaging through hangers and shifting piles of clothes that Pete had been meaning to organize since the beginning of summer, but Gary eventually found something he deemed acceptable. It was a charcoal-grey, long-sleeved button-up shirt that Pete’s aunt had given him for Christmas two years earlier, an expensive piece of slightly stiff cotton that had remained at the back of his closet because the shoulders were far too broad for his narrow frame. Gary held it up against himself and examined it in the mirror, turning slightly to get a better look at the fit.

“Well, well,” he said, his expression brightening up a bit. “I don’t actually look half-bad for once.”

“I’m glad you’re enjoying yourself.”

Gary pulled off the navy sweater he’d been wearing and tossed it onto the bed before slipping into the button-up. The difference in their builds was immediately apparent; the shirt sat comfortably across Gary’s shoulders, following the lean lines of his torso without bunching around the waist. He rolled the cuffs twice, smoothed down the collar, and gave his reflection one final inspection, looking rather pleased with himself.

Pete himself settled on a mustard-yellow and black striped knit sweater that had belonged to his older cousin, along with a pair of plain black trousers and his cleanest canvas sneakers. The outfit was (probably?) a little too warm for the weather, but Pete had never been particularly good at dressing for the temperature. (That and he preferred having something familiar to wear when he knew he was going to be uncomfortable.) Besides, it’s not like he was dressing for anyone in particular…

Once they were ready, they made their way downstairs, moving quietly past the living room where Pete’s parents had settled in to watch the evening news. They slipped through the kitchen and out the back door, careful not to attract attention or invite questions about where they were going; luckily, neither of them seemed to notice. By now, the air outside had cooled considerably, and Pete breathed a little easier as they crossed the backyard.

He had almost reached the driveway when he noticed a bicycle propped against the stone retaining wall beside his mother’s flowerbed. He slowed, then stopped altogether, as his eyes settled on the reinforced aluminum frame and its wheels. Near the rear brake caliper, there was a funny neon-green sticker in the shape of a twelve-sided die, peeling slightly at one corner.

Hmm. Pete knew that sticker, or at least had heard of it. He was pretty sure that he heard Melvin talk about it in the basement of the comic shop yesterday afternoon, along with the several other modifications he made that apparently made the bicycle a masterpiece of modern engineering. Maybe he planned to include it in his will or something. Aside from that, though, it looked like a normal bike. But certainly Melvin’s, and not his, right…?

Pete looked back at the bicycle, then at Gary. At this point, he could practically see him preparing his defenses, with his shoulders drawing back and his mouth settling into the beginnings of a smirk. Oh yeah, I stole the bike from that nerd prick, maybe. Whose bike? I don’t see no bike here, perhaps. Either way, it was probably going to be a long argument, and they were already running late.

He let out a quiet sigh, reached for the rear metal stunt pegs, and swung one leg over the frame.

“You’re pedaling,” he said, settling himself behind the seat.

Gary blinked at him, apparently satisfied that Pete had decided to not comment on his newly-acquired bike. The relief lasted only a moment before his smirk returned, although the pink in his cheeks hadn’t entirely faded.

“Hop on, Head Boy,” he said, placing one foot on a pedal. “And try not to break my suspension.”

Pete tightened his grip on the pegs as Gary pushed off, and the bicycle wobbled slightly before he found his balance and began pedaling down the driveway. They turned onto the road just as the last of the sunlight disappeared behind the trees.

Little did Pete know that an abysmally slow Gary would help make their ride take about twenty minutes.

The breeze coming off the ocean had turned noticeably colder by the time they arrived. Pete balanced on the rear pegs of Melvin’s bike, with his hands resting on Gary’s shoulders as they rattled over the cracked asphalt, trying not to put too much weight on him whenever the wheels hit a pothole. Gary had barely spoken since they’d left the neighborhood, and although Pete couldn’t see his face from behind, the stiffness of his back suggested that he was having a considerably worse time than he wanted to admit.

Pete had spent most of the ride wondering whether he ought to say something encouraging, but every possibility that came to mind was either too earnest or likely to annoy Gary into cycling directly into a lamppost. Maybe that would be interesting. In the end, he’d decided to leave him alone. Whether that was a good decision was beyond him, but the closer they got to the beach house, the more Gary seemed to retreat into himself.

His shoulders rose whenever they passed a group of people on the pavement, and his hands tightened around the handlebars whenever the road curved toward the shore. Pete was beginning to regret not having come up with a better plan than simply showing up and hoping that Jimmy wouldn’t immediately throw them both off the porch. Great thinking…

When they finally reached the beach house, Pete was relieved to see that its lights were on. It stood close enough to the street that they hadn’t needed to follow any winding road, thankfully. There were no other bicycles or vehicles parked outside, but also no music and, to his relief… no sign of any of the other Bullies. If he was lucky enough, there would be no sign of Zoe here either. As far as Pete could tell, Jimmy was home alone.

Gary slowed as they approached the wooden ramp leading up to the front doors, then brought the bicycle to a stop near its base. The front tire skidded slightly over the grit before he planted one foot on the ground and let the kickstand drop. For a moment, he remained seated, staring at the illuminated windows. Ten or fifteen seconds later, he then dismounted, shoved his hands into his pockets, and looked at Pete with an expression that was vaguely casual.

“We can still leave,” he muttered, pitching his voice low enough that the wind nearly carried it away. “Y-you know, ride down to the pier, get some fries, throw rocks at the seagulls. Or people— yeah, yeah, that sounds like a much better use of the evening, if you ask me.”

Pete stepped off the rear pegs and straightened his sweater, brushing a little dust from the front before looking up at him. “We’re going inside, Gary,” he said, keeping his voice gentle as he caught his eye. “Come on, man. You said you’d try.”

Although Gary’s mouth tightened at the reminder, he followed Pete up the ramp. Pete reached the door first and paused to collect himself, glancing back once to make sure Gary hadn’t changed his mind and wandered off down the street. Amazingly, he was still there, standing just a few feet behind him.

Well, it’s time.

Pete took a breath and knocked three times against the heavy oak door. They could hear some footsteps moving around inside, followed by the clunk of the deadbolt turning. When the door swung inward, it was the one and only Jimmy Hopkins, no real surprises here. He was wearing a faded denim vest over a black T-shirt, and there was also a, uh, smear of grey dust across his hands. He also held a half-eaten green apple in one of them.

“Petey,” Jimmy said, breaking into a grin. “Hey, I was wondering if you were gonna show up eventually. Heh, you know, thought your mom put you on house arrest or some—”

He stopped when he noticed Gary over Pete’s shoulder.

The smile faded gradually, his gaze settled on Gary, and for a second Pete thought Jimmy might simply close the door again. Instead, Jimmy’s jaw tightened and his posture shifted almost imperceptibly. He was definitely surprised, even if he didn’t make it visible. Gary went equally still behind Pete. His hands remained buried in his pockets, although the fabric pulled taut around his knuckles, and he lifted his chin a fraction as he met Jimmy’s stare.

For several seconds, nobody said anything, all while Pete stood between them and wondered whether he’d made a spectacular mistake in bringing them together. After all, these were the same two boys who had nearly killed each other on the roof of Bullworth Academy, and even with some distance between that afternoon and the present, it was difficult to look at them standing across each other without remembering how badly things had gone. What was he thinking…?

Pete glanced from Jimmy to Gary, waiting for either of them to show some sign of common sense, and received absolutely nothing in return. He felt a bead of cold sweat trace its way down his spine. Say something, he told himself. Do your job. It looked like he was going to have to do this by himself for once.

“Hey, Jim,” Pete said at last; his voice came out rather brighter than he’d intended. He stepped forward a little, positioning himself more firmly in the doorway so that neither boy had an unobstructed view of the other. “So, uh, I brought company. And well, um…”

Jimmy looked down at him. He was incredulous at what he was seeing. “Pete,” he said slowly, “What is this?”

“It’s a truce!” Pete replied, speaking quickly before Jimmy could interrupt him. “Um, a real one. I-I’m serious, Jimmy. I’m so tired of you two avoiding each other, and plotting against each other, and acting like, uh, you’re waiting for somebody to ring a bell so you can start punching each other again. School’s out, man. We’re in the middle of summer, and I thought we could, you know, get some quality time together. Like old times, right?”

Jimmy’s eyes narrowed slightly, and Pete hurried on before he could lose his nerve.

“I’m not asking you to be best friends or anything. God knows that would be a little much— yeah, yeah, it might be. But I just want you to sit down, talk if you feel like it, and try to get through the evening without turning it into another disaster. An hour at least, maybe two, perhaps three, Jimmy. That’s all I’m asking.”

Jimmy watched him for a moment, and Pete could see him trying to decide whether the whole thing was a joke. It would’ve made this much easier to understand, and he could go back to beating the crap out of Gary like old times. But those old times were long gone now. Eventually, his gaze moved past Pete again, settling on Gary, who still hadn’t said a word since they’d arrived. With his shoulders squared and his jaw clenched, he looked every bit as arrogant and miserable as Pete had expected him to. If Gary had been hoping to give the impression that he was very comfortable with the situation he was in, he was doing a remarkably poor job of it.

Chewing slowly, Jimmy took another bite of his apple while he considered them both. Then he let out a long breath through his nose and shook his head. “Wipe your feet,” he muttered, stepping back from the doorway and pulling the door open a little wider. “I just swept.”

Pete nearly sagged with relief. “Thanks, Jimmy.”

“Don’t thank me yet, Petey.”

He turned and walked into the living room before Pete could think of anything else to say, leaving the doorway open behind him. Pete looked back at Gary, who genuinely couldn’t quite believe he’d been allowed inside. His eyes flickered toward the dark stretch of sand beyond the street, and Pete wondered whether he was seriously considering making a run for it. Well, not this time… Pete reached out, caught him lightly by the elbow, and gave him an encouraging tug.

“Come on,” he murmured.

Gary glanced down at Pete’s hand before looking back at the open doorway. He hesitated for another second, then followed him inside, muttering something under his breath that Pete suspected was meant to be an insult, but didn’t quite have the energy to finish.

The beach house was considerably tidier than it had been since last time, for sure. The empty soda cans and burger wrappers that had accumulated around the coffee table were gone, and the floor had been swept clean, leaving only a few scuff marks near the couch. A stack of unremarkable comic books sat beside a small plastic toolbox on the table, and the faint smell of sawdust suggested that Jimmy had spent at least part of the afternoon fixing something around the house. At least the place wasn’t looking so neglected.

Jimmy had stopped beside an armchair, leaning back against its edge with his arms folded across his chest. He looked from Pete to Gary and back again, waiting for one of them to explain what was supposed to happen next. Gary, meanwhile, had stopped just inside the living room, about three paces beyond the doorway, with his hands still buried in his pockets and his weight balanced awkwardly between his feet. What was he supposed to do now?

When it became clear that neither of them seemed to actually do anything, Pete marched forward, caught Gary by the right wrist, and hauled his hand out of his pocket.

“Shake hands,” he ordered, turning toward Jimmy. This, of course, was a very normal request.

And yet both boys looked at him as if he’d suggested they swallow a live frog. “Pete, don’t be stupid—” Gary began, attempting to wrench his wrist free, but Pete tightened his grip and rounded on him.

“I’m not being stupid!” Pete snapped. “You two are going to shake hands like normal people, or I swear to God I’m walking back home a-and never seeing you two again!”

Jimmy’s eyebrows climbed, and he looked at Pete for a moment before letting out a short, if rather disbelieving, chuckle. This was a new low even by Pete’s standards, but maybe there was a point to it…?

“Alright, alright,” he said, pushing himself away from the armchair and stepping forward. “Relax, Pete, jeez. Don’t blow a gasket.”

He extended his right hand, and Gary stared at it for several seconds. Every instinct in him urged him to refuse, if only because accepting Jimmy’s hand on Pete’s orders felt like, well, a surrender. It obviously was! He’d never agree to such a thing, he had never surrendered in his life before. But as he glanced at Pete, whose furious expression was beginning to give way to something more vulnerable (maybe?), and his fingers trembled slightly where they still held Gary’s wrist, the fight seemed to drain out of him in one long breath. With a hiss, Gary reached out and clasped Jimmy’s palm.

It was an uncomfortable handshake, to say the least. Neither boy squeezed hard enough to suggest he was trying to establish dominance, but neither was willing to release the other first. They stood facing one another with their arms rigid and their expressions blank, pumping their joined hands once, then twice. Maybe the handshake had not yet run its course? Pete watched their arms continue to hover between them, until he could no longer stand it.

“Okay,” he said cautiously. “You can let go now.”

Neither moved, and Pete’s smile faltered.

“Seriously, guys. It’s getting weird.”

At that, both boys jerked their hands away and immediately wiped their palms against their trousers. Very smart. Pete decided it was probably best not to comment on this, since he was already having considerable difficulty maintaining the illusion that any of this had been a good idea.

“Right,” Pete said, clapping his hands together in a desperate attempt to put the whole thing behind them. “So um, if you don’t mind, I’m gonna see what we’ve got to eat. Let’s uh, make ourselves at home?”

He escaped toward the little kitchenette at the back of the room before either of them could reply back, leaving Jimmy and Gary standing several feet apart in the living room. To Pete’s surprise, the pantry turned out to be rather better stocked than he’d expected. The counter held a family-sized bag of barbecue potato chips, a box of glazed doughnuts, and a packet of beef jerky, and there was even an unopened twelve-pack of root beer that occupied most of the little refrigerator. Interesting… Pete began arranging everything on a pair of paper plates taking care to distribute the snacks evenly and straighten the cans, partly because he wanted to be fair. (And also because he wanted an excuse to keep his back turned for another minute.)

Behind the counter, though, the silence continued. Gary had since drifted toward the makeshift couch and was examining a faded photograph of a sailboat hanging crookedly on the wall. It had a great composition, surely, but wasn’t too insightful besides that. Jimmy leaned against the wooden television stand and picked at a strip of electrical tape wrapped around a thick black cable, and eventually cleared his throat.

“So,” he began, still looking down at the wire in his hands, “you, uh… know anything about cable connections?”

Gary turned his head slowly. “What?”

Jimmy nodded toward the little CRT television, where a thick-ish grey coaxial cable trailed from the back of the set. Its copper core was visibly frayed and bent, as if someone tried to force it into a wall or something. “I’ve been trying to get the signal running on this thing. Lucky came by earlier and tried to strip the wire, but I think the guy’s an idiot. Every time I plug it in, all I get is static and some religious channel from Jersey. I’m not watching that.” He finally looked up. “Pete said you were good with tech.”

Gary stared at him. Oh, how thrilling. Here, at last, was an opportunity to remind Jimmy that he was dealing with a man who could outthink him at any given moment, and that Jimmy was nothing more than an illiterate Neanderthal, one who could not understand how to use basic tools, and whose puny intellectual merits made him think that electricity was generated by tiny little hamsters running on a wheel. Yeah, right.

Still, Gary swallowed the insult, not willing to disappoint little Pete at such short notice, and looked back at Jimmy. There was a truth to what he was saying though… he was no Nerd, but he was not so bad with tech at some thought he would be. “Those stupid greaseballs you hang around with think hot-wiring stuff makes them look like Nikola Tesla,” he muttered, already crossing the room. “How exciting…! Give me the pliers.”

Jimmy handed them over without comment, and Gary dropped to his knees on the rug, reaching behind the television. His long, thin fingers worked quickly, stripping away the damaged section of plastic jacket, straightening the exposed copper core, and using the pliers to tighten the metal collar until it sat flush against the input jack. It was better than it looked like beforehand, surely.

“You’re using a two-way splitter on an unamplified line,” he explained. “So basically the signal’s degrading before it even reaches the tuner. If you were smart, you would know that. You need to ground the shield wire against the chassis properly, otherwise the interference is going to drown out half the UHF band.”

Jimmy leaned over the television to watch, doing his best to follow just enough of the explanation. Half of his brain had no idea what the hell Gary had just said, and he couldn’t have repeated any of it afterward. “Is that why the picture kept jumping?”

“Obviously,” Gary said, though this time there was no real malice in it. He gave the collar one final turn, sat back on his heels, and reached up to flick the television’s power switch.

Eventually a burst of static cracked through the room and some grey-ish fuzz rolled across the screen, and finally the picture snapped into focus. A sun-drenched Miami operating room, how fancy, appeared on the glass, all polished surfaces and bizarrely attractive doctors. This is why audiences kept going down every year, Gary thought. Jimmy’s face lit up as though Gary had just performed an actual miracle, which, to be fair, was not far from the truth.

“Holy shit. It actually worked.”

He looked down at Gary, who was still kneeling beside the television, wiping dust from his palms onto his trousers, and for a moment his expression brightened up. “Well, uh,” he said, as a slow grin spread across his face. “Can’t believe you’re actually useful for something besides causing riots.”

A faint pink crept into Gary’s cheeks before he could stop it. He pushed himself upright a little too quickly, brushed the dust from his knees, and folded his arms across his chest.

“Yeah, right, don’t get used to it, James,” he muttered, looking away from Jimmy and toward the television. “I wasn’t going to spend all my time listening to you whining anyway.”

“Whatever you say, man,” Jimmy replied, laughing as he dropped onto the center of the couch and picked up the remote. He flicked through the channels until he found the one he’d been trying to get working, then settled back against the cushion. “Sit down. It’s almost nine, my show’s coming on.”

“Your show…?”

Gary was halfway toward the couch when he glanced at the television and stopped so abruptly that Pete nearly walked into him. It was all so unusual to him that he barely noticed his eyebrows slowly climbing toward his hairline.

“Are you serious? Nip/Tuck? You actually watch this trash?”

“Hey! It’s not trash, it’s just drama,” Jimmy retorted. At this point, he settled deeper into the cushions with his sneakers propped on the edge of the coffee table. “They’re like, plastic surgeons, but they do shady stuff for the cartel. It’s honestly pretty neat.”

“Oh come on. These things are just soap operas for… uh… you know, middle-aged housewives with their stupid breast implants!” Gary threw his hands up. “The writing is an absolute trainwreck. I don’t know who was the brainlet who thought this was a good show. I mean, if the main character slept with his business partner’s therapist— ugh! It’s all so cliche.”

Jimmy stopped moving. He turned his head toward Gary, and the grin spreading across his face was so insufferably smug that Pete could practically see the gears turning behind his eyes.

“Oh,” Jimmy drawled. “So you do watch it…”

Gary’s mouth snapped shut. A flush crept up his neck and settled in his ears, and for a moment he looked genuinely offended that Jimmy had managed to catch him out so easily. “I-I was flipping channels in the hospital! Not my fault they had few stations… you prick.”

“Uh-huh.” Jimmy patted the empty cushion to his left, looking quite pleased with himself. “Sit down, Gary. You’re gonna miss the opening credits.”

By the time Pete emerged from the kitchenette with the paper plates piled high with chips and jerky, the argument had thankfully died. He set the snacks on the table and squeezed onto the right side of the couch, leaving Gary to lower himself reluctantly onto the left while Jimmy remained planted in the middle. It was a tight fit, particularly with Jimmy taking up enough room for two people, but nobody suggested rearranging themselves, and Pete found that he was rather glad of it.

After all, for the first time in what felt like ages, he didn’t have to think about where everyone ought to sit, what he ought to say, or whether one of them was about to get up and leave. This was normal, this was fine. It was fine, it was all fine.

The next hour and a half passed and by then they had demolished the barbecue chips, worked their way through their drinks, and taken turns picking apart the misfortunes of the show’s… impossibly attractive characters. Hmm. Gary turned out to be a perceptive commentator himself, predicting every melodramatic twist several minutes before it happened… or at least those he recalled, and acting insulted when the writers failed to surprise him. (And all of this while Jimmy tried to do the opposite.)

Pete, who knew next to nothing about plastic surgery and considerably less about the intricacies of… whatever was going on in here, mostly contributed by laughing at them both. He laughed hard enough at one point that his ribs began to ache, and when Jimmy accused Gary of secretly keeping a notebook of the show’s plot holes, Gary threatened to write one specifically about Jimmy’s personality.

Maybe it would be something he’d do, right? Someone attentive enough, he wondered, could do it well. Maybe someone along the likes of Beatrice or one of the nerds, but that’d be another story.

It was during the second commercial break, though, that Jimmy reached into the front pocket of his canvas vest and produced a small plastic baggie of weed, along with a pack of rolling papers and a cheap plastic lighter. He placed everything on the table, and while he expected no reaction from either of them, Pete immediately sat a little straighter. Gary, on the other hand, watched with mounting disbelief as Jimmy poured a clumsy little pile of ground herb onto the paper and began trying to roll it between his thick fingers. What the fuck was he doing? After a decade or so of squeezing the paper hard enough, and half a millennium of fiddling, he came up with a lumpy-ish, wrinkled object that looked more like a dead worm than an actual joint.

“What even is this?” Gary asked at last, leaning forward to inspect the thing, whatever it was. It was not worthy of his respect.

Jimmy glanced up, licking the gum strip with a little shrug. “Uh-duh, it’s a joint.”

“Pfft. Who taught you how to roll that, a toddler?”

“Well, it works fine,” Jimmy grumbled. Surely there was nothing wrong with his handiwork, right? He struck the lighter and brought the flame to the uneven tip, then took a long pull. Did he know what he was doing? The answer arrived in the form of a violent coughing fit, one that bent Jimmy forward over his knees, and his face turning red as he wheezed and spluttered into his fist. Yikes. Smoke soon drifted through the living room, and all Pete could hope for was that his father wouldn’t kill him if he smelled it on him.

Gary burst out laughing. Even he was somewhat amused at Jimmy’s antics, and before Jimmy had recovered enough to protest, he snatched the joint out of his fingers.

“You’re pathetic, Jimmy. If you really wanted to burn the carpet, I know some guys that can do the trick. Now gimme that.”

He produced a pocketknife from his jeans, trimmed the ragged end of the paper, and, interestingly, brought the joint to his lips. Then he took a slow drag, holding the smoke in his lungs for maybe three or four seconds before exhaling a thin ribbon of smoke toward the ceiling fan. And unlike Jimmy, without coughing once. Gary looked down at him.

“That is how civilized people do it,” he announced. “But not you, no. You have the drug tolerance of a choir boy. So… sad…”

“Yeah, right, blow me,” Jimmy wheezed, wiping his watering eyes with his sleeve. He had finally stopped coughing, although his voice still sounded a bit rough, and anything else was undercut somewhat by the fact that, well, his eyes were streaming. “Let’s see Pete do it, then, if you’re such an expert.”

Gary turned toward Pete, and the expression on his face changed immediately. “Oh, absolutely. Come on, Pete. Show the so-called loser king of Bullworth that even you have more lung capacity than he does.” But Pete only pressed himself farther into the cushions.

“No, no, absolutely not. My dad would literally murder me. My mom, uh, she’d smell it on my skin from three miles away, and do worse, actually, and um…”

“Your parents couldn’t care less whatever you’re doing… they let us two hang out, after all,” Gary pointed out, leaning a little closer. To be fair, what he said was true, even though it was also a very obvious attempt to get Pete into trouble. “Just one puff, man. Don’t let Hopkins think he’s the only tough guy in the room.”

“Come on, Pete,” Jimmy added, settling back against the couch with a small grin. “One hit for the team.”

Oh, there was something so unfair about all of this. Even now, after everything, the two of them had somehow found the shared objective of embarrassing him. Pete looked from Gary’s teasing smile to Jimmy’s somewhat broad grin, trying to summon the resolve to refuse them both, but at this point, the room had become so comfortable, and their laughter so infectious, that he found himself reluctant to be the only person who couldn’t join in.

It wasn’t as though he actually wanted to smoke the thing… but… he was tired of being so cautious while everyone else got to have their experiences and make mistakes. He had enough mistakes in his life already, so why not do something different? It’s what Jimmy wanted him to do. Hang out, meet new people, do normal crap.

“Fine!” he groaned, holding out his hand. “Give it here.”

His confidence lasted until the joint reached his fingers. Pete held it between two of them as though it might explode, glanced once at Jimmy, and took a tiny, if rather conservative, puff. The smoke caught the back of his throat almost immediately, and he felt like he’d inhaled a mouthful of sawdust. God. What was he doing? His eyes widened as his body rejected the whole enterprise. No, seriously, what was he thinking? He doubled over, clapping both hands over his mouth as a cough tore through his chest, and his legs knocked against the table hard enough to send one of the empty drink cans clattering across the floor.

Jimmy burst out laughing. It was very unhelpful, and it was such a loud bark that seemed to surprise even him, and he had to brace a hand against the couch to keep from folding forward himself. Gary, meanwhile, made an attempt to maintain some semblance of dignity. He pressed his lips together and turned his face away, as Pete continued to cough, but the effort was doomed from the start. A strangled little snort escaped him, and he quickly covered his mouth with his fist, pretending it was a cough of his own. Ah, not this again. When he glanced back at Pete and saw him hunched over the table, eyes streaming and face rapidly turning red, the corner of his mouth twitched again.

“Are you alright?” Gary managed to ask, as unbelievable as that was. He reached toward Pete, before he abruptly reconsidered what he was doing and settled for patting him awkwardly. “Here, uh. Breathe. Just—breathe normally, you dumbass. Can you…”

It was this concern that finally undid him. Gary ducked his head, but another laugh slipped out before he could smother it, and he had to turn away altogether, with his shoulders shaking as he stared at the opposite wall. He wasn’t laughing at Pete, he told himself. Or at least not entirely, but this was all so ridiculous… he couldn’t seem to stop himself. He tried once more to compose his face, but when Pete finally sat upright and glared at him through watering eyes, Gary’s expression crumpled.

“I’m sorry,” he said, although he was still smiling, and he wasn’t doing a very good job of hiding it. Typical.

“You’re not sorry at all!”

“No,” Gary admitted, turning away again as another chuckle escaped him. “Not at all, really.”

Having recovered enough to sit upright, Jimmy reached over and gave Pete a pat on the back, one that nearly sent him coughing all over again. “There you go, Petey. Y’know, first time’s always the worst.”

Pete was not very impressed. “You said one hit!”

“Yeah, and you took one hit.”

“I thought I was going to die!”

“Well, you didn’t.” Jimmy gave his shoulder another, gentler pat, still grinning. “See? You’re fine. Just take it easy, alright?” Of course, this attempted consolation might have been more convincing if Jimmy hadn’t immediately started laughing again, but at least he seemed to feel a little guilty about it.

“I hate both of you,” Pete muttered, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand. “God, I hate you so much.”

“Not more than everyone else,” Jimmy said, giving him a shove with his shoulder before reclaiming the joint. “Now you know better than to challenge the professionals.”

“Professionals?” Pete repeated incredulously, glancing at Gary, who by now had managed to stop laughing, if only by staring at the television and pretending nothing actually happened. “You were both laughing at me! There’s n-nothing professional about that…”

“Hey, I’m being respectful,” Gary said. His voice was suspiciously composed. It also did not convince Pete, who threw an empty chip bag at his head. Gary caught it against his chest, and Jimmy, apparently taking this as an invitation to join in, started laughing all over again.

It was all so strange that soon enough, he was laughing along with them. At least his humiliation began to feel a little less unbearable now, even considering that neither of them seemed inclined to let him forget it.

Eventually, their conversation petered out, not so much ending as losing the energy required to continue. The television had given up on Nip/Tuck some time ago, thankfully, and had drifted into a late-night infomercial for non-stick frying pans or something like that. Smoke lingered near the ceiling, quite sweet and skunky, escaping in wisps through the windows facing the water, and Pete had sunk so far into the couch that he wasn’t entirely sure he’d be able to get back up without help. His arms and legs felt pleasantly heavy, and his thoughts were moving at such a pace that, well, he was simply letting things pass through his head. It was a warm feeling.

Jimmy had rolled onto his side, with his back to the room and one arm flung over his face, and was, unsurprisingly, snoring. Meanwhile, Gary was tucked into the other corner, cheek pressed against the corduroy cushion, and his eyes nearly shut. Every so often though, he seemed to remember he was supposed to be awake and made a halfhearted effort to sit up, only to sink back into sleep a few seconds later.

Pete watched them for a while, smiling to himself. It was nice, having them both here. This could’ve ended very poorly, and yet here they were. If anything, it was a little frightening how nice it was. Scary, even. He couldn’t quite bring himself to believe that everything was all right now. They’d had a good evening, they’d laughed, they’d even managed to spend several hours together without anybody threatening to kill anybody… did he even deserve this?

Obviously, all of this didn’t mean tomorrow wouldn’t be different. Jimmy might wake up and remember that he hated Gary. Gary might wake up and decide the whole thing had been a mistake. Maybe they would fight each other, maybe worse. If that happened, Pete might go home and spend another week trying to work out what he was supposed to do with himself. If the two people he cared about most couldn’t stand each other, what else would he do? He really needed to stop treating all good moments as evidence that things were getting better. When he thought they were having fun during Halloween, it didn’t take long for Gary to betray Jimmy. That just made him feel stupid.

Still, he was glad they’d done this. God, he’d spent so much of the summer thinking what he could do, as if there had to be a mistake he could identify and fix, some conversation he’d failed to have or some warning he’d been too stupid to give. If time travelling was a thing, would he have tried it? Absolutely. If all he needed to do was just work out where things had gone wrong, perhaps he could stop them from getting any worse. It would’ve all been so easy, so convenient. But the longer he thought about it, the less certain he became that there was anything he could have done. Maybe he just wanted there to be something… the alternative was admitting that the people he loved could hurt each other, yes, and in many ways he couldn’t fix. And loving them didn’t necessarily make him any better at knowing what to do.

“You know,” he said, a little louder than he’d intended, then glanced at the two of them to see if either had heard. “I’m really glad we… we did this.”

Neither answered. That was good? Jimmy snored on, and Gary’s eyelids remained lowered, although Pete couldn’t tell if he was asleep or just too stoned to bother reacting. Pete rubbed his thumb along the seam of his sweater, picking at a loose thread. He’d been meaning to say something like this for ages, though he hadn’t known what the something was supposed to be, and now that he’d started, well, he wasn’t sure he wanted to finish. It was embarrassing enough to have feelings about things; explaining them to other people seemed like an excellent way to make everything worse.

“I thought it was all over for a while,” he admitted. “With us, I mean.”

He paused for a moment. This was less ridiculous in his head, the way he was saying all of this. “I dunno. I thought maybe none of it had meant anything, if you two could just turn on each other like that.” He looked down at his hands. “I guess I was wrong.”

And he wasn’t sure he believed that, either. The trouble was that, okay, he couldn’t make himself stop caring about either of them, even when he sometimes wished he could. It would’ve made his life so much easier, and yet here he was anyway. Jimmy had been one of the most popular kids at school, partially due to the fact he beat the shit out of everyone that disliked him last year, and partially because yes, the guy also had an anti-bullying crusade of his own. There were people that, all school things aside, genuinely liked him. Of course, Pete had watched that affection turn sour, watched people who’d once cheered Jimmy on decide they hated him, and also watched Jimmy give them plenty of reasons to feel justified. It wasn’t easy.

Gary, meanwhile, hadn’t needed to disappoint anybody. It’s not like anyone liked him, even when he was younger, and he’d spent so much time proving them right that it wasn’t like there was, say, anything underneath it all that Pete could still reach. One of them had been loved and might yet become someone everyone despised, again; the other had been despised from the start and had been making an unusual career out of it. And Pete, somehow, had wound up in the middle of both.

Sure, he knew what people thought of Gary. He knew what they’d started to think of Jimmy, too, and he couldn’t honestly say they were entirely wrong about either of them. Neither of them were saints, and he thought of this many times by now. He’d told himself he was being loyal, and that there was a difference between standing by someone and approving of everything they did, and maybe there was? But sometimes he wondered if he’d been using that distinction as an excuse. It was easier to tell himself his friends weren’t really so bad than to admit that they could be bad (sometimes deliberately so), and that he kept choosing them anyway.

And he wasn’t exactly innocent himself. All he had to do was just choose a damn side. Had he sided with Jimmy, he could’ve probably had better luck taking Gary to him, or— yeah, he could’ve been more open about all of Gary’s plans to him. It would’ve ended it much much sooner. Or he could’ve just sided with Gary and actually spent more time with him, even if he wasn’t going to be a part of his wicked plans. Instead, he tried to play both sides, and look how that went…

Maybe it suited him for a bit, but as things progressed, it was clear that he was too afraid of what would happen if he tried to stop them. He’d spent a lot of time feeling sorry for himself, too. Maybe he’d been so busy worrying about being the one who got left behind, that he hadn’t thought enough about what he was standing beside. He couldn’t decide whether that made him a better person than either of them. You’re a coward, man.

The thought made him uncomfortable, though less so than he thought it would. Maybe that was the weed. Maybe he was just tired of going over the same things, he had no clue. If people like Melvin and Tom, like Russell and Zoe, like… others, maybe, didn’t fit into his labels, what the hell was he thinking of? Why did he think that trying to sort everyone into the good and the bad, and himself into whichever category left, would work? It just made him feel ashamed.

Pete didn’t think he could make himself stop caring about them, and honestly, he didn’t want to. There was also no way he could keep doing what he’d always done, smoothing things over and hoping the damage would somehow mend itself. It never did. He couldn’t just be the tagalong for both of them, pretending nothing wrong happened. There had to be something in between that he could do.

“We’re all pretty messed up,” he murmured.

It came out sounding almost like a joke, and he was grateful for that. He looked at Jimmy, whose arm had slipped far enough down his face to expose his open mouth, then at Gary, who had managed to curl himself into a position that looked quite uncomfortable. It was hard to imagine either of them as the villains people made them out to be… that he made them out to be at times… when they were asleep like this. It didn’t change anything they’d done, but… ugh.

He reached for the remote and switched off the television. This left the room dark, except for the moonlight coming through the windows. Pete listened to the ocean noise outside for a while. It made him feel quite calm. He had the urge though to say something, maybe make the evening feel like a turning point instead of a few hours they’d managed not to ruin. Yet nothing came to him. Maybe it was better that way. He’d spent enough time trying to screw things up anyway.

Beside him, Jimmy’s left hand lay open on the cushion. Gary’s right hand rested near the edge of the blanket, and curled loosely inward. Pete looked from one to the other, considering the distance between them. It wasn’t much, really…

He wondered what would happen if he reached out and took their hands. It seemed childish… and a little embarrassing, okay… and he could already imagine Gary waking up and making some awful remark about it. (Which he would.) Jimmy might laugh, too, if he noticed. Pete wasn’t sure he could explain himself if either of them asked. And though he nearly changed his mind halfway through, he did it anyway.

His right hand settled into Jimmy’s palm, and his left closed around Gary’s thinner fingers. He didn’t squeeze particularly hard; it’s not like he wanted to hurt them or anything like that. It’s not as if he were trying to hold them together, exactly, or to make some kind of promise on their behalf. But he wished he could. If for a moment, he could, well, be close to both of them… if he could make a wish, which one would it be?

I wish we could all be normal, Pete thought. I’d do anything in my life to be happy again. He frowned.

Jimmy made a small noise in his sleep and his fingers closed around Pete’s, giving his hand a brief squeeze before relaxing again. Pete immediately felt something loosen in his chest. This was such a small thing, barely even a response, but he found himself holding onto it anyway.

Perhaps surprisingly, Gary didn’t move. Pete glanced over; in the moonlight, Gary’s face looked younger than it usually did. He briefly wondered, not for the first time, what it would take for Gary to let himself be comfortable around someone. He wondered whether Gary would ever be able to do that with him… or rather, whether he had done it already and just didn’t know. God, he’d already made many allowances, given him too many chances, and made it too easy for him to believe there would never be any consequences. He didn’t know. He wasn’t sure he wanted to know tonight.

And then Gary’s eye opened. For a moment, he stared at Pete’s hand wrapped around his own, and then he looked up at Pete. The surprise on his face was unmistakable. What was this guy doing, after all? Pete felt a small urge to let go, perhaps to pretend he’d been reaching for the blanket or had mistaken Gary’s hand for something else. It would have been easy enough to laugh it off, honestly. He could even have made a joke first, before Gary had the chance to do it himself.

But he was tired of always trying to get there first. And so Pete held still, with his fingers resting around Gary’s, and waited. This is fine. Gary’s expression tightened as if he were about to pull away, and Pete braced himself for the usual response. Okay, this is not fine. Was Gary angry? Embarrassed? For a few seconds, neither of them did anything. Maybe confused?

Then Gary’s hand relaxed. It wasn’t much. He didn’t squeeze Pete’s fingers or say anything, and Pete couldn’t tell if the faint change in his expression was even a smile. It couldn’t have been one, right? It might have just been the light. Either way, the tension seemed to leave him, and he closed his eye again without pulling away.

Pete looked at him for a little longer, trying not to turn the moment into something it might not be. He really, really wanted it to mean that Gary trusted him, that Jimmy would forgive him, that the three of them might eventually work out how to be friends again. It would be a blessing, but… again, what would they do tomorrow?

He let his head rest against the couch. Gary’s fingers were still curled around his, and he felt as if they were warming up a bit. Pete couldn’t make any promises on Gary’s behalf, or Jimmy’s, or even his own, ultimately… God knows what would happen when they woke up. If he really wanted to, he could finally blurt out all the questions in his head.

Are we alright? Will we ever be alright? Do you know how tiresome it is to care about you guys?

Instead, Pete closed his eyes. His mouth twitched a bit; there was something to be said for keeping quiet, after all. The sooner kids started talking, the sooner they started talking back. He let his eyes fall shut, with the faintest smirk still on his face.

I hope we never say a word.
