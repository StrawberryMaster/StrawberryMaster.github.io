Chapter Summary

It's morning, and Pete realizes he'll have to stick with Gary for a bit longer. Across town, Jimmy begins planning his day.

Chapter Text

The storm had exhausted itself sometime before dawn, and left behind one of those strange, but really neat, summer mornings that felt almost too clean to belong to the night before. The sky beyond Pete’s bedroom window had faded into a bit of a pale, washed-out blue, while the trees outside sat perfectly still (as they should have…) beneath a layer of lingering humidity. Every leaf was glistening as though the rain had polished them individually. How curious! And sunlight filtered through the white blinds in long golden bands, stretching across the floorboards and climbing halfway up the bedroom wall.

Reluctantly, Pete surfaced from sleep.

For a little while he simply lay where he was, suspended somewhere between dreaming and consciousness, content to listen without really thinking about what he was hearing. Compared to Bullworth, where 11 out of 10 mornings began with somebody yelling, the silence here almost felt unnatural. But it was so much better.

His brain took its time catching up. There was something… different. Pete frowned faintly before opening his eyes.

For one second, he couldn’t remember why there should be another person breathing in his bedroom. Then the events of the previous night came rushing back all at once and— ah, right. Yeah.

Carefully, Pete turned his head toward the spare mattress beside the bed. Apparently, Gary was still asleep.

He hadn’t moved very far during the night, seemingly. One arm rested carelessly across his forehead in a hopeless attempt to block out the sunlight creeping through the blinds, while the other had disappeared somewhere beneath the blanket. His breathing remained slow and even. Looking at it now, the sweatpants fit him well enough, but Pete’s shirt stretched just a little too tightly across Gary’s shoulders, and the hem had ridden up enough to expose a narrow strip of pale skin above his waist.

It was strange seeing him like this.

At school, Gary always seemed to exist in motion. Even when he wasn’t speaking, it was like he was always plotting or looking as though he’d already thought three conversations ahead of everyone else. Sleeping erased all of that. Pete could count on one hand the number of times his technically former roommate slept in their room after that situation with Jimmy and Russell down in the Hole.

Pete found himself staring a second longer than he’d intended before sitting up. And then reality arrived a few moments late. His parents, ah, yeah.

He closed his eyes for a second. Yeah, that was going to require an explanation, and a good one, too.

It wasn’t as though Gary was a stranger, of course. He’d spent enough afternoons in this house over the years that Pete’s mother still knew exactly who “Gary” meant without needing a surname attached. Back then, before Bullworth and before… well, everything else, the two of them had drifted back and forth between each other’s houses often enough that nobody thought much of it. They’d ride their bikes through the Vale, disappear into the woods for hours at a time, or spend entire weekends inventing increasingly elaborate games that made perfect sense to eleven-year-olds, and absolutely nobody else. It was fun, but things had changed since.

His parents had never forbidden their friendship, fortunately. That wasn’t really their style. On the other hand, Bullworth hadn’t exactly improved Gary’s reputation. Pete had lost count of how many stories had escaped the academy over the past year, each one stranger than the last. Some were exaggerated, surely. Others not so much. But whether people called him troubled, dangerous, disturbed or worse, whatever good reputation he had waned pretty quickly after the whole school mayhem.

Honestly, Pete wondered how Gary was still walking around at all. He and Jimmy could’ve died from that whole thing. And even after everything that had happened, it felt as though somebody ought to have hauled him off to Happy Volts by now. The fact that nobody had was either a miracle or some sort of oversight…

…which made the fact that Pete had smuggled him into the house sometime before dawn feel much harder to explain.

Pete eased himself out of bed, taking care not to disturb the mattress beside him. Gary barely moved, save for a faint shift beneath the blanket before settling again, and perhaps completely oblivious to the fact that he was sleeping in someone else’s house. Quietly, Pete crossed the room and cracked the bedroom door open just enough to peer into the hallway. He waited there for a few seconds, listening.

Somewhere downstairs came what he assumed was the scrape of a spatula against a frying pan, followed by the smell of bacon drifting lazily up the staircase. Hmm. His father was probably already gone, given he always left for the library at precisely seven-thirty, so convinced that opening the building half an hour early was worth it if it meant enjoying a little silence. Pete checked the clock on instinct anyway. Eight-fifteen. Whew! Which meant only his mother was home, and she’d probably be leaving for either yoga or grocery shopping before long.

One parent is easier than two, he told himself, though he wasn’t entirely convinced.

Pete made his way downstairs, automatically avoiding the third step from the bottom. It was funny to think about it, but it squeaked every single time without fail, and after sixteen years in the house he still hadn’t figured out why.

The kitchen was warm enough, luckily. Sunlight spilled across the countertops, catching little curls of steam rising from a stack of pancakes, all while his mother stood at the stove with her hair clipped loosely behind her head, humming absentmindedly to whatever song had lodged itself in her brain that morning. (For the last week, apparently, it had been All Saints’ “Heaven” according to his dad. Turns out he wasn’t the only one in the family with a knack for English music…)

Her handbag rested beside a neat pile of unopened mail on the island, along with her car keys and a half-finished mug of coffee, all reliable signs that she’d be heading out before too long. This was good to know.

“Morning, sweetie,” she said without looking around. It turns out she’d always had an uncanny ability to recognize Pete’s footsteps. “There’s coffee if you want it, and I made pancakes before they get cold.”

“Morning, Mom.”

Pete slid onto one of the stools, wrapping both hands around the edge of the countertop more to occupy himself than for any other reason. For a moment he simply watched the pancakes cooking, trying to decide how the hell he would even explain… or try to explain… or what he would ignore and what he would explain… regarding all of this.

And so he cleared his throat.

“…um, Gary’s here.”

The spatula stopped halfway through turning a pancake. His mother looked over her shoulder, blinking once as though she’d misheard him.

“Gary?” she repeated. “The Smith kid?”

Pete nodded, suddenly finding the grain of the wooden countertop so… very… uh… intensely interesting.

“Yeah, he… he showed up last night.” He rubbed awkwardly at the back of his neck. “It was pouring outside, and he’d gotten completely soaked. And um, he said he couldn’t get into his house, so… I let him stay here.”

His mother was quiet for a few moments before easing the pancakes onto a waiting plate.

“Peter…” she said gently, setting the spatula down. “I mean, I’m not upset that you helped him.”

Pete looked up, surprised.

“But you really should’ve woken me.”

“I know.”

She sighed. “Does anyone know where he is? His parents, I mean.”

That was a bit of a complicated question. Gary’s father probably knew Gary wasn’t home. Whether he’d noticed, or cared, were entirely different matters though.

“Not… exactly,” Pete admitted. “I think they were asleep.”

Before she could say anything else, a shuffle of footsteps echoed from the hallway. Both of them turned toward the doorway, and sure enough, there was Gary.

Gary stood there looking thoroughly disoriented, and Pete’s old Pinkerton shirt was hanging a little too tightly from his shoulders as he blinked against the morning light. But he looked sort of presentable, at least. For a moment, it seemed like he genuinely forgot where he was.

“…uh,” Gary said after a beat. “Morning.”

“Mrs. Kowalski,” Gary said, stopping just inside the doorway as though he wasn’t entirely sure whether he was (still) welcome there. “I’m… really sorry about this.”

This apology came easily, but not carelessly. His voice was softer than Pete was used to hearing, and sure enough, he offered her a small, rather awkward smile that seemed reassuring enough.

“I got caught in the storm last night after seeing a classmate,” he continued. “And my phone died, and by the time I realized how late it was…” He gave an apologetic shrug. “Pete was nice enough to let me dry off for the night. I hope I didn’t inconvenience anybody.”

For a moment she simply looked at him before her expression softened.

“Oh, sweetheart, you didn’t inconvenience anyone,” she said. “I just wish you’d used the front door instead of making Peter sneak you in like some sort of fugitive.”

Gary let out a sheepish laugh.

“Yeah… in hindsight, that probably would’ve made more sense.”

“It usually does.”

Happy that this wasn’t another of those situations like in Cheaper by the Dozen, she set another plate on the island before nodding toward the stack of pancakes.

“Ah, sit down. I’ve made far too many for just the two of us.”

“Thank you, ma’am.”

Gary slipped onto the stool beside Pete, offering another polite smile before reaching for a fork.

Pete watched him quietly. It was fascinating, in a slightly unsettling way. If Pete hadn’t known him as well as he did, he might have believed this was simply who Gary was. But he was smart enough to know how to adjust himself by a few degrees, sanding off every sharp edge until he fit comfortably into the room. If only he just did that more often.

They ate for a few moments until Pete’s mother spoke again.

“So,” she asked casually as she poured herself another cup of coffee, “how are your parents doing? I don’t think we’ve seen much of either of them this summer.”

The question couldn’t have been more ordinary, and yet Gary’s fork stopped halfway to his mouth. It lasted barely a second — so brief Pete doubted anyone else would’ve noticed! — but it was there, yes. His smile remained, though it seemed to lose whatever had made it genuine a moment earlier.

“Oh…” Gary looked down at his plate, dragging the edge of his fork through a puddle of maple syrup instead of answering straight away. “They’re doing alright.”

Well, that lie came smoothly enough.

“My dad’s been working a lot lately.” Another small pause. “Late shifts and uh, you know.”

He swallowed.

“Mom’s been… away for a while. Visiting some relatives near, um, Utica or something.”

It wasn’t a particularly elaborate story, which somehow made it more convincing. Generally speaking, people who lied often volunteered details. Gary usually offered almost none.

Pete glanced sideways at him. The hand holding the fork had tightened almost imperceptibly, and his knuckles were paling beneath the morning light. Sure enough, his eyes never left the plate.

“Well,” she said gently, letting him off the hook without making it obvious, “tell them we said hello if you see them.”

“I will.”

That was another lie.

She checked the clock above the refrigerator and sighed. “Oh dear. If I don’t leave now, the bank’s going to be packed.”

Collecting her handbag and keys from the island, she leaned over to squeeze Pete’s shoulder before looking between the two boys.

“There’s plenty left if either of you are still hungry, and Peter…” She smiled knowingly. “Please don’t mess up the kitchen. Behave, both of you.”

Pete smiled. “We will, promise.”

“I’ll believe it when I see it.”

She turned toward the hallway.

“And it was nice seeing you again, Gary.”

He stood almost instinctively. “Ah, you too. Thank you… for breakfast.”

After the front door closed behind her, the kitchen seemed noticeably quieter.

Gary sat back down and stared at the untouched half of his pancake for a long moment, before letting out a slow breath through his nose. Pete quietly pushed the plate of bacon toward the middle of the table without saying anything.

“You can have the rest,” Pete murmured. “I’m not that hungry.”

His friend glanced at him, then at the bacon.

“You’re a terrible liar.”

“Hm, maybe.”

“Works out for me!”

He reached over, stole two strips instead of one, and ate with some enthusiasm. Pete watched Gary in silence; he wondered if breakfast had been a fairly irregular occurrence for him lately. Maybe, he didn’t know. He’d never ask him something like that. Pete’s mind, unfortunately, had other ideas, and all sorts of questions popped up there.

Why did you take me to The Hole last night?

No.

Why didn’t you tell me about this beforehand?

Worse.

Are you even taking your medication anymore?

God, no. Absolutely not. He could practically see the conversation collapsing before it had even begun. Pete sighed quietly. Maybe he’d need to do something else.

“Your mom is way too nice, Petey,” Gary muttered. “Makes me wonder how she ended up with a dweeb like you. Must be, hmm, what is it they call…? Ah, a recessive gene.”

“Yeah, yeah, just eat your damn food.” Pete rolled his eyes. “Actually, uh, can I ask you something?”

Gary shrugged without looking up from his plate.

“You just did.”

Pete resisted the urge to roll his eyes. “Can I ask you two questions?”

“Go on.”

“Why didn’t you ever come by?”

That, at least, made Gary stop eating.

“I know there’s been a lot going on but like,” Pete traced a thumb absentmindedly around the rim of his coffee mug. “We live, what… five minutes apart? Less if you cut through the park. A-and you said you’d visit soon and I… thought it would be sooner, and uh, not at that time of night.”

Gary chewed slowly, buying himself time. “Eh. I don’t know.”

Pete decided to wait a little longer. Sensing he would not get a proper response, he sighed.

“I had things to deal with.”

“Things?”

“Things. What, you’ve never heard that word?”

Hoping there might be more, Pete looked at him for another second. But nope, there wasn’t.

Gary popped the last piece of bacon into his mouth, brushed the crumbs from his fingers, and leaned back in his chair with a far more satisfied sigh, as though that settled the matter entirely.

“Besides,” he said, “you make it sound like I disappeared forever. It was, what… three days? Four days? Five…?”

“Almost a week.”

“See?” Gary spread his hands innocently. “Not forever.”

“Your sense of time is horrifying.”

“That’s what makes me interesting.” His grin returned, small at first, before growing into something much more recognizable. Glancing around the kitchen, Gary’s eyes almost lit up. “But you know,” he said, “this place still isn’t half bad. Beats the dorms, for sure.”

Pete narrowed his eyes. “I’m going to regret asking where this is going, aren’t I?”

Gary smiled. “You bet. I was just thinking we could set up a little headquarters here. Like old times, y’know? You, me, and none of those school suckers trying to pound each other’s heads…” He paused just long enough for the grin to sharpen. “…and, best of all, no Jimmy-boy.”

He didn’t seem too happy about saying Jimmy’s name, though.

“Speaking of His Majesty…” Gary stabbed another piece of pancake with unnecessary force. “Where’s Hopkins been, anyway? Haven’t seen his bulldog face skulking around town in a while.”

Pete shrugged, though the movement lacked much conviction. This would’ve been an easier-to-swallow question if one could ignore the fact Gary and Jimmy fought and fell out of the school’s bell tower a week ago or so. What actually happened up there was still unclear to Pete; Jimmy wouldn’t bring it up much and Gary hadn’t mentioned it at all.

He involuntarily glanced at Gary’s collar, half-expecting to see faint bruises or glass scrapes hidden beneath the fabric of his shirt. “Around, I guess,” Pete mumbled, staring down into his coffee. “Probably, uh… with Zoe.”

“Just Zoe?”

Pete gave him a look.

“What?” Gary shrugged. “Last I checked, the guy had enough girls chasing him to start his own little fuck-and-suck. Hell, I wouldn’t be surprised if he’d started working his way through half the boys by now, too.” He snorted to himself.

Pete couldn’t help rolling his eyes. “That’s not fair.”

“No?”

“No.”

Gary opened his mouth, ready with another remark, but ultimately stopped; Pete had gone quiet again.

Although he tried to keep his voice neutral, Pete couldn’t entirely hide the disappointment that lay underneath. It was a thought that had been nagging at him all summer. When Jimmy had defeated Gary and established himself as the undisputed “king” of Bullworth, Pete had genuinely thought things would change. He had thought that, with the cliques finally kept in check and the school relatively peaceful, they would actually be able to spend the summer together. That meant there’d finally be time to do ordinary things! Go into town, waste an afternoon at the beach, sit around doing absolutely nothing…

Instead, Jimmy somehow became even busier than before. It was insane. For whatever reason, there was always another favor to do, another clique to smooth over, or another crisis somewhere in town that apparently required him to intervene personally. Whatever free time remained seemed to disappear into Blue Skies with Zoe.

And Pete never complained. But he hated to think that, once again, he’d been left behind, relegated back to the sidelines, and his role as “best buddy” apparently was only useful when there was a fight to be won.

Gary leaned back in his chair, watching him for another second before waving a hand through the air. “Eh. Well, the less said about Hopkins, the better.”

Pete looked up. “…Really?”

“Really.” Gary reached for another strip of bacon. “I’m already bored of talking about him.”

Of course, that wasn’t entirely true. Gary could’ve spent another hour listing Jimmy’s many shortcomings if given the opportunity. But looking at Pete now, he wasn’t sure it’d accomplish anything besides making their breakfast miserable.

Besides, Jimmy had occupied enough of both their lives already.

“Come on,” Gary said, nudging Pete lightly with an elbow. “Don’t get all dramatic on me, Petey. Tell you what… you’ve still got that prehistoric computer upstairs?

“Why?”

“Well, there has to be something on it besides all your drawings and whatever depressing music you’ve been listening to.” Gary stood, stretching the stiffness out of his shoulders. “Surely you’ve got a game or two hidden away…”

Pete smiled faintly. “Uh, it barely runs Windows some days.”

“Perfect.” Gary grinned. “Come on then.”

They wandered back upstairs, Gary following a step behind as Pete disappeared into his bedroom and pulled the desk chair out from beneath a bulky beige computer. That one looked as though it had been assembled sometime during the previous century.

“This,” Pete said, reaching beneath the desk for the power button on the tower, “used to be my dad’s. I don’t think I told you about it.”

The machine answered with a long, protesting whirr, followed by a click somewhere inside the case that did not sound particularly encouraging. That thing might’ve been on its last legs, but Pete would never admit that.

“So he bought it years ago, back when Windows 98 was new.” Pete smiled faintly. “I used to think computers just… took ten minutes to turn on. I didn’t realize ours was the weird one until I went to a friend’s house.”

This was a nice way of saying fuck Windows 98. Gary folded his arms as the hard drive chattered away to itself.

“But… we upgraded it a couple years ago,” he explained. “Dad figured XP would buy us another few years.”

“And did it?”

“Sort of.”

The desktop eventually appeared after a lengthy stretch of grinding from somewhere inside it. “It still slows down if you ask it to do more than one thing at once,” Pete admitted. “Dad keeps saying we’ll probably have to replace it before Vista comes out.”

After some time listening, Gary snorted. “Probably? This thing is prehistoric. You could go in a junkyard and find something better.”

Pete ignored him, pulling open the bottom desk drawer. It gave a stiff screech before revealing a pile of jewel cases, with more than a dozen cracked plastic hinges, missing back-inserts, and Sharpie-scrawled titles on yellowed masking tape. He dug past a half-scratched RollerCoaster Tycoon CD and a couple of old PC Gamer demo discs before pulling out the clear plastic case he was looking for.

“Here,” Pete said, tossing it upward.

Gary caught it effortlessly with one hand. He flipped it over. “Re-Volt? Oh, come on…”

“What, you’ve played it?”

“This antique?” Gary’s face stayed completely straight as he slid the disc out by its center ring, and held it up to the morning light like a prize. “I’ve stolen it.”

Pete paused, looking up from his chair. “What?”

“What, you thought I was being for real? Dumbass.” Gary smirked, shoving the drawer closed with his knee and hitting the drive button. The CD tray slid out. “Slide over.”

A few clicks later, the Re-Volt logo bounced onto the screen, accompanied by a spectacularly compressed soundtrack that sounded as though it had been forced through a pair of dollar-store speakers. (Which is, to be fair, exactly what was happening.)

Gary winced. “Christ. Was it always this loud?”

And so Pete hurriedly reached for the volume knob. “I-I mean, this is no Future Space Racer. It sounded better when I was twelve.”

“I doubt that.”

Unfortunately, neither of them owned a second controller, so they resigned themselves to sharing the same keyboard, Pete taking the arrow keys while Gary commandeered WASD. (It worked about as well as expected.) And unsurprisingly, just about every turn became a wrestling match for elbow room, with both of them leaning with their tiny RC cars, as though shifting their own weight might somehow help them around the corners…

“Would you quit shoving me?” Pete snapped. His pinky jammed down on the Up Arrow as his car fishtailed on the track.

“Nope,” Gary hummed. “I’m driving.”

“Into me!”

“Collateral damage, simple as that.”

Gary assumed the game itself was broken, either that or a combination of Pete’s shitty PC making everything worse. For example, the cars bounced off walls at impossible angles, which was pretty dumb, but also pretty funny, and he was laughing too hard to care.

“This is the dumbest game I’ve ever played.”

“You say that like it’s a bad thing…” Pete chuckled.

It wasn’t, surprisingly. At least until halfway through the second race, Gary reached for another power-up, missed the key entirely, and smacked the monitor’s power button instead. The screen, unsurprisingly, went black, and Pete stared at it for a long second before slowly turning toward him.

“Come on man! You turned it off!”

“In my defense,” Gary said, already fumbling for the button again, “that was a design flaw.”

“You hit the only thing you weren’t supposed to touch.”

“Terrible design, I tell you. They practically invited me to press it.”

By the time the picture returned, both of their cars had driven themselves neatly into opposite walls. Gary looked at the screen, then at Pete, and just shrugged.

“Oops… looks like we have to start over!”

Pete rolled his eyes, though he was smiling as he clicked Restart Race. In fact…. somewhere between his computer groaning under the strain and Gary’s remarkable ability to accidentally sabotage absolutely everything he touched, he realized he was laughing harder than he had in months. For the first time since term had ended, it felt less like he was trying to forget everything that had happened at Bullworth and more like he simply had.

And that was refreshing.

Meanwhile, clear across town in Blue Skies, morning arrived a little differently.

The sun was already climbing over the warehouses and rusting cranes by the docks, and its light was filtering through a pair of faded curtains that, admittedly, did a pretty poor job of keeping the room dark. Somewhere outside, he could hear a truck rumbling down the street, followed by the not-so-distant bark of a dog. All of this was far too early for Jimmy’s liking.

He groaned, rolled over, and immediately bumped into someone. Zoe, of course. She was still half-asleep beside him, her red hair was refusing to cooperate after a night’s sleep, and one arm was draped across his chest.  She clearly had no intention of moving anytime soon, and honestly, neither did he.

So Jimmy smiled to himself.

“Morning,” he muttered.

Zoe answered with something that sounded vaguely like “mhm,” buried her face against his shoulder for another few seconds, then cracked one eye open.

“You awake?”

“Unfortunately.”

She let out a soft laugh. “Good. That means you can go make breakfast.”

Jimmy snorted. “Pretty sure this is your house.”

“Yup. Which makes you my guest worker.”

Shaking his head, Jimmy realized he’d lost the argument before it even started. Fascinating. He’d wrestled football players, greaseballs, and half the student body at Bullworth over the past year, and yet somehow, Zoe was still the one person who could boss him around before he’d even had a chance to wake up. It was interesting to think about.

But he wasn’t much of a thinking person, and so Jimmy just leaned over and kissed her.

It wasn’t exactly the kind of kiss people wrote all those fancy, sappy love songs about. (Amen to that!) They were both still half asleep, neither of them particularly committed to moving more than necessary, and it ended with Zoe smiling against his lips before lazily stealing another quick kiss just because she could.

The night before had been good, actually. They’d wandered around Blue Skies for a while, climbed onto the roof of one of the old warehouses by the docks, watched the lights from the harbor flicker across the water and talked about whatever happened to come into their heads. School barely came up once, which Jimmy figured was probably a new personal record.

He rested his forehead against hers for a second, looking at her without saying much. He didn’t say anything, but Zoe caught him staring almost instantly.

“What?” she murmured.

“Nothin’.”

“If you keep staring at me like a lost dog, Hopkins, I’m gonna whoop your ass.”

“Am not staring,” Jimmy grunted, though he didn’t shift his gaze an inch.

“Are too.”

“Nope.”

“Jimmy.”

“Okay, fine, maybe a little.”

She grinned and gave his cheek a light shove with the palm of her hand. “Creep.”

“Yeah, well,” Jimmy grumbled again, ignoring the insult and pulling her closer instead. He wrapped both arms firmly around her waist, tucking her against him and burying his face into the warm crook of her neck. “Can’t help it.”

He genuinely liked her, honestly. More than Lola, with all her constant drama and her manipulation of the greasers. More than Pinky, with her endless demands for expensive gifts. And definitely more than Beatrice, who was sweet but always seemed to look at him like a science project, or Mandy, who was often too wrapped up in her own status to see anything else. Not that he liked rating the girls he’d dated, but…

Zoe was different, or at least she was very much real. She didn’t expect him to be a hero, and she didn’t expect him to be a monster. In fact, all she ever did was treat him like an ordinary teenager instead of Bullworth’s problem-solver. That was good enough for him.

If the cliques decided they hated him again when he returned, okay, fine. He’d just kick their asses again. He’d done it once, and he could do it a hundred times more if he had to. It wasn’t his problem right now.

“Hey,” he said after a while.

“Hm?”

“Beach tomorrow?”

Zoe thought about it for a bit. “Nah. Let’s check out the carnival,” she whispered, as her fingers gently traced the short hair at the back of his neck. “I wanna win one of those giant stuffed bears.”

Jimmy raised an eyebrow. “You know those games are rigged, right?”

“That’s why I’m bringing you.”

He laughed. “Fair enough.”

As warm air drifted through the open window, Jimmy tightened his arm around Zoe almost absentmindedly, earning a sleepy mumble in return, and closed his eyes again. The rest of the world could get started without them for a while. It wasn’t going anywhere.
