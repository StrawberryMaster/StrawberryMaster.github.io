Chapter Text

The rest of the day passed more quietly than Pete had expected.

His father eventually came home from the library, and discovered — much to his surprise! — that the one hit wonder Gary Smith was sitting at the dining room table eating a sandwich and reading one of Pete’s old science magazines. He reacted with little more than a long sigh. There had been a brief conversation behind the kitchen door while Pete was upstairs, quiet enough that he never caught more than the occasional murmur, but sure enough, whatever had been said stayed between his parents. His father wasn’t thrilled about the arrangement, that much was obvious, but he also seemed to recognize there were bigger problems in the world than a friend of his son spending an afternoon under their roof.

Gary, for his part, behaved almost suspiciously well.

He thanked Pete’s mother whenever she handed him something, offered to help wash the dishes only to be politely refused, and somehow managed to avoid making a single sarcastic remark within earshot of either parent. It was quite interesting. In fact, it was such an unusual display of self-restraint that Pete wondered whether someone had swapped Gary with an eerily convincing imposter while he wasn’t looking. Huh. That was actually a credible theory. He should look into it again.

The moment they were back upstairs, however, the real Gary reappeared. “You know,” he said, dropping onto Pete’s desk chair, “your bastard of a dad scares me a little.”

Pete looked up from where he was sorting through a stack of old CDs. “My dad?”

“He barely says anything.” Gary shuddered theatrically. “You never know what he’s thinking. That’s unsettling.”

“But he’s just a librarian…”

“Exactly. Unlike you, I’m keeping my eyes open.”

Pete couldn’t help a small snort.

Gary stole the notepad lying on the desk and began absentmindedly scribbling on the first blank page. It appeared his pen never seemed to stop moving for more than a few seconds. Little arrows became boxes, boxes became names… and names were crossed out almost as quickly as they appeared. What was he doing? Whatever it was, it looked less like planning and more like his thoughts trying to outrun one another.

Watching him, Pete couldn’t help but wonder about Gary’s head. Specifically, his medication.

Of course, everyone knew well what happened the last time he was off his meds. Back at Bullworth, every conversation they had eventually circled back to conspiracies, betrayals, or Jimmy. Gary had spent months convinced everyone was plotting against him, jumping at shadows only he could see until it had all finally come crashing down.

And now… nothing!? They were really just going to hang out and pretend none of that happened?

Pete found himself looking toward the corner of the desk almost instinctively, half expecting to see a familiar amber prescription bottle… like the time when he discovered one of these bottles buried beneath Gary’s sheets just months earlier, back when they still shared a dorm room. He remembered unscrewing the cap out of curiosity and finding it practically full.

Is he taking them again? Or is he just… same old same old, and I’m just waiting for the next storm to hit? Part of him wanted to ask him this outright, and maybe it would’ve been easy enough, but he knew he couldn’t. God, no, he definitely couldn’t.

If Gary was taking them again, Pete wasn’t sure he wanted to embarrass him by bringing it up. If he wasn’t… uh, well, Pete wasn’t entirely certain he wanted to hear that answer either. So maybe it would be wise to just be oblivious about it, he thought. Yeah, that could work.

“Pfft. Quit staring at me, Petey,” Gary murmured suddenly. He hadn’t looked up, and his pen was still cross-hatching a box on the paper. “Don’t be a prick. You’re making me self-conscious.”

Pete blinked, caught off guard. “What? I was just thinking.”

“Dangerous habit.”

Gary finally tapped the pen twice against the pad, leaving two dark ink spots on the paper, before tossing it casually onto the desk. He stood up, throwing his arms overhead and stretching until something in his spine gave a loud, satisfying crack. Great.

“Ah, there we go.” He glanced at the clock on Pete’s nightstand. “Perfect.”

“For what?”

Gary didn’t answer. He simply grabbed his jacket from the back of the chair and tossed Pete his.

“Now come on,” he commanded, clapping his hands together once in the room. “Put your shoes on.”

Pete frowned, looking from the scribbled pad up to Gary’s face. “What, why? Where are we going?”

“Uh duh, out.”

Confused, Pete turned his head to check the glowing red numbers on his nightstand clock: it was precisely 10:51 p.m. (It turned into 10:52 p.m. just a few moments later.) Probably not too late by his standards, but after being awake last night, he’d really appreciate if he got a good night’s sleep today.

“But Gary, it’s like really late,” Pete whispered frantically, gesturing toward the floorboards. “A-and my parents just went to bed!”

“Which means nobody’s going to stop us,” Gary grinned, already sliding his hands into his jacket pockets. “Trust me, Petey. It’s not like you’re not having fun… the night’s just getting started.”

Pete sighed, but he didn’t argue. He knew that trying to get a straight answer out of Gary when he was in this kind of mood was entirely pointless. At least he wasn’t sending him out on the very late hours of night to watch a fight, or something. He pulled on his sneakers and his light jacket, quietly following Gary down the stairs and out into the cool night air.

Five minutes later they were coasting through the streets of the Vale, with Gary pedaling while Pete stood on the rear pegs, and one hand resting on his shoulder whenever the bike bounced over a crack in the pavement. It was the same bike Gary had borrowed last night, but at this point, he wasn’t going to be complaining. The neighborhood was almost eerily still at this hour, aside from the sound of the tires over the asphalt and the occasional chorus of crickets.

Pete recognized the street before Gary even slowed down.

“Huh. Tad’s place?”

Gary answered with a grin that, unfortunately, was just ambiguous enough to be worrying. “Oh, glad to see your sense of direction’s improving, femme-boy…”

He rolled the bike to a stop outside the Spencer estate, leaned it carefully against the stone wall, then reached into his jacket pocket and produced a small silver key, twirling it between his fingers.

Pete stared at it. “Uh…”

“What?” Gary asked, tossing the key an inch into the air and catching it effortlessly. Smart.

“Why do you have a key to Tad’s house?”

“Great question. Great, really great question…”

“That’s not an answer.”

“No, it isn’t,” Gary agreed smoothly.

Pete let out a long sigh through his nose. “Did you steal it?”

To his surprise, Gary looked genuinely offended, and placed a hand over his chest. “What, do you think I just go around doing that all the time? Please. ‘Steal’ is such an ugly word.”

He slipped the key into the gate’s lock anyway. With a soft click, the mechanism opened with surprising ease. Gary paused for a moment, resting a hand on the iron bars. “Oh, it was such a moment…” He chuckled softly to himself. “Jimmy-boy thought he could just sneak around and get everyone to kiss his feet… and he ended up with about five thousand eggs thrown at him. What a shame, a shame indeed…”

The funny thing is, Pete did remember that moment, though mostly because Jimmy had smelled faintly like rotten breakfast for days afterward. From what he could recall, there was some confusion between him and the Preps that led to it, whatever it was, and he didn’t come back from it feeling very happy.

Eventually, Gary pushed the gate open just wide enough for the two of them to slip through.

“Let’s just say,” he said over his shoulder, “I picked up something useful while everybody else was busy chasing the wrong person.”

Pete considered asking another question, or, understandably, pointing out that trespassing on the lawn of an unhinged, ultra-wealthy Prep at eleven at night was a terrible idea. Then he remembered who he was talking to. Ignorance was probably healthier for both of them, Pete thought. And so he followed Gary up the driveway instead.

“Okay,” Pete whispered as they crossed the patio, glancing nervously toward the dark second-floor windows. “Say you’ve got a key to the gate. You don’t have a key to the house.”

“Of course I do,” Gary said, stepping onto the brick patio near the back of the mansion. He approached a set of French doors, reaching into his pocket and pulling out a small, flat piece of metal; it was a simple putty knife. “Tad’s dad might be rich, sure, but he’s an idiot. He never replaced the faulty latch on these stupid patio doors.”

With a surprisingly gentle nudge, Gary slid the metal blade between the doors, lifting the internal latch. With a soft click, the door swung open, revealing the dark interior of the mansion.

“It’s funny, actually,” he murmured. “Guy’ll spend half a million on landscaping but won’t call a handyman. Little prick.”

Pete’s stomach lurched. “Gary…” He lowered his voice even further, as though speaking too loudly might somehow summon the police, or rather, anyone who might see what they were doing. “L-look what we’re doing. This is breaking into somebody’s house, it’s serious shit.” He rubbed a hand over the back of his neck, already feeling the knot forming there. “I-I’m Head Boy now. If anybody found out I was doing this, then I-I’d be…”

He trailed off, not because he’d finished the sentence, but because he wasn’t entirely sure how it ended. Expelled? Arrested? Beaten senseless by Tad (or worse, someone like Bif or future Hall of Famer Derby…) for disgracing his clique…? None of the possibilities sounded particularly appealing, and all of them would end up with him getting a bad beating.

Gary watched him for a moment before stepping closer, and the grin on his face softened into something almost amusing. “Oh, wow, Head Boy,” he repeated quietly. “Petey, you say that like you’ve spent the last year volunteering at a soup kitchen or something.”

Pete frowned.

“You spent half the school year tagging along with Hopkins,” Gary continued, keeping his voice low as he ticked the points off on his fingers. “The same guy who vandalized City Hall, robbed half the kids in school blind, broke into the asylum, started at least three gang wars… maybe even worse, I dunno. And you were there for most of it. So it’s not like you can complain. Don’t be a moron.”

Although Pete opened his mouth, nothing came out. Well… erm…

To be fair, Gary wasn’t entirely wrong, and that was the irritating part. Pete had never thrown a punch himself unless he absolutely had to, but he’d stood beside Jimmy often enough, patched together enough plans, and looked the other way enough times that pretending he occupied some higher moral ground felt… optimistic. And it was true that Jimmy had done plenty of things that would have landed him in juvenile hall if the local police weren’t so completely incompetent.

He was no saint, sure. No one in this city was, and Pete obviously wished things had been far more peaceful. In a happier world, Gary would’ve just taken his goddamn meds, the three of them would go on exciting adventures, and they would live happily ever after. Yet none of that happened, and Pete found himself often involved in dragging Jimmy back up… and finding a way to deal with Gary.

So was he a sinner…?

Gary gave him a light pat on the shoulder as he slipped through the doorway. “Relax. We’re just gonna be borrowing the place.”

“That’s… that’s not how houses work.”

“Depends who you ask.”

Pete sighed through his nose. Every instinct told him to turn around, get back on the bike, and pretend none of this had happened. He knew how to pedal, even though he hadn’t ridden a bike in quite a while, and he could definitely do it. But instead, after one last glance over the backyard, he ducked through the open door behind Gary and quietly pulled it shut.

Tad’s house was eerily still. Despite its size, however, it didn’t seem that different from his house… aside from all the rich paintings, vases and other pieces of furniture, naturally.

“Where is everybody?” Pete asked, instinctively lowering his voice despite there being nobody around to hear it.

Gary wandered down the hallway with his hands in his pockets, looking entirely too comfortable. He seemed to be having fun, and they hadn’t even done anything yet.

“Private island somewhere,” he said with a shrug. “Caribbean, I think? I don’t know, I don’t really care. A source of mine, erm, said that stupid kid Gord was supposedly hired to house-sit and keep an eye on things. But as you can see, that moron is too busy staring at his own reflection to actually patrol the place. It’s basically ours for the night.”

That logic wasn’t nearly as comforting as Gary seemed to think it was, but Pete followed him anyway, all the way into an enormous kitchen whose spotless marble counters and industrial-sized refrigerator looked more like something out of a cooking show than an actual home.

Gary gripped one of the stainless-steel handles and pulled the refrigerator open, and its bright interior light spilled across his face as he peered inside.

“Now,” he said with some satisfaction, “let’s find out what those WASPs think counts as food.”

It took just a few moments for him to disappear into the refrigerator. Had he discovered some sort of buried treasure? It appeared so, as his eyes grew wide with amusement.

“You’ve got to see this,” he muttered, pulling out jar after jar. “Look at this shit… pâté, truffles, smoked salmon…” He held up a tiny glass bottle, squinting at the label. “Sparkling apple cider from um… uh… Condomville in France, whatever. Who would’ve thought normal apples aren’t sophisticated enough?”

Pete stayed planted in the doorway, hands buried so deep in his pockets they may as well have been stitched there. He shouldn’t have been there. God, he’d never even get access to this kitchen in normal circumstances; he and Tad were hardly close, and their parents did not mingle.

“Gary…”

“Relax, I told you.”

“I’m serious.”

“So am I.”

And sure enough, Gary continued rummaging. Every shelf seemed to produce something stranger than the last: tiny jars with labels Pete definitely couldn’t pronounce, cheeses wrapped in wax, and chocolates arranged in immaculate little rows. They definitely looked expensive.

“I swear,” Gary said, “these people spend more on snacks than Bullworth spends on textbooks. Which isn’t so hard to g—”

“Can you stop touching everything?” Pete hissed. “What if they notice?”

Gary looked over his shoulder, then slowly glanced back at the refrigerator.

“Petey.” He gestured broadly at the shelves. “What, do you honestly think these people are conducting inventory on fucking cherries?”

Before Pete could answer, Gary reached for a squat glass jar filled with dark cherries floating in syrup. He twisted the lid open with a pop, fished one out with his fingers, and tossed it into his mouth. He chewed for a few seconds, then stopped in place.

“…well, damn, this is the best cherry I’ve ever had. And I don’t think I had many.”

Pete blinked. “I-I really think we shouldn’t…”

Gary plucked another cherry from the jar before wandering over, holding it between two fingers.

“Come on.”

Pete shook his head. He shouldn’t do this, even if it was just a cherry. It was wrong, he wasn’t like this.

“Don’t be such a party pooper. Live a little, man.”

“But I’m living.”

“Debatable…” He held the cherry just beneath Pete’s nose. Oh, it had a sweet smell. “One cherry, that’s all. If you don’t like it, I’ll let you go stand in the hallway like a good little boy.”

Pete tried to hold out for another few seconds before sighing through his nose. There really wasn’t much point arguing anymore; Gary had already decided how this conversation ended.

Leaning forward just enough, he took the cherry from Gary’s fingers. It burst with a sweetness that was somehow richer than any cherry Pete could remember eating, balanced by a faint tartness that lingered long after he’d swallowed it. He hated to admit it, but Gary hadn’t been exaggerating. This was pretty damn good.

Pete rolled his eyes, though he couldn’t quite suppress a smile. “It’s good.”

“See?” Gary smirked, stepping back and popping another one into his own mouth. “Told you. Much better than the crap Edna slashes onto the trays.”

“It… tastes funny,” Pete admitted, swallowing. “But it’s good, I think.”

“That’s the taste of high society, I tell you,” Gary laughed. He set the jar down on the counter and gestured toward the grand staircase at the end of the hall. “Now come on. Let’s go see Tad’s room.”

They climbed the wide stairs to the second floor, entering the master bedroom at the end of the hall. The room was, predictably, quite lavish: there was a mahogany canopy bed with dark green velvet curtains, expensive oak dressers, and a row of tennis trophies (silver, in fact) lined up neatly along the mantelpiece like tiny little soldiers. The air also smelled of overly sweet cologne. Yikes.

While Gary went straight for the closet to rifle through Tad’s jackets and anything vaguely useful, Pete wandered over to the nightstand. Among a stack of journals and a silver pen set sat a sleek, metallic, cylindrical device about the size of a flashlight.

Curious, Pete picked it up, feeling the weight of the aluminum. It was heavier than he had thought. “Huh,” Pete mused aloud, turning it over in his hands. “Who could’ve thought? Tad’s got one of those high-end muscle massagers. I guess for his shoulders after tennis?”

He found a small button near the base and pressed it. And sure enough, the device immediately sprang to life with a (surprisingly) powerful bzzzzz, vibrating so hard in Pete’s palm that his whole forearm went numb for a second.

Gary turned around from the closet. The moment his eyes landed on the cylinder in Pete’s hand, his entire expression froze. He sat in silence for maybe three or four seconds, before letting out a cough. “You… uh… turn that off.”

“Why?” Pete asked, squinting at the settings on the side. “It’s actually pretty strong. My dad actually has one of these massagers for his lower back, though his is way bigger and plugs into the wall—”

“Pete,” Gary interrupted. His voice dropped about an octave, and was perfectly flat. “That’s not a massager.”

Pete paused. He looked down at the shiny cylinder in his hand. Then he looked at Tad’s nightstand, and finally looked back at Gary, whose mouth was already beginning to twitch at the corners.

Well, that was awkward. This realization hit Pete in stages. First came confusion, then a sickening click in his brain, followed immediately by a rush of heat so intense it felt like his entire face had been dipped in boiling water.

“Oh,” Pete choked out, his voice squeaking. “Oh, God.”

Panicking, Pete tried to press the button again to turn it off, but instead his thumb slipped on the smooth metal. The device buzzed even louder, vibrating out of his grip entirely and dropping onto the hardwood nightstand with a very encouraging THUMP-THUMP-THUMP-BZZZZZZ, rattling violently against the lamp base.

“Turn it off! Turn it off!” Pete hissed in a whisper, making wild swatting motions at the nightstand. Was the device like a live snake to him? Apparently so.

Gary didn’t really help. He slumped against the closet doorframe, clutching his stomach, and shoulders shaking as he let out a silent-ish laugh. “You— oh…. come on… how can you not—”

“Shut up! Stop fucking laughing and help me!” Pete cried, finally managing to scoop the vibrating cylinder up with the edge of his sweater sleeve, frantically mashing the power button until the sound finally died down.

He shoved it back onto the nightstand, hiding it behind the stack of journals, and slammed his hands behind his back, backing away about three full paces. What the hell just happened? His face was like a glowing shade of crimson, and his heart was hammering against his ribs. God, what if someone caught them? Like this?

Leaning against the wall, Gary wiped a tear of laughter from his eye. He caught his breath, looking at Pete’s horrified expression with delight. This was definitely the best thing he’d seen this year.

“Well,” he wheezed, sliding an inch down the wall as he tried to compose himself, “I guess I should’ve expected something like that to happen with you.”

“We are never, ever, ever talking about this again,” Pete choked out, as his voice cracked violently on the second ‘ever.’ He wiped his palms frantically against the sides of his jeans as if trying to scrub off the memory of holding it. “This didn’t happen. T-this never happened. Do you hear me?”

“Eh. Deal.”

“Right, okay. Moving on…” Pete nodded fervently; his ears were burning so hot he was starting to think they were glowing in the dark room. He wiped his hands on his pants one last time, desperately scanning the bedroom for literally anything else in the world to focus on.

To Pete’s immense relief, however, his eyes landed on the massive, flat-screen television setup in the corner of the room. Sitting on the mahogany shelf underneath was a white console with a circular-ish power button. An Xbox 360!

Though hardly new at that point (especially with the recent releases of the just as strong Playstation 3 and the less strong Wii), Pete’s jaw dropped. “He has the three-sixty?”

“Of course he does,” Gary scoffed, walking over and picking up one of the wireless controllers. “Why wouldn’t he? All those rich kids get whatever they want the moment it hits the shelves. Especially Derby. Let’s see what games he has.”

They opened the drawer beneath the TV, revealing a neat row of green plastic cases. Gary thumbed through the collection for a moment before pulling out a copy of Project Gotham Racing 3.

“This any good?”

Pete’s eyes widened. “You’ve never played it?”

“Do I look like I played half of these games?” Gary noted. “That and I don’t usually break into people’s houses to review their game libraries.”

“…fair point.”

Compared to the Piece of Crap sitting in Pete’s bedroom at home, this thing felt impossibly modern. They settled onto the carpet with a controller each, and before long the first race was underway. And Pete barely made it through the opening lap before blurting out, “this looks insane.”

And it really was! If there was anything like this at the dorm, people would go crazy over it. They already fought enough for the TV, so God knows what they would’ve done to any vaguely popular game. But that would never happen, not as long as the school’s funding went to the girls’ dorm.

The game was incredible, he thought. The cars looked like actual, physical metal, and they all gleamed under city lights… with all their super shiny reflections sliding across the bodywork, as they flew through the streets at speeds Pete’s old computer could only have dreamed of rendering.

Gary glanced sideways without taking his eyes off the screen. “What, you planning on admiring the scenery all night? Let’s fucking race.”

Pete laughed and immediately clipped a wall. “Shut up.”

“Thought so.”

They ended up staying far longer than either of them intended. As they continued, one race turned into another, then another after that, until they stopped keeping score altogether. This was definitely more fun than yesterday, although Pete wished for the circumstances to be more, erm, how could he describe this… normal? (Or at least that they could’ve done this somewhere a little less illegal.)

Gary could also agree this was a good playthrough. This was much better than that Future Street Racer garbage they had in the dorms. If he remembered correctly, that machine thing had been donated by one of Derby’s twenty or thirty wealthy “cousins”, who’d decided the students could use “healthy recreational activities” of some kind. Amazingly, the news that most of the cooler machines went over to Harrington House and not the boys’ dorm never made its way into public knowledge.

It was all pretty stupid, but probably the most fun Pete had had since school let out. In the top ten, at least. By the time the digital clock beside Tad’s bed crept past two, Pete’s latest yawn was impossible to hide, and Gary noticed this immediately.

“Alright,” he said, standing up with a groan as he stretched. “Our gig is up.” He switched the console off, waited for the television to go dark, then carefully put both controllers back exactly where he’d found them. “You don’t know me, I don’t know you, none of this happened.”

“You really are unbelievable, you know that?”

They slipped back through the French doors, with Gary taking just as much care locking everything again as he had breaking in. The latch clicked neatly back into place beneath the putty knife, the curtains settled exactly as they’d found them, and by the time they eased the iron gate shut behind them, Tad’s estate looked as though nobody had set foot there all night.

Which was true. Neither of them had even come close to that house. They were all in Pete’s house, doing nothing of relevance and sleeping. Yeah, yeah. Maybe that was relevant, actually.

The ride home was quieter. Neither of them had much left to say, really, and the cool night air felt good after hours inside the air-conditioned mansion. The streets were almost deserted, and Pete could’ve sworn he saw the first faint hint of dawn creeping into the eastern sky.

Five or ten minutes later they rolled into Pete’s driveway. Pete climbed off the rear pegs, stretching the stiffness out of his legs while Gary kept one foot planted on the pavement, balancing the bike almost absentmindedly.

“So…” Pete glanced toward the house before looking back at him. “You coming in?”

Gary smiled, though it looked tired around the edges. “Nah.”

“You sure?”

Pete wasn’t convinced. Gary had never sounded particularly enthusiastic about going home, and he certainly didn’t now. Odd. It felt less like he had somewhere else to be and more like he’d simply decided he couldn’t keep accepting his hospitality forever… no matter how badly he wanted to.

“I’ve got…” He paused, searching for something that sounded vague enough to discourage further questions. “…um, a couple of things to sort out tomorrow. Business, see.”

“Business?” Pete raised an eyebrow. “What kind of business?”

“Nothing exciting. Just… stuff. Don’t worry about it.” He let out the faintest laugh, more to fill the silence than because anything was funny. “Besides, your dad’s already generous enough pretending not to notice I exist.”

Pete smiled despite himself. “Oh, he noticed.”

Gary snorted. “Yeah, yeah. Your old man notices everything. At least he doesn’t make a big deal out of it.” That, oddly enough, sounded almost like a compliment. “But uh, I’d better get going.”

Sure, Pete wanted to ask where, wanted to ask whether he’d be alright, whether he’d actually sleep anywhere tonight, or whether all this mysterious business was really as harmless as Gary insisted it was. But at this point, Pete was bad at asking questions, and he felt like it would ruin whatever thing had managed to exist between them over the past day or two. So he just nodded.

“Alright,” he said instead. “I’ll… see you around?”

Gary’s grin returned. It seemed genuine this time. “Yeah. You will.”

He pushed off almost lazily, coasting a few yards before looking back over one shoulder. Pete was still standing in the driveway watching him, so Gary lifted one hand from the handlebars in an almost awkward little wave. Pete answered with one of his own, smaller and somehow even more awkward, but to his relief, that seemed to satisfy them both.

Gary faced forward again and pedaled away at an unhurried pace, disappearing between the rows of trees until his silhouette was swallowed by the waking morning. (If he could still call it that.)

Pete stood there for a while longer. After a minute or two passed, only then did he slip quietly through the back door, climb the stairs one careful step at a time, and ease himself back beneath his blankets before anyone else in the house stirred.

Sleep didn’t come immediately though. As he lay there, staring at the ceiling, his mind began to process the events of the night.

Gary had been mischievous, sure, this was not news to anybody. And okay, yeah, he had broken into a house, and he had teased like usual, but… he hadn’t been cruel, or at least not paranoid. Just like yesterday, he had seemed almost… normal. More like the Gary he had grown up with, at least before he twisted something else.

Maybe… the thought lingered in Pete’s head. Maybe there’s still something left to save. That fight on the roof with Jimmy might’ve done something to him. He could be a new man after all!

It was a beautiful thought, but as heartwarming as it was, Pete knew he couldn’t entirely trust his own hope. He was too close to the situation, and too blinded by his own desire to have his friend back. Still, as Pete’s eyelids finally began to grow heavy, some hope crept in despite his better judgment.

I wonder what Jimmy would’ve made of all this.

With that lingering somewhere between curiosity and doubt, sleep finally caught up with him.
