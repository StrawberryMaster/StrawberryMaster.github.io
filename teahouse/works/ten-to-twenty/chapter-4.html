Chapter Summary

Pete has a strange dream.

Chapter Notes

Chapter Text

Strange. The path behind the school didn’t usually stretch this far. Pete knew the grounds of Bullworth well enough to walk them blindfolded… most of them, at least, but tonight the space between the back of the library and the backwoods of the town seemed to go on forever. The gravel beneath his shoes didn’t crunch so much as give way under his weight with a heavy wet sound, and the trees in the distance looked strangely flat.

Actually, it was almost like they were painted scenery. The sky wasn’t quite dark, either; it hung overhead in a gray-ish twilight, and one that made it as though the whole school had been left underwater.

His arms were beginning to ache. He was carrying something, he noticed.

Pete had both hands wrapped around the corner of a long black plastic sack. At first he couldn’t even tell what it was, and its material slipped against his fingers every time he tried to adjust his grip. It looked like one of the bags Mr. Burton sometimes used after clearing out the athletic sheds, only much larger, and its shape uneven beneath the plastic, as though whatever was inside refused to fit comfortably.

The thing was heavy, certainly, but that wasn’t what bothered him most. The way the plastic sagged between their hands, shifting slightly whenever they moved, and brushing against Pete’s legs made him instinctively try not to think about what was inside.

Gary was walking backwards several feet ahead of him, keeping a surprisingly easy pace despite the weight between them. He seemed almost cheerful, murmuring a melody Pete could not recognize, while his boots clicked against the rusted rails. He was wearing a crisp white collared shirt, very similar to the one he wore at school, yet there was no sign of his vest. That wouldn’t be unusual given he was expelled, but… although he looked clean against the mud and weeds around them, Pete noticed that the fabric across Gary’s lower back had begun to darken.

At first he thought it was rain, and then he realized it was spreading. A deep red stain had soaked slowly through the fabric, and was creeping outward in uneven patches. Gary didn’t seem to notice, or perhaps he simply didn’t care. Maybe both. When he turned around to look at Pete, he gave him a warm smile.

“You’re doing great, Petey,” Gary said, almost fondly. “Honestly, I couldn’t have done this part without you.”

Pete swallowed and tried to look down at his own hands, but his eyes seemed reluctant to leave Gary’s face. “Gary, my arms hurt. W-where are we even taking this? The library’s like, right up there, Mrs. Carvin’s going to see us.”

“Well, nobody’s looking,” Gary said with a quiet laugh, stepping over a patch of wet weeds growing between them. “And we’re almost finished anyway. We’re just putting things back the way they’re supposed to be, that’s all.”

Of course, the words should have reassured him. Instead, something in Pete’s stomach tightened.

“Putting things back…?”

He repeated the phrase without meaning to, and for some reason it sounded familiar, almost comforting, despite the fact that he couldn’t remember ever saying it before. At least not in such a context.

Pete’s grip shifted on the plastic. His thumb pressed into the corner of the bag, and something warm seeped through the material. He froze. No no no. The night air was cold enough that his fingers should have been numb, but the warmth beneath the plastic was unmistakable. It wasn’t rainwater or mud or whatever else it could have been, no. It was body heat.

And he stopped walking.

“Gary.”

Gary continued for another few steps before realizing the weight behind him had disappeared. He turned, and his expression was still calm. “What’s wrong?”

“I… I don’t want to do this anymore.”

His smile faded, although not completely. If anything, he looked almost disappointed. Pete didn’t like the look of that face.

“Oh, don’t be like that, Petey.”

Pete tried to let go of the bag, but for some reason his fingers wouldn’t move. They were cramped tightly around the plastic, and the harder he pulled, the more firmly they seemed to hold. It was as if his tendons had turned to stone or something.

“I mean it, Gary,” Pete gasped. “We need to stop. W-we have to call somebody… anybody, please—”

Gary paused. He turned and took two slow steps toward Pete, his boots sinking into the sodden mud with a soft squelch. Noticeably, he seemed unnervingly calm.

“We agreed to do this, Petey,” Gary said smoothly.

“No, we didn’t!” Pete’s breath hitched. “I never agreed to—”

“You were tired of him.”

“I wasn’t!”

“You were.”

He said it so simply that Pete almost believed him. It couldn’t be real, right?

“It’s just like you said,” Gary continued, closing the small distance between them until Pete could smell the rain clinging to him. “You were tired of him taking up all your time. Always dragging you into his stupid fights with Russell and his dumbos, always treating you like an errand boy, making you clean up his messes… you wanted—you wanted us to be quiet again.”

Pete shook his head, although even inside the dream he could feel his certainty beginning to slip away. “I never said that,” he whispered. “I never… I’d never say that.”

Gary gave him a small, almost sympathetic smile. “You didn’t have to, Petey,” he murmured softly. “I always knew.”

He crouched beside the bag and placed one hand against the plastic. Pete watched his fingers find the zipper, and something inside him immediately understood what was about to happen.

“No.”

Looking up, Gary frowned. “You know who it is.”

“No, Gary.” Pete’s heart began pounding. “I-I-I don’t—”

“Pfft. You knew before we even left. But if you insist so much on a refresher…”

“Wait, don’t—”

And Gary pulled the zipper. The sound seemed to go on far longer than it should have, and the plastic scraped apart with a slow and really ugly noise that Pete felt somewhere behind his eyes. He tried to look away, but he couldn’t. No, he couldn’t. His gaze remained fixed on the opening as Gary peeled the black plastic back.

First came the stubbly blonde hair, matted dark with rain. Then his pale, round-ish jaw, then the eyes.

Jimmy was lying inside the bag as quietly as though he’d simply fallen asleep. His face was pale, his mouth closed, and his blue eyes stared blankly toward the gray sky above them. There was no anger in them, no pain, not even fear. It was as if he were at peace, despite the circumstances.

But worst of all, it felt as though Jimmy’s pupils were locked entirely onto him. Pete felt his stomach drop. Gary took the opportunity to rest a hand against Jimmy’s shoulder.

“See?” he murmured. “Everything’s fine now.”

Pete felt like he couldn’t breathe. This wasn’t real, none of this was real.

“It’s just us again,” Gary said, looking up across the bag with a soft smile. “Me and you, buddy.”

“No…” Pete choked out.

“But it’s what you wanted.”

“No, it isn’t—I never—”

Gary’s fingers gave Jimmy’s shoulder a bit of a companionable squeeze. “Come on. Look at him, Pete. Look at what we did. We fixed it.”

Pete tried desperately to shut his eyes, but his gaze remained glued to Jimmy’s hollow, milky blue stare. Tears were already spilling down Pete’s cheeks.

“Look at what we made,” Gary repeated, smiling.

“No!”

Pete tore his hands away from the bag—

—and woke up with both palms slammed against the wooden frame of his bed.

For several seconds he genuinely couldn’t understand where he was. It was clearly morning, and his bedroom blinds were casting narrow yellow stripes across his blankets. Everything seemed normal. His room was normal, at least.

Pete sat completely still, breathing hard enough that his chest hurt. He looked down at his hands. To his relief, they were clean. They were trembling badly enough, though, that he had to clench them into fists to make them stop.

He lowered his head, pressing his forehead against his knees while he tried to slow his breathing. Shit. It had only been a dream, although this would definitely count as a nightmare. Of course it had.

Closing his eyes for a moment, Pete tried to analyze the dream. For some reason, the thing that frightened him most wasn’t that Gary had, apparently, fulfilled his life-long dream of killing Jimmy. It was that, in the dream, Gary had been so certain Pete had wanted him to.

God, what a terrible dream. He opened his eyes again. The image of Jimmy’s eyes, unfortunately, refused to leave him. Eventually he gave up on trying to reason with his subconscious and got out of bed. After all, if he stayed there much longer he was probably going to start imagining Gary standing in the corner, or Jimmy crawling out from under the mattress, something like that. There were only so many indignities he could inflict on himself before breakfast…

Before he could think of doing anything though, he decided to have a shower. He made his way down the hall toward the bathroom, still rubbing the sleep from his face, and shut the door behind him before turning on the shower. The pipes groaned somewhere inside the walls before the water finally came through, hot enough to sting. Pete stepped beneath it and stood there for several minutes with his eyes closed, letting the water run over his hair and down the back of his neck while he tried (with very little success) not to think about anything related to the dream.

Eventually he reached for the soap and scrubbed his hands, even though there was nothing on them. Once, then again, rubbing his palms together until the soap had worked itself into a thick white lather. It was ridiculous, he knew, but the imagined smell of, what was it, copper? still seemed to cling somewhere around him, and Pete found himself washing his hands a third time before finally deciding that enough was enough.

By the time he returned to his room, his hair was still damp and his face had gone pink from the heat. The mattress beside his bed immediately caught his attention. It was empty, obviously. The blanket was nicely folded over, though, and so he assumed Gary had actually made an effort before leaving. Maybe he was planning to leave early all along. Or maybe he was just being nice, given it wasn’t his bed…?

Well, at least Gary isn’t here.

This thought brought a small measure of relief, followed almost immediately by guilt. Why was he thinking like this? Gary was still his friend, right? He had spent more time with him during summer than Jimmy, for instance, or… uh… anyone else. But the idea of hanging out with him made him feel guilty, in some way.

Pete sighed and sat down at his desk, opening his sketchbook in the vague hope that occupying his hands might keep his brain from inventing another corpse. It worked, eventually.

He started with a frog. It was a cute frog, he thought. The frog quickly became a frog wearing a comically large crown, the crown led to a castle, and the castle eventually ended up sitting on the back of a giant turtle. Pete had no idea why he was drawing any of it, as cool as it was, but that was rather the point? None of it had to make sense. His life was a lot like this, too. (Except with fewer castles, crowns, and of course, frogs.)

By late morning, Pete had managed to fill several pages with similarly pointless sketches, and the dream had finally begun to lose some of its grip on his brain. His stomach, unfortunately, had not been so cooperative. He was halfway through drawing a particularly ugly bird when he remembered something. If he wasn’t mistaken, his mother had left a list the previous evening, which he assumed was on their refrigerator, of groceries he would need to buy: milk, bread, eggs, coffee, vegetables, and a few other things that, Dad said, were necessary for their survival.

He stared at the list when he found it, reading it twice before realizing there was one fairly obvious problem. They were out of almost everything! Well, how great.

Pete groaned and folded the paper into his pocket. His mother had asked him to pick the groceries up in Bullworth Town if he had the chance, and although riding into town was hardly the adventure of the century, it at least gave him somewhere to go. Staying inside all day with his thoughts seemed far less appealing.

The family car was out of the question, sure, and walking would take forever, so Pete headed for the garage instead. Amidst a collection of boxes, old gardening equipment, and things his parents might need again in fifteen or so years, ta-da: his bicycle. It was tucked behind a stack of cardboard boxes near the back wall, and he genuinely forgot when was the last time he’d even seen it. Pete pulled it free, sending a small cloud of dust into the air.

“Oh, God.”

The bike looked way worse than he remembered. The tires were soft, the frame was coated in yet another layer of dust, and one of the handlebars had developed a bit of a slight, but strange tilt. That wasn’t there last time he used it, he was sure of it! Nevertheless, Pete wiped the seat with his sleeve and gave one of the tires a tentative squeeze.

Yeah, it was not ideal. Then again, neither was spending another afternoon doing nothing. He wheeled the bicycle out into the driveway, gave the tires a few pumps from an old hand pump hanging beside the garage door, and adjusted the seat as best he could. After a brief inspection of the chain, which was somehow holding up, Pete decided it would do.

Besides, Bullworth Town was only a short ride away. The worst thing that could happen is if someone threw shoes at him.

The good news was that his trip to town was mercifully uneventful. Nobody had thrown anything at him, at least. By late afternoon, everything was put away, his mother had thanked him, and Pete had retreated upstairs with nothing particularly pressing left to do.

He lay across his bed for a while, staring at the ceiling and thinking about the last day or so. It had been an oddly busy twenty-four hours, considering how little he’d actually planned to do. Gary had appeared at his window in the middle of a storm, slept on his bedroom floor, stayed with him for the rest of the day, disappeared the next morning with some vague excuse about having business to attend to… and Pete had done nothing out of the ordinary after he was gone.

He sighed and rolled onto his side. Was this really what the rest of the summer was going to be like? Gary appearing whenever he felt like it, dragging Pete into whatever strange scheme he’d cooked up, and Pete following along because, despite everything, he still couldn’t quite bring himself to say no? Again!?

There was something almost embarrassing about it, honestly. He had spent the entire school year worrying about Gary, worrying about Jimmy, worrying about Bullworth and the endless mess surrounding both of them, and apparently his reward was to spend his vacation being summoned through windows like some kind of genie…?

He rolled his eyes at himself and pulled the pillow over his face. “How brilliant, Pete.” The worst part wasn’t even Gary’s unpredictability; it was knowing that no matter how much he complained in his own head, he would still say yes the very next time Gary appeared. This was pathetic.

A few seconds later, right on cue, something tapped against the window. Pete lowered the pillow and stared at it. The tapping came again, fairly light against the glass.

“Oh, come on. You’ve got to be kidding me.”

He got up anyway. When Pete pushed the window open, the evening air came rushing into the room; it was much cooler now that the sun was beginning to disappear. And of course, the devil himself — Gary — was waiting below in the driveway with one foot planted on the ground and the other resting on the pedal of a dark mountain bike (which he did not recognize). He looked unusually cheerful.

“Hey, Petey,” Gary called quietly, evidently making an effort not to wake Pete’s parents. (Not that they’d be sleeping at this time, for the most part.) “Get dressed. We’re going to the carnival.”

Pete rested his arms against the windowsill and looked down at him. The carnival had been there for like, ages now? Either way, it would surely be crowded, noisy, and full of people looking for an excuse to cause trouble. Given that the last carnival he’d attended had involved a, erm, considerable amount of property damage, and Jimmy punching somebody near a rollercoaster, yeah, he wasn’t exactly eager to repeat the experience.

“The carnival?” Pete asked, glancing down at his pajamas before looking back at Gary. “What, you’re gonna take me to China next time?”

Gary shrugged. “Well, if you have a shovel and start digging, I guess you could say it’s plausible…”

“You do realize it’s going to be packed, right?” Pete sighed. “Besides, I don’t have any money.”

He waved that concern away with a flick of his hand, and Pete caught sight of a small leather wallet in his pocket. “But I’ve got money. I took care of a few things today, remember? Besides, you can’t spend the entire summer hiding in your bedroom. Come on, Petey. It’s gonna be fun. You need to get out of there before you turn into a fungus or something.”

Pete glanced back at his desk, where his sketchbook was still open to the frog he’d spent time drawing earlier. (It was a good frog.) For a moment, staying inside sounded much easier. He could read, draw, maybe take another shower, and spend another few hours pretending that his nightmares and misfortunes in life never happened.

Then he looked down at Gary again. “Fine. Give me ten minutes.”

“Make it five!” Gary shouted.

Pete shut the window and headed for his closet before he could change his mind. He found himself taking more care with his clothes than he’d expected, eventually settling on a clean cream-colored sweater and a pair of dark jeans. He combed his hair properly, checked himself in the little mirror on his desk, and felt ridiculous. This was just a trip to ride cheap carnival attractions and eat overpriced food, what was the point in dressing up?

Still, he looked presentable, so maybe that was alright.

When he slipped out the back door, Gary was waiting by the porch steps. Pete paused at the bottom of the stairs, momentarily thrown off. Gary had actually dressed up! He was wearing a dark forest-green button-down shirt with its sleeves neatly rolled to his elbows, paired with dark grey trousers that actually fit him. His hair had been slicked back with a little water, and— okay, he had to admit, he looked cute. It was a bit of an involuntary realization, though, that sent a jolt straight through Pete’s ribs.

“Oh, look at you,” Gary smirked, as his eyes scanned Pete’s sweater. “Very respectable, Petey. Your mom would be thrilled you’re gonna be studying accounting.”

“You don’t look half-bad yourself,” Pete shot back; his face warmed slightly as he stepped up onto the rear pegs of the bike. “Where did you even get that shirt?”

“Back of my closet,” Gary said, pushing off against the asphalt with a hard kick. “Had to shake a few moths out of it, but it does the trick. Hold on tight.”

The ride to the carnival was easy enough, with the last of the daylight disappearing behind the rooftops as they made their way toward the edge of town. By the time they reached the entrance, the sky had gone a deep blue and the first carnival lights were already flashing above the trees, bright enough to be seen from several streets away.

They could hear the place before they reached it. The rides, the carousel music, blah blah blah, plus carnival workers shouting over one another as they tried to convince people that their particular game was the one worth wasting money on. Maybe they had a point.

Gary locked the bicycle near the entrance and immediately started toward the midway, apparently having forgotten that Pete was supposed to be the one being dragged around. “Come on, Petey. We’re wasting valuable time.”

“Valuable time doing what?”

“Winning things. What else do you think I took us here for?”

He pointed toward a game booth where a pyramid of metal bottles sat behind a counter, waiting to be knocked down with a softball. Simple enough. Hanging above the booth was a collection of enormous stuffed animals, including a particularly ugly purple gorilla.

Gary stared at it with growing interest. “Oh, I want that one.”

Pete looked at the gorilla, then at him. “Why?”

“It looks like that… uh… gorilla virus thingy. Y’know, what was the name of it again?”

“SIVgor?”

“I was thinking of Bonzi, but yeah, yeah, sure. Anyway… I want it.”

Pete laughed as Gary slapped a couple of crumpled bills onto the counter and picked up the first ball. His aim was actually pretty good, but the bottom bottle barely moved when he hit it. Unimpressed, he stared at the remaining stack.

“They’re weighted.” Pete folded his arms.

“Oh, I know that.”

“You said you were going to win.”

“I am going to win. As a wise man once said… you miss 100% of the shots you don’t take.”

The next throw went wide. The third knocked two bottles over and left the rest standing. Okay, maybe he wasn’t good at this at all. Gary dropped the ball onto the counter with a sigh. “Man, screw this. You cursed me.”

“Ooh, yes, Gary, my negative energy definitely defeated you…”

“Don’t get too cocky, Petey. You’re ruining my concentration.”

They left the booth without the gorilla, although Gary continued insisting that the game had been rigged for the next several minutes. (There was a grain of truth to it; the carnival operator had probably seen Gary coming from a mile away.) After that, their evening became pretty much aimless. They wandered wherever the crowds carried them, stopping for cotton candy, arguing over which rides looked least likely to kill them (spoiler: none of them), and eventually splitting a paper tray of fries that were so hot Pete nearly burned his tongue.

But importantly, Gary seemed genuinely happy, maybe even happier than he had been at his home. He was also back to getting competitive over stupid carnival games and making fun of Pete whenever he lost, but that was nothing new.

If anything, it was all quite amusing. At one point Gary managed to win a cheap plastic keychain; it was shaped like a skull, and while he insisted it was the finest object he had ever owned, he accidentally dropped it down a storm drain ten minutes later. Pete laughed until his stomach hurt.

They eventually ended up on the Ferris wheel, sitting opposite each other in the small metal carriage as it climbed above the midway. From up there, the carnival looked almost peaceful. The rides were all little circles of colored light, the crowds blurred together, and Bullworth itself stretched beyond them in patches of yellow streetlights and dark rooftops.

Gary leaned against the side of the carriage and looked down. “You know,” he said, quieter now, “this place is actually pretty decent when nobody’s trying to punch you.”

Pete smiled. “That’s a pretty low standard.”

“It keeps life manageable! Don’t you forget that.”

When they got off, the crowd had thickened around the carousel a bit. People were moving in just about every direction… Jesus, was this place always this crowded? Pete was trying to avoid a group of younger kids when one of them ran directly across his path. Great. He stepped sideways, bumped into Gary, and instinctively reached out to steady himself.

His hand caught Gary’s, though neither of them reacted immediately.

For perhaps two or three seconds they simply kept walking, as Pete’s fingers loosely curled around Gary’s hand while the crowd pressed around them. When Gary glanced down and noticed what had happened, he looked back at Pete with an expression that was, admittedly, difficult to read.

And so Pete began to pull away. “S-sorry,” Pete muttered.

“Yeah, uh,” Gary said, equally awkwardly. “Pretty crowded tonight.”

That was all there was to it, really. At least, Pete told himself it was. They had barely taken another few steps when Gary stopped.

Pete noticed this because Gary’s hand, which had been swinging loosely beside him, suddenly went still. He looked over, expecting another joke or any commentary of sorts, but whatever expression he’d been wearing had disappeared.

“Gary?”

Gary didn’t answer. Pete followed his gaze across the midway, and, from what he could see, near one of the ring-toss booths stood Jimmy and Zoe.

This by itself wouldn’t be unusual; they were practically dating after all. Jimmy had a huge stuffed purple bear tucked beneath one arm (presumably the result of some victory Pete was going to hear about later…), while Zoe stood beside him with one hand still resting against Jimmy’s. They had apparently been laughing about something a moment earlier, and they were definitely having fun, but both had gone quiet.

Jimmy was looking directly at them. But more specifically, he was looking at Gary.

Pete felt the strange little knot in his stomach return. He noticed Gary’s face had gone pale, even more so than usual. He wasn’t angry, exactly, as one could expect, and he didn’t look frightened either. But he definitely didn’t look happy.

Zoe followed Jimmy’s gaze and looked between the two boys. Her eyebrows rose a bit.

At that point, Pete suddenly became painfully aware of how close he and Gary were standing. And then he remembered that Jimmy hadn’t seen either of them hanging out recently. In fact, he hadn’t likely seen Gary since their rooftop fight, which meant he was now seeing his friend hang out with his former friend who might or might not have wanted to kill him…

Uh-oh.
