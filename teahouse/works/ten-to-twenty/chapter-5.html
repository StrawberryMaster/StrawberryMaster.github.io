Chapter Summary

Pete rapidly finds himself at six and sevens. There may be, however, a way to wriggle himself out of it.

Chapter Notes

Chapter Text

The sudden absence of Gary’s hand was worse than the shock of being spotted.

For one brief second, Pete didn’t move. The carnival surged back around him all at once… from the roller coaster grinding up its track to the carousel playing its tinny little tune, and yet he felt strangely detached from it, as though everyone else had continued moving while he’d somehow been left behind. He glanced to his left, but there was nothing there.

No shirt, no hair, no irritating little grin. Once again, he was gone. Gary had vanished into the crowd between one blink and the next, and although Pete stared after him for a moment, hoping he might spot him between the passing families and teenagers, nope, there still was nothing. But how? He hadn’t said goodbye, obviously, but he hadn’t even looked back. One moment he’d been beside Pete; the next, Pete was standing there alone.

Shit, Pete thought, as panic flared hot behind his ribs. Shit, shit, shit.

If he was smart, he could do the same, turn on his heel and blend into the nearest flock of screaming middle schoolers. But he barely had time to decide whether following Gary was a good idea before a voice reached him through the noise.

“Pete?”

Pete flinched and turned around.

Jimmy was already making his way through the crowd, with his shoulders pushing easily between people as he approached. The enormous purple bear was still tucked beneath one arm, and its oversized head was bouncing against his denim jacket with every step. Zoe followed a little behind him, with her hands tucked into the pockets of her dark denim skirt, watching Pete with an expression that was somewhere between curiosity and mild suspicion. Did she see them? Apparently so.

Yeah, there was absolutely no convincing way to explain what had just happened without sounding like an idiot.

“Jimmy,” Pete croaked, and his voice cracked violently on the first syllable. He cleared his throat, forcing his hands into his pockets in a desperate bid to hide how badly they were trembling. “H-hey! Fancy seeing you here… uh… great night, yeah!”

Jimmy stopped a few feet away, his brow furrowing as he looked Pete over. His eyes then moved past Pete’s shoulder, scanning the crowd as if he were half-expecting someone to jump him.

“Who was that?” he asked. His tone wasn’t exactly furious, though it was not enough to calm Pete down. “Was that… who I think it was?”

Pete’s brain scrambled desperately for an answer, settling on the first thing that came to mind. “Who? What do you mean? I-I was just… I was watching the carousel. It’s got, uh, very nice paint on the horses. Lots of detail.”

Zoe gave a bit of a breathy laugh behind him. “Nice try, kid. Unless horses can wear clothes and walk, you definitely weren’t alone.”

He felt the heat creep up his neck. “I… well, I…”

“Pete,” Jimmy said, taking a half-step closer as the confusion on his face gave way to irritation. “Don’t play dumb with me. I know that smell from a mile away. That was Gary. What the hell were you doing with him?”

“Nothing,” Pete said far too quickly, his hands tightening inside his pockets. “W-we weren’t doing anything, Jimmy, honest. We just… bumped into each other.”

“Bumped into each other?” Jimmy repeated. He genuinely could not believe this, and his jaw tightened as he adjusted the stuffed bear under his arm. “You were standing here hanging out with a guy who tried to throw me off the roof of the school less than a month ago. Are you out of your mind?”

“He didn’t throw you off! You p-probably just fell,” Pete blurted. Of course, he realized almost immediately that this was probably the worst possible thing he could have said. He knew so little about the actual rooftop fight, why start assuming things?

Jimmy stared at him for a second, visibly thrown by the correction. Zoe raised an eyebrow.

“Oh, yeah, that’s very reassuring,” she muttered. “He only ‘accidentally’ tried to kill Jimmy.”

Pete winced. “Look, it’s complicated, okay? It’s really, really complicated, all of this, and um, I don’t know how to explain it here. Can we just talk about this later, somewhere that’s not in the middle of a carnival?”

“No,” Jimmy said, folding his free arm across his chest as he shifted the bear under the other. “We’re talking about it now. Because from where I’m standing, it looks like you’ve spent the whole summer avoiding me so you can hang around with a total psycho.”

The accusation landed harder than Pete expected. A sharp flare of anger pushed through the exhaustion he’d been carrying around for days. He looked at Jimmy properly then, at his jacket, the ridiculous carnival prize, and the way he stood there beside Zoe. He seemed so comfortable here… as though he’d spent the summer having exactly the sort of fun Pete had once assumed they would have together. Great…

Pete’s face tightened. “Avoiding you?” His voice rose just enough to make a passing family glance over, and he quickly lowered it again, though the irritation remained. “Are you serious, Jimmy? I haven’t been avoiding you. You disappeared.”

Jimmy frowned, looking genuinely caught off guard. “I didn’t disappear, man. I’ve been around.”

“No, you haven’t,” Pete shot back, taking a step closer as his hands came out of his pockets, gesturing helplessly as he tried to get the words out in some order. “You’ve been in Blue Skies, or with the Preps, o-or running errands for the Greasers, or whatever else you’ve got going on. Like, every time I see you, you’re already on your way somewhere, and you tell me, ‘Maybe later, Petey,’ and then later never happens.”

This was, of course, true. Jimmy had been hanging out with just about every person he could hang out with, and when he did have free time he was usually spending it with Zoe. In fact, Zoe herself noticed this too, glancing sideways at Jimmy with a look that suggested she wasn’t entirely unsympathetic to what Pete was saying.

Jimmy didn’t answer immediately. He stood there for a moment, absorbing the whole thing, his expression gradually losing some of its irritation. When he finally spoke, his voice was calmer, almost matter-of-fact.

“Petey, ‘disappeared’ is kind of a biased way of putting it. I mean, to myself, I’ve always been right here. I’m in town, I’m at the beach house, I’m around the skate park sometime. You know where I am. You could’ve come looking for me. If anything, I could just as easily say it’s you who has disappeared.”

Pete stared at him. “So it’s my fault?”

“I didn’t say that,” Jimmy replied, shaking his head. “I’m saying you could’ve called, or shown up, or just told me you wanted to hang out. Instead, you went and started hanging around with the one guy in town who’s spent the last year trying to screw with your head.”

“He’s not—”

“Pete, come on.” Jimmy’s tone hardened again, though he kept it low enough that the conversation didn’t turn into a public scene. “Gary tried to make me crash through the roof with him. You saw what happened, you saw us fall into Crabblesnitch’s office.”

Pete swallowed. Even now, he mostly just remembered the situation as a blur of bodies, shouting and splintering wood. He had never really understood what Gary had been doing up there.

“I thought you both just… lost your footing,” he admitted quietly.

“Well yeah, one could guess that at first,” Jimmy said. “But Gary kept backing toward the edge. He was practically daring me to follow him, and then, when he realized things were going bad, he started acting like he was ready to go over with me. I think he wanted me to fall with him. Maybe he figured if he went down too, I dunno, he could make himself the victim.”

Pete’s stomach tightened. “He would do that?”

“Yeah, he would.” Jimmy gave him a little shrug. “That’s Gary, you saw how he’s like. He gets an idea in his head and then everybody else has to live inside it.”

Pete looked down at the gravel, and his fingers were curling against the fabric of his pockets. “H-he’s not like that right now,” he muttered after a moment. “I mean, we were friends before all of this. Before you even came to Bullworth. It’s… it’s so different when it’s just us.”

Jimmy sighed, rubbing the back of his neck. “That doesn’t mean he’s different. He uses people, Petey. You know that better than anybody.”

The words stung because Pete knew exactly what Jimmy meant. Still, he couldn’t bring himself to agree. This wasn’t true, it just wasn’t. Maybe at some point it was, sure, but not now. He wasn’t being used.

“He’s been nice to me,” Pete said quietly. “That has to… count for something, right?”

“It counts for something,” Jimmy conceded, though his expression remained skeptical. “I just don’t think it counts for enough.”

For a few seconds, neither of them said anything. Sensing the conversation was only gonna get more awkward from there, Zoe finally reached over and gave Jimmy’s arm a small squeeze. He glanced down at her hand, then back at Pete, as some of the tension left his face.

“Look, forget all this for tonight,” Jimmy said, adjusting the enormous purple bear under his arm. “Come by the beach house tomorrow morning. Some of the guys are coming over. We’ll watch movies, eat a bunch of junk, whatever. Russell won’t be there; he’s got some bike thing, but Trent and Tom are stopping by.”

Pete hesitated. “Huh? But Jim, those two used to stuff me into lockers.”

And so did many of the other Bullies, in fact. Although things got better over time after Jimmy defeated Russell, they didn’t particularly become friendlier to him. He could arguably call Russell a friend, perhaps for obvious reasons, but he couldn’t say the same about these two, or any of the others, really.

“They’re cool now,” Jimmy said. He didn’t seem too worried about it. “I told them to knock it off, and they did. They’re not gonna mess with you. And seriously, you need to get outside and touch some grass for a while.”

As if I’m not doing that already…

Pete glanced toward the carnival, then back at Jimmy. Part of him still wanted to disappear before the conversation could get any more uncomfortable, but another part, the much more tired part, was relieved that Jimmy was actually asking him to come along somewhere again. Maybe the two of them could go on a proper adventure now.

“Okay,” he said after a moment; the word came quieter than he intended. “I’ll come by.”

“Good!” Jimmy finally smiled, and this time there was nothing forced about it. “Around uh, ten, then. And don’t show up looking like you’re about to, uh, teach Victorian literature or something. This ain’t the 1600s anymore. I’ll find you a shirt, promise.”

Pete gave him a reluctant smile. “Pfft. I’ll try to dress less offensively.”

“That’s all I ask.”

Zoe snorted softly beside him. “Oh, he’s doing his best, Jimmy.”

“Can’t disagree with that. Just don’t be late,” Jimmy added.

“I won’t.”

Pete gave Zoe an awkward little wave, muttered something that might have been a goodbye, and turned toward the carnival exit before either of them could say anything else. He was relieved, at least a little. Jimmy had been angry, but he had also asked Pete to come over tomorrow, which was more than Pete had expected when he first saw him there. Maybe things weren’t completely ruined after all!

That thought lasted until he reached the bicycle rack. Pete stopped and looked around. It looked like the mountain bike was gone.

He stared at the empty space for a few seconds before letting out a quiet sigh. Of course Gary had taken it. (Whether it was his or not didn’t really matter at this point, given he was riding it and now he was gone.) Apparently disappearing without warning wasn’t enough; he just had to leave Pete with no way home, too. Pete rubbed his forehead and looked back toward the carnival, half-expecting Gary to reappear from behind a booth with that grin of his.

But nothing came.

“Not this again,” he muttered to himself, before turning toward the road.

The walk home wasn’t particularly pleasant. The evening had grown a bit cooler, and a light breeze was coming in from the coast as Pete followed the road away from the carnival, with its bright lights gradually disappearing behind him. His cream sweater wasn’t exactly designed for walking home at night, and his sneakers picked up a fine layer of dust along the shoulder. Walking alone at this time of night was not an option for him, so he’d need to take the bus.

By the time he reached the first bus shelter he could see near the beach, he was tired enough that the cracked plastic bench there looked almost comfortable. And so Pete sat down, resting his elbows on his knees and staring out toward the dark water while the waves rolled against the shore. The ocean was actually pretty beautiful at this time of night. He hadn’t been here in a while.

Eventually, a municipal bus appeared through the mist, with its headlights washing over the shelter before the vehicle groaned to a stop. That would suffice. Pete climbed aboard, dropped his change into the fare box, and took a seat near the back. There were only a few other passengers, all of them looking about as interested in conversation as he was. To his relief, he didn’t recognize anyone, although he could’ve sworn one of the people there looked very similar to a Bullworth student. Brown hair…. uh… maybe brown eyes? But that was half the school he was talking about anyway.

The ride through town was pretty quiet, too. Pete watched the streetlights and shop windows slide past the glass, occasionally catching his own tired reflection staring back at him. He looked horrible, but that was hardly news to him. He tried not to think about Gary vanishing into the crowd, or Jimmy’s expression when he’d realized who Pete had been with, or the uncomfortable fact that tomorrow he’d promised to spend the morning at the beach house. Which wasn’t a bad thing, but still.

For a while, he managed to think about nothing at all.

The bus eventually dropped him near the old park in the Vale. That place was pretty calm, too. From there, Pete had another ten-minute walk through the streets before reaching his parents’ house. He slipped through the back gate and eased open the rear door, taking his shoes off before heading upstairs so he wouldn’t wake anyone.

Unsurprisingly, the house was completely dark. His parents were presumably asleep, blissfully unaware of their son’s misadventures tonight. They had better things to worry about.

Pete paused halfway up the stairs. “God,” he whispered to himself, shaking his head.

He made it to his room without waking anyone, changed out of his sweater and jeans, and crawled into bed. For a while he simply lay there staring at the ceiling. Hmm. Thinking about the events of tonight again, Pete thought there was something in the way Gary had disappeared that bothered him. It reminded Pete too much of last year, when he had suddenly gone from being everywhere to being nowhere, after the whole Hole (no pun intended) fight with Jimmy and Russell. If he couldn’t easily find him at school, oh, he’d never find him in town.

Even then, things were clearly different now. They had to be. Gary had been different all evening, almost strangely so. He’d laughed with Pete, bought him food, and seemed genuinely happy to be there. When was the last time they had so much fun together? Not in a long time. And none of that looked like the Gary Pete remembered from the worst parts of the school year.

Maybe Gary would come back tomorrow. Maybe he’d show up at the window like nothing had happened, complain about Pete taking too long to get dressed, and drag him off somewhere else. That was probably what would happen. Or maybe he wouldn’t; he was bad at guessing.

What he knew for sure is that tomorrow, he would go to Jimmy’s. That was probably a good thing, right? He needed to see him. But he also needed to stop spending every waking hour wondering where Gary was going to appear next, or what he was planning, or whether Pete was somehow becoming responsible for whatever mess came next. Ugh.

Pete sighed, rolled onto his side, and pulled the blankets up around himself. He was exhausted, and whatever problems tomorrow was going to bring could wait until morning. Until then, he could pretend that everything was normal. He just wasn’t sure anything felt normal anymore.

The next morning arrived with far too much sunny clarity that made Pete’s eyes burn.

He woke up a little after nine, and his body was feeling like it had been run over by a lawnmower, but he forced himself out of bed anyway. After splashing some cold water on his face to wash away the last foggy remnants of sleep, he padded downstairs, bracing himself for his usual morning routine.

To his surprise, his father was still home. When was the last time that happened? Instead of being off at the library, Mr. Kowalski was sitting at the kitchen table in a short-sleeved plaid shirt, reading through the morning paper with a cup of black coffee steaming beside him. He looked up over the rim of his reading glasses as Pete shuffled into the kitchen, taking in his son’s rumpled hair and generally miserable expression.

“Morning, Peter,” he said quietly. “Sleep well?”

“More or less,” Pete mumbled, opening the pantry and finding a piece of bread. He put it in the toaster, then leaned against the counter while it worked.

His father folded the newspaper slightly. “I thought you’d be sleeping until noon.”

“I was going to,” Pete admitted, rubbing one eye. “Couldn’t, though.”

“Fair enough.” His father took another sip of coffee before returning to the paper. “You know, I would’ve done it if I were you. I don’t have to be at the library until one, actually. Inventory day.”

Pete nodded, and for a moment the kitchen fell quiet (except, of course, for the faint pop of the toaster). It was a good-looking toast, he thought. He ate his toast standing by the counter, then pulled on his sneakers and reached for the back door.

His father looked up again. “Going somewhere?”

“Yeah. Just down to the beach for a while,” Pete said, keeping his answer deliberately vague. “Get some fresh air and uh, stuff.” Of course, Pete wasn’t particularly interested in explaining the beach house, Jimmy, or the fact that he had agreed to spend the morning with a collection of boys who had spent most of the school year making his life miserable. That would be too much for him, and maybe he’d even be exaggerating.

“Take some sunscreen,” his father said, nodding toward the hallway closet. “It’s going to be hot, and well, you burn almost as quickly as your mother does.”

Pete smiled faintly. “I will.”

“Good!”

He grabbed an old bottle from the closet, tucked it into his pocket, and gave his father a quick wave. “See you later, Dad.”

“Have fun.”

I hope so. Pete stepped outside and headed for the garage. To his relief, his bicycle was still where he’d left it, tucked behind a stack of old boxes and a collection of lawn equipment that hadn’t been used in years. It looked even worse in the morning light, and he should’ve left it in a better place. He was pretty sure that one tire was noticeably softer than the other, but there wasn’t much he could do about that. At least not yet…

Pete stared at it for a moment. He could give it a good name sometime. “Well, you’re going to have to do.” He wheeled it out into the driveway, checked the tires, and gave the chain a cautious spin. Though it squeaked a bit, nothing seemed immediately likely to fall off, which was a good thing!

The ride toward the beach was hot and, admittedly, a little miserable. The sun was already high, and the bicycle decided it was a great time to make a weird noise every few seconds… all of this while Pete’s shirt slowly stuck to his back. Still, by the time the beach house came into view, he was mostly too tired to care about the ride.

Jimmy’s clubhouse sat on the rocky bluffs just below the old lighthouse. Amazingly, this was a once-expensive beach house owned by one of the Preppies, but at some point they lost interest in it. Unusual, perhaps, but it did explain its poor conditions: the paint on the porch was peeling, one of the shutters hung slightly crooked, and there were a couple of empty soda cans sitting beside the steps.

Yeah, no surprises here. Pete pushed his bicycle up the wooden ramp and leaned it against the porch railing. He wiped the sweat from his forehead, took a breath, and walked up to the door. For a second, he hesitated, but he knocked anyway.

The door swung open almost immediately. Jimmy stood there in a sleeveless white undershirt, loose dark sweatpants, and a pair of battered sneakers that looked as though they had survived several fights and at least one unfortunate encounter with a puddle. His hair was still slightly damp, and he was holding a half-empty bag of corn chips in one hand.

“Pete! You actually made it,” he said, stepping aside. “Come in.”

Pete stepped through the doorway and paused while his eyes adjusted to the dim interior. The beach house was a tad cooler than outside, and it smelled faintly of salt, for some reason. That and fried food. It looked the same as last time he was here, though with a notable difference: there was a small television on a battered wooden stand against the wall, surprisingly clean and in good condition despite everything else in the room looking like it had been dragged there from a thrift store. Hmm.

In front of it was a makeshift couch assembled from two mismatched cushions and an old mattress, with a couple of blankets thrown over the top. There were soda cans, paper plates, and a stack of burger wrappers scattered across what was supposed to be a coffee table.

To no surprise, Trent and Tom were already there. Trent had taken over a floral armchair in the corner, wearing a faded black T-shirt, loose jeans, and scuffed sneakers. His blond hair stuck out in several directions, and he was turning an unlit cigarette between his fingers while staring at the television. (When was the last time Pete even saw him wear something that wasn’t white?) His friend Tom was sitting on the edge of the makeshift couch in a dark red T-shirt and baggy shorts. His usual black eye was still very much present, and the yellowish-purple bruise standing out against his face. Pete wasn’t sure how that happened, although from what he could recall, this bruise was smaller than it had been weeks, or rather months ago.

Both looked up when Pete entered, and that didn’t help much. For a second, Pete’s body reacted before his brain did. His shoulders tightened, and he instinctively waited for the inevitable shove, insult, or sudden attempt to make his morning worse. God knows how much the Bullies, and virtually any of the cliques at school, made his life hell before Jimmy came by.

“Oh,” Trent drawled, giving Pete a two-finger wave. “Hey, uh… Kowalski. You look like you got dressed for school.”

Pete glanced down at himself. Compared to Jimmy and the others, he felt a bit out of place, even though he wasn’t really wearing anything out of the ordinary; a pale blue crewneck tee and neat dark shorts. “It’s just clothes,” he said, though he couldn’t help looking slightly self-conscious.

Tom lifted his can of cola in greeting. “Looks fine.”

“See?” Jimmy said, closing the door behind Pete. “Told you they weren’t gonna kill you, man.”

“Well… I just wanted to make sure, you know.” Pete replied.

Jimmy laughed. He walked over to the mini-fridge by the unused bar, grabbed a cold can of soda, and tossed it toward Pete. Pete caught it against his chest, nearly dropped it, then opened it and took a cautious sip.

“There’s burgers on the table,” Jimmy said, pointing toward a paper bag. “If I were you, I’d grab one before Trent eats all of them.”

“Ah, screw you. I don’t eat that much,” Trent complained from the armchair.

“You ate four.”

“Four isn’t that many!”

Tom looked over at him. “There were like… six burgers, dude.”

“Exactly.”

Pete smiled despite himself, and sat down on the end of the makeshift couch. It was a little cramped, but after a few minutes everyone had settled into a fairly comfortable arrangement, with Jimmy beside him, Tom on the other side, and Trent still occupying the armchair. That would do.

Right after that, though, Trent leaned forward, rummaging through the pocket of his pants. Eventually he pulled out a tiny foil packet, tapping a small, pink-speckled tablet onto his palm. He looked at it with some sort of fascination before glancing over at Pete with a loose, half-baked grin.

“Psst… Pete,” Trent mumbled, extending his hand across the table. “You ever tried 2-C? Makes the… the colors on the wall start breathing and shit. It’s real good.”

Pete’s eyes widened, his throat locking up as he stared at the little pink pill like it was live dynamite or something. “I—uh, I really don’t—”

Before Pete could finish stumbling over his refusal, however, Jimmy’s arm shot out across the coffee table, swatting Trent’s wrist aside and snatching the pill clean out of his palm.

“Yeah, I’ll have it.” Jimmy tucked it between his fingers.

Trent blinked slowly at his empty hand, looking more confused than offended. “Aw, man… that was my last one.”

“Too bad then,” Jimmy grunted, grabbing a black plastic remote from the table. “There’s a time and place for everything, I’m not doing that here.”

Pete had been staring at the television and pretending this exchange hadn’t happened, but he couldn’t keep doing that forever. “So what are we watching?”

Trent pointed toward a VHS cassette lying on the coffee table. “O.”

“The letter?”

Tom chuckled. “Nope.”

Pete picked up the case and looked at the cover. “Ohh, O. The Shakespeare one?”

“Yeah,” Trent said. “My brother had it.”

Although Pete had no clue that Trent had a brother, to be fair, it wasn’t like they were close anyway. Eventually, Jimmy grabbed the cassette before Pete could put it down. “You know, I wanted the British one.”

Pete looked at him. “The British one?”

“Yeah. You know, the actual Othello.”

“That’s… not how that works.” Of course it wasn’t how it worked; Othello had been dead for like, at least 200, 300 years? And even then— oh, no, wait. He meant the film Othello. That made more sense. (Unbeknownst to Pete, Jimmy meant the lesser-known 2001 version of Othello.)

Jimmy ignored him. “Couldn’t find it anywhere, though, so this’ll do.”

Tom shrugged. “It’s got basketball. I’m pretty sure it does.”

“You bet it does,” Jimmy said, apparently satisfied with this reasoning.

He pushed the tape into the VCR, and the television flickered before settling into the opening screen. They shifted around on the couch, pulling one of the blankets over their legs as the movie started.

For the next couple of hours, the four of them watched a movie that, admittedly, had just about anything you’d expect from most of these in the romantic genre. Jealousy, manipulation, friendships slowly collapsing under the weight of paranoia, which Pete found mildly unfortunate given the circumstances, among others. Half the films nowadays were like that, sure, but… it was an odd sight. Jimmy watched with surprising concentration, occasionally muttering about whichever character was getting on his nerves, while Tom mostly reacted whenever somebody got punched. Trent spent much of the movie slouched in his chair, occasionally leaning toward the open window to smoke.

Pete mostly kept quiet, eating one of the burgers and slowly finishing his soda. It was strange sitting there with them, obviously. The Bullies were still the Bullies, and even though none of them were bothering him, he wondered how long it would last, and who of them would start throwing insults at him first. Aside from some slight teasing here and there, though, nothing like this happened. For once, he was simply treated as a normal person. If only this also happened in school…

When the credits finally began to roll, Jimmy leaned back and stretched his arms over his head.

“Pretty good,” he said.

Pete glanced at him. “You spent half the movie complaining about it.”

“Yeah, but I liked it.”

Eventually, the television went blue for a moment before the VCR clicked loudly, and began rewinding the tape. Trent stretched in the armchair, raising both arms over his head until his back produced a series of surprisingly loud pops. He winced, rubbed one shoulder, and glanced down at the battered little digital watch on his wrist.

“Well, that was depressing. I gotta split, anyway. Promised a, uh, friend I’d help him move some… you know, scrap metal down by the docks, and apparently I’m the only one stupid enough to volunteer for that.”

Although Pete didn’t seem to believe it, there was no way he’d be complaining; he took the opportunity to stand as well, realizing only then how stiff his legs had become after spending nearly two hours wedged into the corner of the makeshift couch. “O-oh yeah, um… I should probably get going, too. My mom left me a grocery list yesterday, and I still forgot half of it, and if I come home… she’s not gonna like it.”

Jimmy looked over at him, then pushed himself up from the couch. “You’re really going to let your mom win that argument?”

No such grocery list existed, in fact, but there was a grain of truth to it, as she would surely be giving him another list today. What was all that for, anyway? Not specifying that, Pete gave Jimmy a tired little smile. “I don’t think I have much of a choice.”

The four of them gradually made their way toward the front door, stepping around half a dozen empty soda cans, the discarded popcorn bag, and old magazines that had accumulated beneath the table. Jimmy held the door open while Trent headed down the wooden ramp, and Pete followed with Tom lumbering along behind him.

“Thanks for coming by, Pete,” Jimmy said as Pete stepped onto the porch. He gave him a heavy pat on the shoulder. This gesture would have felt completely ordinary coming from almost anyone else, perhaps. Coming from Jimmy, after the past few weeks, though, it carried a little more weight. It was pretty nice.

Pete glanced up at him. “Yeah. It’s not what I do often but uh, thanks for inviting me.”

“You should come around more often,” Jimmy continued, nodding toward the two bullies, who were already making their way toward the driveway. “Even if I’m not here. Seriously! You need people around you, dude. I’m not gonna be around forever to make sure nobody screws with you.”

Pete’s smile faltered almost imperceptibly.

He knew Jimmy hadn’t meant anything by it; if anything, the words were supposed to be reassuring. Jimmy was telling him that he had friends now, that he didn’t have to spend every afternoon alone in his bedroom waiting for someone to remember he existed. That was true. Yet there was something about I’m not gonna be around forever that lodged itself unpleasantly in Pete’s chest.

Even though he’d only known Jimmy for about a year, it felt as if he was one of countless fixtures in his life. Even when he didn’t see him for days at a time, Pete had never seriously considered that he might simply move on one day, leaving Bullworth behind along with everything and everyone he’d met there. But perhaps that was exactly what was happening. Jimmy had a girlfriend, a wider circle of friends, a beach house, and half the town apparently calling him whenever something needed fixing. Pete had spent the summer waiting for his old friend to come back, but Jimmy knew that there was an entire world outside the two of them.

It very much felt like the world had turned and left him there.

“Thanks,” Pete said after a moment, his voice quieter than before. “I’ll keep that in mind.”

Jimmy seemed satisfied with that answer, though. “Good. Now go before your mom sends out a search party or something.”

Pete laughed softly and headed down the ramp. “Heh. See you around, Jimmy.”

“Later.”

He reached a small gravel driveway right next to the lighthouse and walked over to where his bicycle was leaning against the railing. He wrapped one hand around the handlebar and was just about to pull it upright when a voice came from behind him.

“Hey, Pete.”

Pete turned. Tom was standing beside a battered maroon station wagon parked at the edge of the driveway. The car, as it appeared, looked as though it had spent the last decade being used for purposes no automobile had ever been designed to endure; the rear bumper was held on by two strips of silver duct tape, one hubcap was missing, and a faded pine-tree air freshener hung from the rear-view mirror. The interior, though, already smelled strongly of motor oil and old fast food. Was it even his?

He jingled a set of brass keys between his thick fingers. His already bruised-looking left eye ended up looking more swollen and dark in the afternoon sunlight, which wasn’t a good sight.

“You need a ride?” he asked, jerking his chin toward the car. “Gotta head into the town center anyway. My uncle wants me to pick up some auto parts and a pack of smokes.”

Pete stared at him for a second. “You drive?”

Tom looked faintly offended by the question, although it was difficult to tell whether that was because Pete had doubted him, or because Tom himself had only recently become comfortable with the idea. “Yeah, uh. Got my license last month. Car belongs to my uncle, he don’t really use it much.”

Hmm. Pete glanced down at his bicycle. Given its current (and not exactly stellar) conditions, something told him the ride back home was going to be long, and a bit sweaty too. Besides, about the whole ‘grocery list’ thing… the markets and grocers in the Vale’s commercial center charged an arm and a leg for basic staples. In fact, he was pretty sure a single carton of organic milk there cost more than an entire meal in town!

On the other hand, getting dropped off in Bullworth Town meant he could hit the local grocery store, grab what he needed for half the price, and figure out the rest from there. He wasn’t going to be carrying much anyway, and worst-case scenario, it was a much easier pedal back home from the town center than all the way out here from the bluffs.

“Uh, sure,” Pete said. “If you’re really going that way, yeah, town works great for me.”

“Hop in then,” Tom grunted, already lumbering over to the bicycle.

Before Pete could object, Tom grabbed the bicycle by the frame with one hand and lifted it into the back of the station wagon. The rear hatch groaned as he forced it shut around the crooked handlebars, leaving the bicycle resting across a pile of old blankets and what appeared to be two spare tires.

Pete stared at the arrangement. “…is that going to be okay?”

“It’ll be fine.”

“Are you sure?”

Tom shrugged. “It’s a bike. What did you think it’s made of, pudding?”

Well, there wasn’t much to argue with there. Pete climbed into the passenger seat, trying his best to ignore the cracked vinyl that stuck slightly to his back. The dashboard had several mysterious scratches across it, the radio’s display was missing two segments, and there was a faded sticker on the glove compartment that warned passengers not to slam the door. As Pete could see, the car’s previous owners didn’t respect that, as the door appeared to have been slammed about ten thousand times already. Awkward.

Tom climbed into the driver’s seat, making the suspension creak beneath his weight. He shoved the key into the ignition and twisted it. The engine coughed a bit. He tried again, and unsurprisingly it coughed again. Annoyed, Tom stared at the dashboard before turning the key a third time. This time though, the engine finally roared awake.

“There,” Tom muttered. “See? Look at this bad boy, it’s perfect.”

Pete looked at him. “Yeah… perfect,” he agreed.

Eventually Tom pulled away from the beach house and onto the road. For several minutes neither of them said anything. What was Pete supposed to say anyway…? This was all so normal, and yet all so strange. Pete rested his forehead against the cool window and watched the coastline slide past.

He still had no idea where Gary had gone, whether he was going to show up again tomorrow (or today), or whether yesterday meant that Gary had decided to disappear from Pete’s life all over again, just like last year. Part of him was worried that was exactly what would happen. Things had been different this time, certainly, but Pete had learned not to confuse different with predictable. But would this just keep going until one of them broke the cycle…?

Still, for the moment, he was tired enough that none of it seemed worth solving. He had, after all, stuff to buy.
