Chapter Summary

Pete goes to town to finish some errands... or close enough to it.

Chapter Text

As the station wagon rattled along the road, Pete began to think that this entire ride, well, it felt less like a drive and more like, say, an extended test of the car’s (already poor) structural integrity. That thing wouldn’t last long. Every few seconds something underneath the car gave a hollow clunk, followed by a brief vibration through the floorboards, and Pete found himself instinctively tightening his grip on the edge of his seat whenever they hit an ugly patch of asphalt.

He kept one elbow tucked close against his ribs, trying not to let his arm rest against the passenger door. It could fall off at this rate. The midday sun was almost painfully bright, flashing across the hood and through the windshield in rapid bursts whenever they passed between trees. Beside him, Tom drove with the same calm expression he’d worn since leaving the beach house, as his shoulders filled most of the driver’s seat and both hands rested low on the steering wheel.

For a while, Pete simply watched the scenery pass. He didn’t know what to say. The rocky bluffs and scrubby pines around the beach gradually gave way to flatter stretches of dry grass and low commercial buildings, that and a line of telephone poles, many of which leaned slightly toward the road; Bullworth infrastructure hadn’t been great in ages.

Eventually, the silence began to feel awkward enough that Pete decided he might as well do something about it. “So, um…” He cleared his throat and raised his voice a little over the engine. “You drive a lot during the summer?”

Tom blinked and continued staring at the road. For several seconds he said nothing, and Pete wondered whether he’d actually heard the question.

“Sometimes,” Tom finally grunted. He shifted his hands on the wheel, as one thumb tapped against the cracked plastic. “My uncle lets me take it when he’s sleeping. That or when he needs stuff from the hardware store.”

“Oh. Right.” Pete nodded, perhaps a little too enthusiastically. “That’s… um, convenient, I guess.”

“Yeah.”

Pete waited to see if he would bring up something else, but he did not. Tom drove, and another half-mile disappeared beneath the tires. He glanced at him a few times, then back through the window. Apparently, conversation with Tom required the patience of a monk.

“Is your uncle working on something big?” Pete asked after another moment. “I mean, with all the car parts and everything.”

Tom’s brow furrowed. He seemed to genuinely need several seconds to rummage through his memory and reconstruct what his uncle actually did all day.

“Nah,” Tom finally grunted, letting one hand drift off the wheel to scratch his jaw. “He… uh, buys scrap engines off people in Blue Skies, takes ‘em apart in the yard, and then leaves most of ’em there.”

Pete looked over at him, eyebrows slightly raised. “That’s it?”

“Pretty much. Kinda boring, actually.”

“What happens to them?”

Tom shrugged. “Well, grass grows over ’em. Then rain and, uh, after a while, they just look like oily rocks.”

Pete couldn’t help a small laugh. “That sounds… well, environmentally responsible.”

Tom glanced sideways at him, his bruised eye narrowing slightly, as his brain worked out whether he was being mocked or not.

Pete froze, as a nervous flutter hit his ribs, and he quickly pivoted his gaze straight back out the passenger window. “S-sorry. Just a joke.”

“It’s okay,” Tom said mildly, completely unbothered a second later. “He says rust builds character anyway.”

The station wagon rolled past an old water tower, as its rust-streaked legs disappeared into a patch of waist-high weeds. Beyond it stood a pool, which Pete did not recognize at first, but he could see its faded concrete walls glowing almost white under the afternoon sun, while a few distant figures moved around the entrance.

This time, Pete rested his arm against the window and watched the town slowly grow denser. “Well, at least it gives you something to do,” he said after a moment. “Getting out of the house, I mean.”

Tom’s mouth pulled into something that might have been the beginning of a smile. “Yeah. Beats sitting around listening to him yell at the TV.”

“I bet.”

“He yells at the weather, too.”

Pete turned toward him. “The weather?”

“Yeah. Says the weatherman’s always wrong.”

Heh. Old man yells at cloud… Pete stared at Tom for a second before laughing under his breath. “I suppose that’s one way to spend an evening.”

Tom nodded solemnly. “Like, he gets really mad when they say it’s gonna rain and it doesn’t.”

“That does sound serious.”

“It is!”

Pete smiled and looked back out the window.

It was odd, honestly. There had been a time when sitting alone in a car with Tom, or virtually any of the Bullies, would have seemed like a very poor life decision on his part. (One of many.) At school, to him Tom had felt just like an extension of his group, following orders from Russell with little reason to question what he’d been told. Hell, Pete had spent months learning to recognize the sound of the Bullies’ footsteps in the hallways and calculate how quickly he could get out of reach.

Yet here he was, listening to Tom complain about his uncle’s arguments with television meteorologists. The contrast was almost comical! Tom wasn’t carrying any malice, at least right now. If he could be a bit more optimistic… he could very well say Tom seemed almost incapable of holding onto an emotion as complex as genuine hatred. But that would be an exaggeration.

Although maybe it was hinting at something? Pete began to suspect, either way, that Tom wasn’t particularly interested in hurting people unless he really needed to. Left to himself, he seemed content to drive around, eat whatever happened to be nearby, and complain about his relatives. There was something almost reassuring about the simplicity of it. He was fairly normal. Maybe the rest of his friends were too, and he just didn’t realize it…?

By the time they reached the outskirts of Bullworth Town, Pete’s nervousness had eased into something approaching comfort. Not quite friendship, surely, but certainly an improvement over the instinctive fear he’d carried around with him all year.

Tom slowed as they approached the town center. The wagon’s tires rolled heavily over a brick crosswalk near the square, making the loose change scattered somewhere beneath the dashboard rattle a bit. A pedestrian stepped off the curb ahead of them, and Tom braked with surprising patience, waiting until the man had crossed before continuing toward the row of shops.

Pete recognized the grocery store almost immediately. It had gotten some improvements lately. Its broad glass frontage was also covered with advertisements for things he didn’t really care about. Tom pulled over to the curb directly beside the entrance and pressed the brake. He shifted the gear selector into park, and the transmission answered with a weird-sounding clunk.

Wasn’t he forgetting something, though? “Oh, right. My bike.”

His bicycle was still lying diagonally across the pile of old blankets and spare tires, with one pedal sticking awkwardly toward the rear window. Pete opened the passenger door and climbed out, stepping onto the cracked pavement before walking around to the back of the station wagon. Tom was getting out as well.

“Hold on,” Tom said, popping the rear hatch open. “I’ll get it.”

Pete stepped aside, as Tom reached into the back and grabbed the bicycle by the frame. Amazingly, he lifted it out with the same lack of effort he’d shown when putting it in. How could he make these things look so easy? The rear wheel bumped against the edge of the hatch, and Tom adjusted his grip before lowering it carefully onto the pavement.

Pete caught the handlebars. “Thanks.”

Tom glanced at the crooked front wheel. “You gotta fix that sometime. It looks like it’s trying to turn left by itself.”

“I know, I’ve been meaning to.” Pete looked down at it. “But that’s probably why it keeps trying to throw me into traffic.”

Tom gave a quiet snort that might have been a laugh.

Pete straightened the bicycle as best he could, then looked back at the station wagon. “Thanks for the ride, though. Seriously. It would’ve taken me forever to get back on this thing.”

“Ain’t a big deal.”

“You gonna be okay getting back?”

Tom stared at him for a moment, apparently unable to understand why this required checking. “Why wouldn’t I?”

“I don’t know.” Pete gave a small, sheepish smile. “Just, uh, checking.”

Tom shrugged, glancing toward the road. “I’ll be fine. It’s not that far.”

“Right.”

Pete wheeled his bicycle toward the grocery store entrance, but after a few steps he looked back. Tom was standing beside the car, with one hand resting on the open hatch while he watched the traffic coming down the street. He noticed Pete looking and raised two fingers in a little wave. And so Pete smiled and waved back.

The grocery trip itself was mercifully quick, though it did little to ease his budget. The market in town was cheaper than the high-end boutique stores in the Vale, sure, but sixteen dollars and forty cents was still practically everything Pete had in his wallet. He came out carrying two large, brown paper grocery bags filled with the essentials (at least for his parents): a gallon of whole milk, a loaf of white bread, a carton of eggs, a jar of instant coffee, and a few miscellaneous vegetables that his mother would probably ask for eventually anyway.

Doing his best to ignore the midday heat, Pete immediately stopped dead in his tracks. He looked down, then at the bags, and finally he looked at his bicycle.

Right, Pete thought, feeling a sudden dread. How did I think this was going to work?

The bike had no basket, no luggage rack, and no convenient little metal hooks upon which a desperate teenager could hang several pounds of groceries. The tires were still noticeably soft, too. Trying to ride five miles back to the Vale with a gallon of milk tucked beneath one arm and a carton of eggs balanced in the other, well, that would be a fairly elaborate suicide attempt, but hardly a transportation plan. He may have been lucky last time with all the groceries, but there was no way he could stick all of them up his ass now.

Even if he by some divine intervention managed to keep the groceries intact, there was the matter of the bike itself. Pete could not afford a proper repair now, and he had spent almost everything he had just buying food. Seventy-five cents was not going to get him very far at the repair shop, unless the mechanic was willing to accept loose change and maybe some other stuff.

Pete sighed and shifted the bags against his chest, feeling the paper handles dig into his fingers. Maybe he could walk… a couple of miles… in this heat… uh… with the milk. He pictured himself arriving home several hours later with melted groceries, a sunburn, and a pair of arms that no longer functioned.

“Great,” he muttered.

He backed toward the brick wall beside the alley, trying to work out whether there was some other solution he had overlooked. His heel came down on an uneven paving stone. The stone shifted a bit, Pete stumbled sideways, and his shoulder collided with someone coming around the corner.

“Hey! Watch the merchandise, you troglodyte!”

Pete lurched forward, nearly losing both bags. One of the paper handles stretched ominously beneath the weight, and he caught the loaf of bread against his elbow just before it could tumble onto the asphalt. Whew.

“Sorry! I’m sorry, I didn’t see—”

He stopped. Standing in front of him, unexpectedly (not that he could’ve exactly expected someone he knew to bump into him…) was Melvin. He clutched a glossy stack of (presumably?) freshly purchased comic books against his chest, protecting them beneath one arm while the other hand hovered protectively over the top issue. He was wearing a mustard-yellow sweater vest over a pale blue collared shirt, with dark trousers that were slightly too short at the ankles and the same enormous black-rimmed glasses he wore around school. It wasn’t a terrible outfit, all things considered.

A canvas messenger bag hung from his shoulder, bulging with what appeared to be several hard plastic cases of polyhedral dice, a spiral notebook, and at least one miniature figurine still in its little protective box. Melvin shoved his glasses up the bridge of his nose with an unnecessarily aggressive thrust of his middle finger.

“Peter?” he demanded, staring at Pete over the top of the frames. “Watch where you’re going! You nearly inflicted catastrophic structural damage upon the mint-condition variant foil cover of Sorcerer’s Blade, issue forty-two. Do you have any conception whatsoever of the monetary and historical implications of such wanton disregard for collectible preservation!?”

Pete stared at him. He was hot, his arms hurt, but he was in no mood for such conversations.

“Melvin,” Pete said,  his voice completely flat. “I have literally no idea what you just said, but um… can you fix a bike or not?”

Of course, Melvin would hardly be the best person for this task. He wouldn’t even be someone to consider, Pete thought. If he had been lucky enough, he’d have bumped into one of the Greasers (ideally the smaller ones…) and could’ve gotten away with a good fix. On the other hand, the Nerds did have all sorts of weird contraptions and so, Pete assumed, he could get something from him if he at least gave it a try.

Melvin paused. He blinked twice, and his mouth opened and closed a few times. Seeing that he had failed to intimidate Pete, his posture deflated in an instant.

“Well… obviously I can,” Melvin grumbled, adjusting his glasses again, and thankfully sounding much less like an 18th-century nobleman. “I’m the only person in my group who actually understands gear ratios. What’s wrong with it?”

“The handlebars are crooked, the chain keeps slipping off the sprockets, and uh, the tires are practically flat. I can ride it, but barely. And now I’ve got all this.” He lifted the grocery bags slightly to emphasize the problem.

Melvin followed the motion of the bags with his eyes and seemed to notice the gallon of milk for the first time.

“You bought groceries on a bicycle?”

“I-I thought I could figure it out.”

“Well, you clearly didn’t.”

“Gee, thanks.”

Melvin sighed. He shifted the comics carefully into his messenger bag, making sure none of the corners bent in the process, and then walked over to the bicycle, where he gave the left handlebar a single poke with his index finger. The crooked metal immediately wobbled, and Melvin recoiled.

“This thing is a disaster. Where did you pull it out of, Hockomock?”

“It’s been in my garage,” Pete muttered.

“Yeah, clearly,” Melvin scoffed. He crouched beside the front wheel and ran his fingers over the tire, pressing the rubber with his thumb. His expression became even more offended. “Whoever maintained this bad boy should be prohibited from owning tools.”

“My dad probably hasn’t touched it in years.”

Melvin stood and brushed his hands against his trousers. “That explains it. Well, my tools are in the basement. We can take it downstairs and I’ll have a look.”

Pete glanced toward the grocery bags. “Downstairs?”

“Dragon’s Wing Comics, uh-duh.”

Of course, the actual secret was the basement entrance, which was tucked down a narrow concrete stairwell beside the comic shop, mostly hidden from the street by a rusted metal railing and a slightly overgrown patch of weeds. At the bottom, Melvin pushed open a heavy gray door, and Pete followed him inside.

The basement was considerably larger than he had expected. It was less a proper workshop than some sort of mix between a storage room, a hobbyist’s laboratory, and the aftermath of several unrelated yard sales. Cardboard boxes were stacked against the walls, many of them marked with handwritten labels like BACK ISSUES, MINIS, DICE, and DO NOT THROW OUT. A folding table had been converted into a workbench, covered with tiny screwdrivers, wrenches, pliers, rolls of electrical tape, bottles of lubricant, and several mysterious mechanical components Pete could not identify.

Along one wall stood a row of battered metal shelves filled with comic boxes, unsurprisingly. On another sat a half-disassembled radio, a model airplane, and what appeared to be the remains of a remote-controlled tank. Interesting. There was even a small desk fan in the corner, turning slowly from side to side with a small whine.

Pete set the grocery bags carefully on a metal folding chair in the corner, finally letting his arms hang loose at his sides. “Oh, thank God.” He flexed his fingers, discovering that they had gone slightly numb from the paper handles.

Across the room, Melvin wheeled the bicycle toward a small workbench beneath a narrow basement window. He immediately began rummaging through a metal toolbox, eventually producing a rusted adjustable wrench and a half-used can of WD-40.

“Heh. I had a motorized velocity cruiser of my own, you know,” Melvin said, as though this were a natural continuation of their conversation.

Pete looked over. It seemed Melvin had already crouched beside the bicycle and was applying the wrench to the steering column, and with enough force to suggest he was taking out several weeks (or months?) of resentment on the bolt.

“Custom-geared… reinforced chrome rims… precision-calibrated suspension. Oh, the thing was beautiful. I called her Bridgette.”

Pete leaned against the edge of the gaming table. “What happened to it? I mean, her.”

“Stolen.” Melvin said the word with such venom that Pete straightened slightly. “Some sub-human delinquent stole it from the bike rack outside the City Hall three days ago. Three days, Pete. I locked it properly, I-I used a hardened steel cable with a six-millimeter diameter… whoever took it either possessed industrial-grade bolt cutters, or was one of those illiterate Neanderthals from Blue Skies.”

Pete’s eyes dropped to the concrete floor. A stolen bike, huh.

His mind immediately supplied an image of Gary riding the mountain bike he’d shown up with the other night, the one he’d casually claimed he’d “borrowed” from some yard down the street. Then there was the other bicycle he’d used when he and Pete went to Tad’s mansion. Pete pressed his lips together.

Could it be…?

“Uh… that sucks, Melvin,” he said carefully. “People can be really awful.”

“Oh, awful doesn’t begin to cover it.” Melvin twisted the wrench again; the bolt resisted with a shriek. “This entire summer has been an unmitigated disaster. A catastrophe of epic proportions… so horrible.”

Pete watched the wrench slip slightly in Melvin’s hands. “How come?”

“My guild.” Melvin gave him an odd look. That question, to him, was so naïve. He jerked his head toward the room around them. “My, erm, clique or gang or group or whatever, as the uneducated masses insist on calling it, is in complete and utter shambles. Earnest is an absolute, irredeemable scumbag.”

The suddenness of the statement caught Pete off guard. “Earnest?” He blinked.

“Yes, Earnest.” Melvin finally got the bolt to move and gave a satisfied grunt. “If you know, you know. He is a tyrannical dictator with the strategic intellect of a turnip… everyone knows his leadership of the Nerds has been an absolute disaster since winter, if not longer, and everyone keeps pretending he’s some kind of visionary.”

Pete’s eyebrows rose. “You really don’t like him, huh.”

“I didn’t vote for him.”

He was talking about the school president election, probably. Ted won that one by a landslide, but a fellow Nerd not voting for Earnest? That would be surprising. A memory came back almost immediately to him: although no one really expected Earnest to win, and the final tally was not even close, at the time Gary had been almost fascinated by it. He was convinced, as he once told Pete, that somebody within the group had deliberately turned against him. Pete had never known who, and had just assumed it was one of Gary’s crackpot theories. But in this case…

“You voted for Ted?”

Melvin looked genuinely offended. “Ah, no. I abstained.”

“Oh.”

“A protest vote, one may say. If I voted for Ted, I know he and his boys would’ve rounded us up and… well… bind us together from our asses to mouth. Or worse, actually.” He returned his attention to the bicycle, apparently quite pleased with himself. “But Earnest didn’t deserve my mandate.”

Pete almost smiled. There was some truth to what he was saying; Earnest was a bit of a smug guy, and had always been.

“And don’t even get me started on Hopkins.”

The smile vanished.

“Jimmy?”

“I mean, what didn’t he do?” Melvin reached for the pliers, his expression tightening again. “Everyone acts like he’s some grand liberator who descended upon Bullworth to free us from tyranny. Oh, please. He’s a thug, simple as that.”

Pete folded his arms. “That’s not really fair to him.”

“Isn’t it?” Melvin pointed the pliers at him. “I mean, look at how others are treated and how he is treated. Lola, for instance. They call her a tramp, a whore, a bitch, all sorts of things, simply because she talks to boys from different cliques. She can’t walk down the street without somebody having an opinion about who she’s dating this week.”

He turned back to the bicycle and began working at the chain.

“Meanwhile, Mr. Hopkins here can spend half the year kissing girls, flirting with boys, anything in between really, beating up every student he sees in sight, and everybody applauds him for it.”

Pete opened his mouth, though Melvin continued before he could speak.

“It’s a double standard.”

“Jimmy wasn’t just—”

“Just what? Aggressive? Violent? Opportunistic? All three?” Melvin glanced back at him. “Yeah, exactly.”

Pete sighed. He knew Jimmy, sure, and maybe that was the problem. Melvin knew Jimmy too, technically, but only in the way most people at Bullworth knew him: as the kid who had arrived halfway through the year, punched his way through practically every social hierarchy in the school, and somehow emerged as the person everyone was willing to follow. But Pete (and Gary, as much as he could deny it) had seen what happened underneath all of that. He had seen Jimmy step between people when things got ugly, take punishment that should have belonged to somebody else, and had seen him stand up for people who couldn’t stand up for themselves. Like him.

That didn’t make Jimmy perfect, of course. Perhaps he was no saint. But it made Melvin’s version feel incomplete.

“At least someone like Gary could get that part running more smoothly.”

Pete’s attention snapped back toward him. “Huh? Gary?”

Melvin’s hands slowed around the bicycle chain. “Yes, Gary. What, did you think it was the tooth fairy?”

There was something different in his voice now. There was some anger there, but far less than before. “I did some nice work for him, see. Real good stuff.”

Pete’s stomach tightened a bit. “What?”

“See, I was his primary informant during the spring.” Melvin straightened a bit, clearly proud of the title. “Well, sorta. Maybe I wouldn’t say primary, but well, I knew where everyone was, who was talking to whom, what the various kids were planning, which teachers were likely to interfere, etcetera. It was a smart idea! Gary understood the value of information, see. He knew that Bullworth was, aside from being a school, an ecosystem of sorts.”

Rolling his eyes, Pete looked at him. “That’s a really weird way to describe school.”

“Oh, it’s an accurate one.” Melvin pushed his glasses higher. “Yeah, of course, everyone knows Gary was a complete sociopath, obviously. Can I psychoanalyze him? Let’s see…” He tapped his chin. “Narcissistic personality disorder, severe antisocial tendencies, pathological manipulation. But at least he had some vision. Every ecosystem requires an apex predator after all…”

Pete’s discomfort had been growing steadily since Gary’s name came up. If there was one thing he wanted to get away from this morning, it was him and his name. And yet there it was. Maybe he could help muddy the waters a bit.

“G-Gary had a lot going on, Melvin.”

Melvin glanced at him.

“I mean… his da— family, his medication… erm… mental health, uh, whatever was happening in his head. He wasn’t just doing all of it because he thought it was funny or something.”

Seemingly intrigued, Melvin stopped moving. For several seconds, he just said nothing, until he slowly turned around.

“Reasons?”

The word was almost a question, but the contempt behind it made it sound like an accusation.

“Pete, come on. The boy literally tied the principal of the school to a chair. You can’t say that’s normal.”

Pete’s eyes lowered. This was true, but, well, you see—

“I mean, we both saw it. He turned all those townies loose on students. Not to mention the riot that almost burned down the school. Do you know how hard it was to clean up the library!? He manipulated half the school into fighting one another… and when Hopkins finally confronted him, he tried to drag him down the building straight to Hell.”

Melvin’s voice only grew louder with each sentence. “Okay, yeah, you can explain why someone does something, great, sure, but that doesn’t mean you have to excuse what they did.”

Pete swallowed. “I… I know that, I get it.”

“No, I don’t think you do.” Melvin pointed the grease-stained wrench toward him. “His medical history does not erase what happened. His childhood doesn’t erase it, his medication doesn’t erase it. You don’t get to turn acts of violence into some tragic little morality play because you happen to feel sorry for him. Do you?”

The basement suddenly felt much colder. Pete looked down at his shoes. Melvin wasn’t wrong, exactly, it’s just that well… what would he say? Maybe at some point in the past, he probably would have agreed with Melvin without hesitation. Gary had been cruel, yes. Gary had manipulated people, of course. Gary had deliberately hurt people, including Pete, unfortunately. There were entire months Pete could remember only as a collection of humiliations that had somehow all led back to him.

And yet… when he thought of the Gary that had been spending so much time with him lately, following him around without demanding (too) much from him, and who had seemed almost embarrassed whenever Pete caught him looking at him for too long… well, none of those things erased what Gary had done, Pete knew that. But they existed anyway.

His hands curled slowly into fists inside his pockets. So why do I still care? Why can’t I just hate him?

That would have been easier. God, hating Gary would have made everything simple. It would have meant that Pete could look at the memories from last year and put them in the same box, label it BAD, and never open it again. How great it could’ve been. Instead, Pete had spent the entire summer letting Gary back into his life. Worse, he’d enjoyed it. That was the part he couldn’t explain to anyone, especially not himself.

The basement fell quiet around him; Pete couldn’t think of anything to say.

Melvin watched him for several seconds. The anger gradually drained from his face and, as he looked down at the bicycle, then back at Pete, he seemed to realize that he’d pushed much harder than he intended. So he cleared his throat.

“Well, um.” Melvin adjusted his glasses. “Either way, I suppose your, uh, codependent loyalty is… not entirely without utility.”

Pete didn’t move. “Hm?”

He cleared his throat. “Anyway.” Melvin tapped the rear gear cluster with the handle of his screwdriver. “Your bicycle is completely screwed.”

“What?” Pete looked up.

“Well, the bicycle.” Melvin waved the screwdriver vaguely toward the rear wheel. “You really need to stop asking so many dumb questions, jeez. Anyway…”

Melvin crouched beside the rear wheel and spun it slowly, watching the gears turn with a frown. “The rear hub is cracked, and several of the gear teeth are stripped. The chain isn’t sitting properly either, which explains why it keeps slipping. Honestly, I’m surprised you managed to ride it this far without losing a wheel.”

Pete stared at the bicycle. “How bad is that?”

“Bad enough that I can’t fix it with what I have here.”

“Can’t you just—”

“Well, I can replace the assembly. But I don’t have the replacement part.”

How great. Pete’s expression fell again.

Melvin wiped his hands on a dirty rag. “I’ll have to order it from the hobby catalog in Bridgeport. Assuming they actually have the correct model in stock, which I think they should… it should arrive tomorrow afternoon.”

“Tomorrow?” Pete glanced toward the grocery bags sitting on the metal chair. At that point, the milk was already beginning to warm. “Melvin, you know I can’t carry two bags of groceries five miles back to my house.”

Pete picked up one of the bags and immediately regretted it when the handles dug into his fingers. An unenthusiastic Melvin just rolled his eyes.

“Calm down, Pete. I am not entirely without, heh, logistical solutions.”

“What does that mean?”

Instead of answering, Melvin walked toward the far corner of the basement. There was an old canvas tarp piled against the wall, partially concealed behind several unimportant cardboard boxes. He grabbed one corner and pulled. The tarp came away with a whump, and something red emerged from beneath it.

It was a tiny motorized scooter!

In fact, the thing looked almost comically pristine compared with everything else in the basement. How Melvin had one was beyond him. It was bright red beneath what might have been a thin layer of dust, with a rounded chrome headlight, a faded plastic footboard, tiny wheels, and a large wire basket bolted to the front. It didn’t look much like a serious vehicle, though it could be something a particularly ambitious grandmother might use to visit places.

Melvin stood beside the scooter with his chest puffed out, with one hand resting proudly against the handlebars. “This, my little friend,” he announced, “is my secondary urban transport vehicle. Well, not so secondary anymore at this point, but you get it.”

Pete looked back at the scooter. “Your what?”

“Am I really gonna have to repeat myself every time?” Melvin brushed a little dust from the red casing. “This is a 1998 Honda Elite. Fifty or so cubic centimeters, exceptional fuel economy, um, compact wheelbase… it’s great.”

Pete stared at him, then down at the machine, trying to process the sight. It wasn’t all that bad, honestly, and seemed to be spotless beneath the dust. “So you have a scooter,” Pete said flatly.

“Yes.”

“You’ve had a scooter this whole time?”

“Obviously.”

“Why?”

Melvin frowned. “Because bicycles are inefficient. Look what happened when I started riding one. But the point is,” he gestured toward the scooter. “You will use the scooter to transport your low-grade dairy products and other nutritional provisions back to your domicile.”

Pete’s eyebrows went up. “Wait. You’re actually letting me ride this?”

“Lending,” Melvin corrected him, folding his arms so tightly his knuckles pressed into his sweater vest. “What? You will return it tomorrow when you retrieve your bicycle. Provided you don’t drive it into a ditch, of course…”

A puzzled Pete looked at the little red machine again. It was really beautiful but, uh… wasn’t all of this too random to be happening to him? With Melvin, too, of all people?

“But you’re actually lending this to me? For real real?”

“I am. For real real, not for play play.”

There was a brief pause.

“But why? We’re not even that close, what’s the catch?”

Melvin seemed genuinely puzzled by the question. “Look, you are one of the few people on campus who doesn’t actively throw objects at my head. Furthermore, you have proximity to Jimmy, and several members of the various cliques. To me, this presents a potentially valuable intelligence-gathering opportunity…”

“You mean gossip?”

He sighed. “No.” Melvin pointed toward the scooter. “So, do we have an agreement?”

Pete looked at the grocery bags, then at his broken bicycle, and then back at Melvin. This was all so ridiculous that Pete didn’t even have a proper explanation in his head for what was going on. On the other hand, considering the circumstances he was in, maybe that was a good thing.

“Okay,” Pete said. “Deal.”

Melvin nodded, apparently satisfied. “Oh, excellent.”

“Thanks, Melvin. Really.”

“Don’t thank me yet.” Melvin held up a greasy finger. “Wait right there.”

Melvin immediately disappeared behind the workbench and returned carrying an enormous silver motorcycle helmet.

“What is that?”

“Safety equipment, friend.”

“But that thing is huge,” Pete said, backing up half a step. “It’s like, the size of a watermelon.”

“Because your head is apparently huge.”

“It’s not!”

Melvin shoved the helmet into his chest. “Put it on, for God’s sake.”

Pete reluctantly took it. The helmet was comically oversized, covered in glittering silver paint that reflected the room lights, and with a dark plastic visor that made it look like something salvaged from a low-budget science-fiction movie.

“Does this actually belong with the scooter?”

“It belonged to my older cousin during his, erm, short-lived moped phase,” Melvin said dismissively. “But that’s irrelevant. You know me… safety protocols are still mandatory.”

Pete looked down into the dark cavity of the helmet. Yeah, there was no point in arguing with that. Maybe there was, but at this point Pete was beginning to get annoyed. He was hot, exhausted, and really wanted to get his groceries into a refrigerator before he had to explain to his mother why he’d wasted his money on curdled milk.

“Fine,” Pete sighed, gripping the chin bar. “Give me the keys before I change my mind.”

A few minutes later, with Melvin grudgingly shoving from the rear and Pete hauling on the handlebars, they managed to push that heavy little machine up the concrete ramp alongside the stairwell, and out onto the sunny sidewalk of Bullworth Town. Pete hoped no one would see him wearing a helmet that made his head look roughly twice its normal size. To his relief, he had managed to fit most of the groceries into the wire basket, although it required some careful rearranging. The coffee jar went into the corner with the loaf of bread resting carefully on top, the vegetables were wedged around the sides, and the gallon of milk sat in the middle, where it would (hopefully) remain upright. The eggs were too fragile to trust to the basket, unfortunately, so Pete used a piece of stray twine to secure the carton to the rear luggage rack, checking the knot three times before deciding it would probably hold.

He pulled the enormous silver helmet over his head. The foam padding squeezed his cheeks inward. Pete looked at his reflection in the dark visor— wow, he looked ridiculous. There was no other word for it, he could die on the spot right there of embarrassment. But he snapped the visor down anyway.

“Great.” He inserted the key, and the scooter gave a reluctant click. When Pete kicked the starter, the scooter’s tiny engine buzzed to life. It sounded like a weed-whacker, and Pete winced. At least this one was working. He checked both directions down the street, and fortunately, nobody he recognized was nearby. That was something. He twisted the throttle, and the scooter lurched forward.

Pete instinctively tightened both hands around the handlebars, and later relaxed when he realized he was moving at the speed of a jogger, something close to it. As the scooter puttered toward the bridge, Pete kept glancing over his shoulder despite himself, half-expecting someone he knew to witness yet another spectacle of his humiliation. Not even Charlie Brown had endured such torment in his life.

He could already imagine Jimmy laughing. Worse, he could imagine Gary laughing. Would they ever let him live this down? Pete further tightened his grip on the handlebars. Don’t think about it, don’t think about it.

By the time he reached the bridge, the town had begun to fall away behind him, replaced by somewhat quieter roads leading toward the Vale. The wind rattled against the plastic visor, as his groceries shifted softly in the basket.

Pete should have been thinking about getting home, or perhaps how he was going to explain to his parents why he was showing up home with a random scooter out of nowhere. And despite doing his best to ignore the rest of his encounter with Melvin, some of the guy’s words still kept coming back to his head. Something something ’you can explain why someone does something, but it doesn’t mean you have to excuse what they did.’

He knew that, yes. He wished knowing it made things easier, but it didn’t. And as the little scooter carried him onward through the afternoon, there was also a question Pete had been trying not to ask himself, but as with all others, it followed him all the way home.

Why did he still care about Gary?
