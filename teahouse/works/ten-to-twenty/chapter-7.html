Chapter Text

The Smith household was an enormous, soulless pile of brick and slate sitting on a corner lot in the Vale. Its horrid lemon bleach smell sure helped portray it as a boring, stable-ish kind of house to the neighbors, who didn’t actually care what was going on in there anymore. The hedges were trimmed within an inch of their lives, the windows were always clean, and nothing was ever left on the front steps. In fact, nothing ever grew where it wasn’t supposed to, and the lawn looked as though somebody inspected it with a ruler.

Inside, the ceilings were vaulted, the hardwood floors polished to a cold, if slippery glare, and every piece of furniture looked as though it had been purchased straight from a showroom catalog. It was fascinating, from a different perspective. There were decorative bowls nobody was allowed to put anything in, expensive paintings nobody looked at, and silver-framed photographs of the Smith family at various ages, most of them featuring people standing beside one another rather than actually together.

Gary sat in the dark on the floor of his bedroom, his back pressed against the foot of his king-sized bed and his knees pulled tight against his chest. The curtains were drawn despite the afternoon sun outside. He had been sitting there long enough for the room to become stuffy, but he hadn’t bothered turning on the air conditioning. There was something comforting about the heat; it made the room feel smaller.

Downstairs, he heard the front door slam shut, and Gary went completely still. A moment later came another sound, this time the tread of expensive leather loafers crossing the foyer. His father’s footsteps were distinctive, generally slow but never really hurried. After all, there was no reason for him to hurry.

Gary listened as the sound moved farther into the house. A brief pause, he heard, and then a muffled clink of ice cubes against crystal. This came from the study, followed by a click of what he assumed was the television remote. Yeah, his father was home. But Gary didn’t move, and barely breathed either. He didn’t know what the television was saying through the floorboards. Some financial program, probably? His father liked that stuff, watching people argue about numbers while pretending he was also making important decisions. Fantastic.

What was not so fantastic was how Gary’s jaw still throbbed. The black eye he had not too long ago had mostly faded into a sickly yellow-green, and hiding it wasn’t so hard, but the deeper bruise along his cheekbone still burned whenever he chewed. Two of his lower ribs gave a sharp, agonizing pinch whenever he took a breath that was too deep. It was quite painful; how he managed to keep this a secret so far was beyond him.

That had been his father’s welcome-home gift the afternoon Gary was discharged from the county hospital, thanks to the whole ‘I fell from the sky’ incident that he obviously didn’t believe. He could still remember standing in the foyer, barely able to keep his balance, while his father looked him over.

“Expelled?”

He said it so low, so quiet. But Gary had barely had time to answer before the first punch landed. He’d spent the rest of the afternoon wondering whether his father had been angrier about the expulsion itself or about the fact that somebody else had already found out about it.

You think I pay fifty thousand dollars a year for you to make a laughingstock out of this family with that street trash?

Gary had heard the sentence so many times since then that it no longer sounded like his father’s voice. It sounded like something recorded and played back inside his skull. He shifted slightly against the bed, and pain flared beneath his ribs. He opted to stop moving.

The worst part was that, honestly, none of it had even mattered. His father was already fixing everything; he already guessed this would be the case, but all the calls he’d overheard just confirmed it. The board of trustees, the headmaster, one of his father’s friends at some stupid yacht club… someone at the athletic fund… and probably other people he’d never heard of, but whose titles apparently mattered.

It’d be all fine and dandy. Just two large donations to the school, a couple of lunches with Dr. Crabblesnitch, some carefully chosen words about Gary’s “unfortunate disciplinary misunderstandings,” whatever would be enough. And then the school would take him back. They always did.

Money spoke in Bullworth, and his family (on his dad’s side, at least) had enough of it to drown out any amount of broken glass, burned gyms, tied-up headmasters, or other things that would have gotten an ordinary student thrown out for good. If it worked for the Harringtons, it’d work out well for him too… but Gary hated it.

It was amazing, but he hated the hypocrisy of it. His father could reduce the whole disaster to a series of phone calls and donations, as though Gary hadn’t spent an entire year turning the school upside down. But he also hated the fact that some part of him had expected exactly this.

He looked around his room. Everything was immaculate, sort of. His books were arranged neatly on the shelves, his clothes were folded, his desk was spotless (except for a glass of water he’d forgotten to finish). Even the carpet looked untouched. It was like he’d never been here! And Gary hated every square inch of it. There was no living being there anymore.

His mother was another matter. A while ago, she had gone back to New Coventry, where she’d spent most of her life before marrying his father. She still had a place there, he recalled, a small apartment on the poorer side of town that his father pretended not to know existed. It was no wellness resort upstate, but she liked it. Their marriage had never quite ended, to be fair, at least not on paper, but it’s what had happened. Every so often Gary would visit her, usually with a trunk full of groceries or something she’d mentioned needing, and he’d take care of whatever she couldn’t manage herself.

He never talked about it at school, not even to Pete. (There were things best left unsaid.) He’d thought about going to see her after the hospital. He’d even considered calling, too. But what was he supposed to say? Hi, Mom. I got expelled, Dad beat the hell out of me, and I’m going back to Bullworth anyway because my daddy’s money can fix everything.

She couldn’t see him like this. Oh, she couldn’t. And so Gary had stayed away. He tried not to think about where she was, or whether she’d heard what happened, or what she might have thought when the news reached New Coventry. It was easier that way.

This all meant that, for now, the house belonged to his father and him. (Technically, anyway. In reality, it belonged to his father, and Gary was merely another object inside it.) What a great turnaround for him… Gary had spent the entire school year strutting around Bullworth like an emperor. And what a great emperor he’d been! He’d looked down his nose at everybody. He’d called Jimmy a destitute street rat, among many other things, and used those brainless Preppies as his personal piggy banks, as much as he could. He’d convinced himself that he was different from all of them. Smarter, more ruthless, less pathetic.

The thought almost made him smile. Almost. Because the truth was that Gary was just as filthy, just as abandoned, and just as completely ruined as any of them. Maybe even more? He let his head fall back against the mattress with a thud and stared up into the pitch-black ceiling. His head was pounding. There was this vicious migraine bored into his temple, like an electric drill, and his hands trembled where they rested against his shins.

I need a pill. His chest tightened. God, I need something.

He reached blindly toward the nightstand, his fingers scraping across the bare mahogany. Nothing. Gary froze for a second, as though another bottle might somehow appear if he waited long enough. But that didn’t happen, naturally. Then he dragged himself upright, wincing as pain shot through his ribs, and stumbled into the en-suite bathroom.

He slapped the light switch, and the bulb came on with a harsh buzz, reflecting off the spotless white tile and making his headache flare behind his eyes. Gary hissed and turned away, with one hand shielding his face as he crossed to the sink. The mirrored medicine cabinet rattled when he yanked it open. Rows and rows of empty amber bottles stared back at him.

Gary gripped the edges of the porcelain sink and stared at his reflection. God, he looked awful. His skin had taken on a grayish cast beneath the bathroom light. When did that happen? His hair was greasy and unwashed, and also flattened on one side from spending too much time against the bed. His pupils were huge, black and glassy, which made his eyes look almost cartoonishly wide. It was almost as if his eye color wasn’t brown. Oh yeah, and his hands wouldn’t stop shaking. This was not good.

There was something almost funny about it, in retrospect, even if in a twisted or sick kind of way. When he’d stopped taking his medication last year, he’d felt invincible. He’d felt, say, awake, for the first time in his miserable life. Every thought seemed to arrive already connected to the next one, his mind moving so quickly that everyone else appeared to be standing still. It was great! Plans multiplied faster than he could write them down. Every corner at Bullworth became a map, every student became a piece, and sure enough, every weakness was something he could find if he looked long enough. He’d thought that was what being free felt like…

For most of his childhood, his parents had been happy to let private psychiatrists decide what was wrong with him. They hadn’t known much about ADHD, or hadn’t cared to learn. They knew only that their son was loud, erratic, restless, impossible to keep still, and of course, embarrassing to have around. So they had handed him over to doctors. One pill, then another… a stimulant… a sedative… something, anything to make him focus, make him sleep. His brain quickly became some sort of chemistry experiment conducted by people who barely knew him.

Gary remembered one treatment particularly well. He’d been twelve. Six months of some experimental tranquilizer whose name he couldn’t remember anymore. At the time, he’d believed it was turning him into a different person. Maybe it had? He didn’t know.

What he did remember was the fear he developed, this paralyzing terror of dirt. He’d spent hours convinced that everything around him was contaminated. His hands felt sticky even when they were clean. His clothes felt filthy, he felt filthy. The bathroom floor seemed to crawl beneath him. He remembered sitting right here, on this very floor, sobbing until he threw up, then scrubbing his hands with soap until the skin split across his palms and bled. He’d been convinced that if he wasn’t perfectly sterile, something inside him would begin to rot.

His father had called it an episode, one of many. His mother had called it unfortunate, but his psychiatrist had just called it a side effect. Gary had called it hell. He’d stopped taking the pills at school because he was sick of being told what was wrong with him. It had felt very much like a declaration of independence, one of the best there ever was. He had won, he was free. Except he actually wasn’t; the human brain, as it turned out, wasn’t particularly interested in Gary’s philosophies.

It was terrible, but it couldn’t run forever on adrenaline and paranoia and whatever else he’d been feeding it. Eventually there had been nowhere left to go but down. Maybe the bell tower incident had been the beginning of the collapse. Now, the only way to make his thoughts quiet was to drown them.

For the past month, he’d been surviving on whatever medication he could find: leftover prescriptions, pills he’d managed to steal, forgotten bottles, so on and so on, but generally anything that would take the edge off, or at least slow his head down enough for him to sleep. He’d been careful, mostly, but his supply was gone. He’d taken his last two sedatives yesterday morning before going over to Pete’s house.

Gary stared at the empty bottles, and his reflection stared back. Then he thought of Pete. His fingers tightened around the edge of the sink.

The carnival, the lights, all that shitty-cheap cotton candy, the way he’d let his guard down… the way he’d actually let himself smile, let himself hold Pete’s hand like some pathetic, lovesick teenager on a sitcom… and then…

Hopkins.

Gary’s teeth ground together so hard his jaw ached. Jimmy-boy had been standing there beneath the carnival lights, holding that ridiculous purple bear, staring at him with those cold, judgmental blue eyes. And what had Gary done? Jimmy was no longer his running dog. He’d run. How exciting it was to see the great Gary Smith, mastermind of Bullworth’s downfall, panicking and bolting the moment Jimmy looked directly at him.

The shame of it crawled beneath his skin. If he didn’t want Pete to see him this way, oh, he couldn’t let Jimmy see him like this either. He couldn’t let him think Gary was weak. Worse, he couldn’t let him think Gary had been beaten. Again.

Jimmy had crawled out of some broken-down trailer park, whatever slum he came from, fought his way through every clique in school, and somehow come out the other side with people actually liking him. Now he was walking around town like a king, surrounded by friends, holding court at his stupid beach house, gathering all the boys and girls while everybody else laughed along with him.

And Pete would be there, with him.

Gary pushed himself away from the sink. The room swayed for a second, and he caught the edge of the counter and waited for it to settle.

Jimmy was going to take Pete away from him, yet again.

That was what snakes like Jimmy did. He’d smile, act noble, tell Pete he was only looking out for him, and explain that Gary was dangerous and manipulative and wasn’t worth the trouble. Pete would listen because Pete always listened. He’d look down at his shoes, say that maybe Jimmy was right, and eventually stop answering when Gary came around. The thought made something in Gary’s chest seize.

No. This wasn’t right, none of this was right. He needed to think, he needed to get his head straight.

But his skull felt as though somebody had driven a nail through it, and every breath sent a sharp pulse through his ribs. Gary’s hands were still trembling. His thoughts were moving too quickly and too slowly at the same time, colliding with one another before he could get hold of any of them. There was little he could do to scrape out the pain from his skull. There was no bullet-shaped hole where the answers should be.

Shit. He needed something. Painkillers, muscle relaxants, sedatives— anything, really. He didn’t particularly care anymore, he would beg for it if he needed to. Anything. Gary splashed cold water over his face and stood there dripping for a moment, staring at the drain. Then, gradually, a name surfaced in his mind…

Kirby.

He hadn’t thought about Kirby in weeks, and there hadn’t really been any reason to, especially after the Jocks fell in line with Jimmy back then. The thing is, Gary had stumbled across some useful information almost by accident back in the spring. He’d been looking for something else — something involving the Bullies, as he vaguely remembered, what was it again?—, and had ended up seeing Kirby and Trent behind the equipment cage near the football field after hours. At first he’d assumed they were just screwing around, and maybe they were. It wouldn’t be of any surprise; if someone came up and told him that Kirby and the other jocks weren’t virgins anymore but instead were bearing fruit at Island Def Jam or something, Gary would believe it. But then he’d realized what was actually going on.

He hadn’t said anything, and hadn’t needed to. This discovery had instead gone into the mental filing cabinet where Gary kept all the things he might someday find useful. And it turned out Kirby had access to medication. Gary remembered hearing him complain a couple of times about his shoulder after football season, remembered the bottle of prescription painkillers he’d mentioned having at home, and remembered enough about Kirby’s parents to know that the kid would rather chew through his own arm than let them find out he was giving anything away.

Gary stared at himself in the mirror. A small, unpleasant smile appeared. Oh, there you go. Something useful for once. He dried his face, went back into the bedroom, and opened the closet. Most of the clothes hanging inside were expensive enough to make him angry simply by looking at them. Polo shirts, button-downs, blazers he’d worn once; he hated all of these. He didn’t feel respectable.

On the other hand, he didn’t even particularly want to look respectable right now. And so he pulled out a dark gray T-shirt and a pair of black jeans that weren’t wrinkled. After a moment’s consideration, he grabbed a dark hoodie as well. Good enough.

He looked at himself in the mirror; he looked bland, really bland. Fuck it. Bland was fine. Gary pulled the hoodie on, ran his fingers through his hair without really fixing it, and left the bedroom. To make things easier, he took the back staircase instead of the main one. The house was fairly quiet, but his father’s television was still on, so nothing to worry about. Gary moved through the utility room and eased open the side door.

The night air hit him immediately. For a few seconds he simply stood there, breathing, before he remembered he had better things to do. He crossed the lawn toward the garage. Thankfully, a very kind and smart mountain bike he’d “borrowed” from a friend earlier in the week was still hidden behind the hedge. He dragged it free, checked the tires, and climbed on.

He rode downhill toward the edge of the district, ignoring how the ache in his ribs worsened every time he pushed too hard on the pedals. The streets became quieter as he left the Vale behind. At the edge of the neighborhood stood a gas station that, as far as he knew, stayed open all night. Gary leaned the bicycle against the curb and crossed to the payphone beside the air pump.

He dug two quarters from his pocket. For a moment, he stared at them. They were shiny quarters, and they were finally going to be of use. Then he fed them into the slot and dialed Kirby’s number from memory.

The phone rang, once, twice, thrice. Gary tapped his foot against the asphalt. When is this bastard gonna pick up?

“Come on.” Four rings, and then, finally, a click.

“Hello?” Kirby’s voice was thick with sleep.

“Kirby! My man.”

There was a brief pause. Then what Gary assumed was a muffled rustling sound, followed by a door closing somewhere on the other end.

“G-Gary? What the hell? It’s almost midnight.”

“You don’t say,” Gary drawled, though his breath caught on a sharp throb in his chest.

“Where are you calling from?”

“Doesn’t matter.” Gary leaned against the casing of the payphone, closing his eyes against another throb of pain. “I need a little favor from you.”

Kirby was silent.

“W-what kind of favor?”

“You still have those pills from your shoulder?”

Another pause. “What?”

“You know, those Mr. Burton kept stuffing into you and your brainless monkey friends. You have them?”

“I don’t—”

“Kirby.” Gary’s voice remained somewhat quiet; he didn’t need to threaten him yet. “I know you have some…”

He exhaled shakily. “But Gary, I told you, I’m out of that stuff. School’s over. I don’t want any trouble.”

Gary almost laughed. “What, you thought I called because I wanna cause trouble? I mean, me? Trouble?”

“Why else would you—”

“Because I need them.” That came out a bit more honestly than Gary intended, but he didn’t have time to think about this now. There was another silence, and he could hear Kirby breathing on the end of the line.

“What happened to you?”

Gary’s jaw tightened. “Nothing. T-that’s not your business.”

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Jesus, man.”

Gary looked down at the pavement, watching his own warped reflection ripple in a puddle of rainwater pooled beneath the air pump. “Look. I just need something to take the edge off, alright? That’s all.”

Kirby didn’t answer right away. When his voice returned, it had lost most of its edge. “You’re still taking stuff?”

“Do you have them or not?”

“I… I might have a few left,” Kirby muttered reluctantly. “In my bathroom cabinet, um. But I don’t know if my parents—”

“Good. Just don’t let them see.” Gary glanced toward the gas station windows. A clerk was watching television behind the counter, unaware of his surroundings. “Just bring it outside, I’ll come get it.”

“But Gary, I really don’t think this is a good idea. Jimmy said—”

Gary’s eyes closed. The mere mention of Hopkins nearly made his vision spark red, and for a second, the headache seemed to swallow everything else. The fact he was relying on such an illiterate meathead for his own survival made him feel quite vulnerable, but there was only so much he could do. “I don’t give a shit what Hopkins said, and neither do you,” Gary snarled. “Just hurry up already.”

Kirby swallowed hard on the other end, letting out a long sigh. “Fine. Just give me ten minutes.”

Nodding even though Kirby couldn’t see him, Gary smirked. “Ten.” And then he hung up.

Kirby’s house was a fairly modest, single-story ranch with a patchy front lawn and a pickup truck parked crookedly in the driveway. The living room window glowed with a blue, flickering light of a television, but the rest of the house was dark. Gary left the bike beneath the shadow of an overgrown maple tree and crossed the lawn quietly, trying not to put too much weight on his ribs as he climbed the concrete steps.

He had barely raised his hand to knock when the front door cracked open. The devil—Kirby, that is, stood there looking miserable. He was wearing an oversized pair of plaid pajama pants and a faded gray Bullworth Bullhorns tank top that looked okay, actually. His hair was sticking up in every direction, and his brown eyes were wide. Aside from that, he looked alright.

“Get inside,” Kirby whispered.

He caught Gary by the sleeve and pulled him through the doorway before Gary could object, then eased the door shut until the latch clicked softly into place. The foyer was small and carpeted, with a door to the basement immediately to the left and a narrow hallway leading toward the kitchen. Interesting.

“My parents are watching a movie in the den,” Kirby said under his breath. “If my dad sees you here, he’ll take a bat to both of us.”

Gary pulled his sleeve free and leaned against the wall. Despite the pain in his ribs, he managed a faint smirk. “Relax, guy. How’s your night? Where’s your buddy Trent? Tucking you in?”

Kirby’s expression hardened, and his knuckles tightened around the edge of the hallway doorframe. “Just take your damn pills and get out of here, Smith.”

He reached into his pocket and produced a small amber plastic vial with most of the label torn away. Gary could see the tablets moving inside when Kirby held it out. Vicodin? Maybe not strong enough to knock out a horse, but more than enough to quiet the screaming in his skull.

For a moment, Gary simply stared at it. His fingers twitched, then he reached down and pulled a crumpled twenty-dollar bill from his pocket and dropped it onto the little table beside the door. He took the vial from Kirby’s hand, turned it over once, and opened it almost immediately. Two tablets landed in his palm. He swallowed them without water, grimacing at the bitter taste that coated his tongue.

Kirby watched him closely. “Jesus, Gary,” he said after a moment. “You look like shit.”

“Oh shut up, I’m fine.” Gary’s shoulders stiffened a bit.

“Really, you’re shaking.” Kirby looked him over again, taking in the sweat gathering along Gary’s hairline and the way he was holding himself. Hmm. “When was the last time you actually ate something?”

Gary’s eyes narrowed. “I said I’m fine, dumbass. I don’t need your medical evaluation.”

Sighing, Kirby didn’t bother arguing back. He disappeared into the kitchen and returned a moment later with a tall glass of cold tap water, which he pushed into Gary’s hands.

“Drink it.”

Gary just stared at him.

“Come on, you look like you’re going to pass out on my rug.” Kirby folded his arms. “And I really don’t want to explain your dead body to the cops.”

Every instinct in Gary told him to throw the glass against the wall. He could already imagine the look on Kirby’s face if he did it, the shock followed by that stupid concern of his. Why was he so worried? He was fine. Gary wanted to remind him who was in control. He wanted to remind himself, too. But because his throat felt like sandpaper, and the room had begun to tilt gently beneath his feet, he drank it.

The water was cold enough to hurt his teeth, and he swallowed it in long gulps until the glass was empty. It did little for the ache in his head, but the nausea settled enough for the room to stop moving. Gary handed the glass back.

“There,” he muttered, wiping his mouth with his sleeve. “Happy now?”

“Thrilled.”

Kirby set the glass on the table and glanced toward the darkened hallway. “Now go out through the back gate. You can cut through the alley behind the garage. It’s safer. And uh, I don’t want anybody seeing you leave through the front.”

Something hot rose in Gary’s chest. “The back gate?”

Kirby looked at him.

“Pftt. No, I came through the front, and I’ll leave through the front.”

“I’m serious, man.”

He shoved the vial into the pocket of his hoodie. Gary’s pride was already bruised badly enough without being treated like some pathetic secret Kirby needed to dispose of before his parents noticed. “I don’t need you telling me how to leave.”

Kirby reached for his arm. “Gary, don’t be an idiot—”

“Don’t touch me.” Gary slapped his hand away before Kirby could grab him properly. The movement sent a sharp pain through his ribs, but he ignored it, turning toward the door and gripping the brass knob.

“Gary—”

He pulled the door open, and warm air rolled into the foyer. Gary stepped onto the porch with his chin held high, refusing to look back. He didn’t care who saw him. It’s not like Kirby was a drug dealer or something. Gary made it down two steps before realizing he had walked straight into something solid.

The impact almost drove the breath out of him. Pain shot through his bruised ribs so suddenly and violently that his vision flashed white. He stumbled backward onto the wet grass, with one hand flying instinctively to his side.

“Watch where you’re—”

The rest of the sentence disappeared when he looked up. It was Lucifer himself. Jimmy stood on the walkway beneath the yellow porch light. He was wearing faded blue jeans, beat-up skate shoes, and a brown canvas jacket with his hands shoved into the pockets. An unlit cigarette hung loosely from the corner of his mouth, and he had a skateboard tucked beneath one arm. He looked pathetic.

For several seconds, neither of them moved. Jimmy stared at Gary as though his eyes were refusing to accept what they were seeing. This wasn’t real. None of this was real, he wasn’t living through this once again.

Behind him, Kirby made a small, horrified noise. “Ohh, shit.” But Gary barely heard him.

Jimmy’s expression changed slowly. First came surprise, then recognition, and finally something colder as his eyes narrowed. Gary’s stomach dropped; his face felt numb. How could it be? He had spent the entire ride from home thinking about Jimmy, about what Jimmy might do to Pete, about what he might say. All of the crappy arguments, accusations, and smug little speeches about how Gary was a very bad guy and couldn’t be trusted… as much as Gary had imagined being prepared, he had not imagined this.

His gaze moved briefly from Gary to the house behind him, then back again. That glare only pinned Gary to the earth, as if he were an insect under a microscope. Gary’s heart began hammering. Unlike last time, there was nowhere for him to run.

Jimmy took the cigarette from his mouth. “Gary?”

Oh God, Gary’s mind screamed, panic flashing red and blinding behind his eyes. Oh no. Oh no. Oh no.
