Chapter Text

It couldn’t be. The breath left Gary’s body in one violent gasp. The impact drove a white-hot jolt of pain through his lower ribs and made his knees buckle beneath him. Although he reached out instinctively, his sneakers skidded across the dew-slick crabgrass and he went down hard, catching himself badly on one elbow. For several seconds he couldn’t breathe.

His vision narrowed until the edges of the lawn seemed to dissolve into a grayish haze. His chest spasmed around the dull ache in his ribs, and he sucked desperately at the air without getting enough of it. Get up, his brain shrieked at him, as panic clawed at his throat like bile. Get up, move, run, get the fuck up, do something. But his body seemed to have forgotten how.

He scrambled backward on his palms anyway, kicking at the grass as he tried to put some distance between himself and the figure standing on the walkway. His eyes darted toward the street, then the driveway, then the narrow gap between Kirby’s house and the neighboring fence. He was already calculating routes without meaning to, looking for an alley, a gate, anything he could use to get away. There had to be a way, right? Right? But there was nowhere to go.

Jimmy stood beneath the yellow porch light, with his shadow stretching across the lawn. He hadn’t moved at all, and hadn’t even raised his hands.

Gary’s mouth opened as he tried to say something. A sneer, an insult, or any good enough remark that would put him back on familiar ground. His mind offered him several possibilities at once. He could tell Jimmy that his mother was a drunk. He could call him a balding, illiterate ape. He could remind him that, for all his heroic bullshit, Jimmy was still just some stupid delinquent who happened to get lucky.

And yet none of it came out.

“H-Hopkins…”

The name emerged as a wheezing rasp. It wasn’t a taunt, and Gary hated himself for that almost as much as he hated the shaking in his hands. His mouth still tasted bitter from the pills he’d swallowed barely five minutes earlier, and sweat was running down the side of his face despite the warm summer night.

Jimmy stared at him. That was all. The mere sight of him standing there brought the bell tower back with such force that Gary’s stomach lurched.

God. The cold rain, stone slipping beneath his shoes… the bell tower roof disappearing into darkness behind him. That and the awful emptiness at his back as he realized there was nothing beneath his heels but open air. Then the fall, more broken glass, dust filling his mouth and nose, and the thought that his life had ended before he had even reached the ground. But that memory vanished as quickly as it had arrived, and when it did, his brain was quick to remind him that Jimmy was standing only a few feet away.

Oh, yes. The person who had beaten him into the ground, watched him fall, and had somehow walked away from all of it looking stronger than before. Gary’s stomach twisted.

But when he saw Jimmy’s face properly, there was seemingly no anger in it. No smile, no grin, and none of the satisfaction Gary had expected to see. If anything, he looked stunned, and perhaps he could understand why. Beneath the surprise, however, there was something else that Gary recognized almost immediately: pity.

No. Not this. Anything but this. This realization was unbearable to even process, and Gary felt the blood rush into his face. He tried to scramble backward again, but his heel caught in a patch of loose dirt and sent him sideways. His shoulder struck the trunk of a maple tree, and he instinctively curled in on himself, pulling his knees toward his chest and bringing his arms up over his head. His whole body was trembling now.

He could feel it in his shoulders, his hands, even his teeth. The bark pressed painfully against the back of his head as he tried to make himself smaller, and somewhere between one breath and the next, a broken sound escaped his throat. At this point, he was practically begging. He knew what he looked like… a broken, sniveling dog waiting for the boot to come down. He hated this, all of this.

“Gary.” Jimmy’s voice was low and strangely careful, causing Gary to flinch as though he’d been struck.

“D-don’t.” He swallowed and tried again, but his voice cracked before he could force it into anything resembling his usual confidence. “Don’t fucking touch me, Jimmy.”

Jimmy didn’t move toward him, but instead took a small step backward, sliding his hands out of the pockets of his jacket and holding them loosely at waist height, with his palms open.

“I’m not gonna hit you, man.”

Gary stared at him through the narrow gap between his forearms. His chest was still heaving, and tears had gathered in his eyes without his permission. He blinked furiously, trying to clear them before Jimmy could see how close he was to losing control completely.

“Bullshit,” he whispered. “You’re gonna jump me. Y-you and your goddamn friends.”

“There’s nobody here except me, Gary.”

Jimmy sounded genuinely confused, or maybe even baffled. His brow furrowed as he looked at Gary’s shaking shoulders, as though he couldn’t understand how the same person who had spent months provoking fights and manipulating everyone around him could now be sitting terrified against a tree.

To be completely honest, Jimmy had spent the last few days at least slightly irritated out of his mind. Seeing Gary at the carnival had set off every alarm bell in his head, convincing him that the psycho was worming his way back into Pete’s life to stage another coup. He’d half-expected to find Gary lurking in the shadows with an army of townies, or planting firecrackers under his bed. Maybe both! But he really, really hadn’t expected to find him curled up in a ball on a jock’s front lawn, sobbing like a child.

Jimmy had seen a lot of kids look scared at Bullworth, both younger and older. He’d seen nerds cry when their lunch money got stolen, and he’d seen preppies whine when their sweaters got torn. But this was different, and the sight left an uncomfortable pressure behind Jimmy’s sternum. He let out a slow breath and lowered himself slightly, trying to keep his voice from sounding too sharp.

“Come on,” Jimmy said. “Get off the grass. You’re getting your pants all muddy.” And he reached down with one hand, palm open.

Gary stared at it. His jaw worked, and for a moment he looked as if he might slap the hand away purely on principle, as he had done on other occasions. But whatever adrenaline had carried him this far seemed to have burned itself out. His face was pale, his breathing uneven, and his legs were shaking too badly for him to trust them. He didn’t take Jimmy’s hand.

Instead, he planted one palm against the tree and hauled himself upright. His knees nearly gave out before he managed to straighten, and he leaned heavily against the rough bark, clutching his side with one arm while wiping his face with the sleeve of his hoodie. That would suffice. Jimmy kept his hand out for another second, then lowered it.

Behind them, the screen door creaked open. Kirby stood in the doorway, quite pale and wide-eyed, holding a small plastic sandwich bag containing what looked like a couple of grams of weed wrapped in silver tinfoil. He looked from Gary to Jimmy and seemed to realize, with growing horror, that the situation had somehow become much worse than it had been ten minutes earlier. He wouldn’t have minded it had it happened during the day, or at least somewhere other than in front of his house.

Jimmy turned toward the porch. “Got it?”

Kirby nodded quickly. Satisfied, Jimmy took two strides up the steps and held out a crumpled ten-dollar bill. Kirby glanced toward the darkened den, then quickly exchanged the baggie for the money.

“Thanks man,” Jimmy muttered. Kirby barely looked at the cash. His eyes remained fixed on Gary, who was now standing near the curb with one hand gripping the handlebars of the mountain bike he’d dragged out from beneath the maple tree.

“Is… um, he gonna be okay?” Kirby whispered; his voice cracked as he looked back at Jimmy. “He looked real weird when he came inside. I swear to God, I didn’t—”

“Yeah yeah, don’t worry, I’ll take it from here. Go get some sleep.”

Jimmy said it without much force, but Kirby seemed relieved to have been given an instruction he could follow. He slipped back through the screen door, and the inner door shut behind him a moment later, as the deadbolt slid into place with a heavy click. Finally, Jimmy tucked the baggie into the inside pocket of his jacket, then looked back toward Gary.

At that point, Gary had moved to the curb. He was holding the bicycle with both hands now, though using it more as a support than a means of transportation. His knuckles stood out white against the rubber grips, and his head was lowered so far that his chin nearly touched his chest. Aside from that, his breathing still came in shallow, ragged pulls. Jimmy walked down the driveway, picking up his skateboard and tucking it beneath his left arm.

“Come on,” he said, nodding down the street. “Let’s walk.”

Gary didn’t answer. For a few seconds he remained where he was, staring at the pavement. Every instinct in him was urging him to get on the bike and ride as fast as his legs could carry him. He could already picture himself pushing off, gaining speed, putting the whole street between himself and Jimmy before Jimmy had a chance to react. It would’ve been fascinating, as a matter of fact.

But the thought of trying to pedal with his ribs like this and his knees still unreliable was enough to stop him. Besides, Jimmy had always been faster than he looked. If Gary fell, Jimmy would catch him. If he ran, Jimmy would follow. There was no version of this in which Gary escaped with any dignity intact.

With a stiff, jerky motion, he pushed the bicycle forward and began walking alongside the curb. Jimmy fell into step beside him.

For a while, neither of them really said anything. The neighborhood was fairly quiet, except for the occasional chirp of insects in the hedges. Gary kept his eyes on the road, concentrating on placing one foot in front of the other. The pills were beginning to blur the sharpest edges of his pain, thankfully, but they had done nothing for the humiliation crawling through his stomach. This was all mortifying.

Jimmy glanced sideways at him, as his brow furrowed beneath his short-cropped hair. “You gonna fall over?”

Gary swallowed hard. “…no.”

“You look like you’re gonna fall over.”

“Then stop looking at me.”

Jimmy gave him a long flat look, but surprisingly didn’t push it. He shifted the skateboard tucked under his left arm, letting his other hand drop loosely into his jacket pocket as he matched Gary’s sluggish, uneven pace. Of course, Gary hated that more than he would have hated a lecture. Jimmy wasn’t gloating or threatening him. He wasn’t even asking the questions Gary had spent the last hour imagining; he was simply walking beside him. And that made Gary feel exposed in a way that violence never had. It was if his skull had been cracked open and all his rot was out on display. What was he thinking!?

They crossed another intersection, as the asphalt giving way to more cracked sidewalk beneath an overarching canopy of dark oak trees. After that, Jimmy spoke again. “So, well. What the hell happened to you?”

Gary’s knuckles tightened over the rubber grips until the tendons in his wrists ached. “Nothing.”

“Right…” Jimmy muttered.

“I’m fine,” Gary snapped, although his voice was trembling before he could smooth it out. “Always have been.”

“Yeah, you said that already.”

Gary stopped walking. The sudden movement made his ribs flare with pain, but he ignored it and turned toward Jimmy, forcing himself to stand straighter.

“What, what do you want me to say?”

Jimmy stopped too, and let the skateboard hang loosely at his side. “I don’t know. Something that isn’t bullshit would be a nice change.”

Gary opened his mouth, but the words failed to arrive. His head felt heavy, and his throat was still raw from the panic. He looked away before Jimmy could see how close he was to shaking again. After another while of silence, Jimmy stopped twirling the cigarette between his fingers and tucked it behind his ear. He glanced down at the bicycle, then back at Gary.

“Nice ride,” he said, almost casually. “Didn’t know you were into mountain bikes. Looks expensive.”

He felt heat creep up the back of his neck. His fingers tightened around the handlebars until his knuckles went pale. “Yeah, yeah,” he muttered, his voice still rough from crying. “None of your damn business.”

Jimmy gave a quiet snort but let it go, and amen to that; Gary didn’t particularly want Hopkins discovering that his grand return to criminal genius consisted of stealing bicycles from people. (Which he, of course, never did. This was all rumors.) They walked another half block without speaking. It wasn’t comfortable silence, exactly, but it was strangely ordinary, given everything between them in the last year. But eventually Jimmy cleared his throat.

“Look, Gary… uh, about that day on the roof.”

Gary’s shoulders tightened immediately. “There’s nothing to talk about, James.”

“I know there is, seriously.” He kicked a loose pebble into the gutter. “Pete’s been losing his mind trying to play social worker for both of us,” Jimmy continued. “And, yeah, seeing you two together at the carnival pissed me off. I thought you were pulling the same crap again. Getting close to him, making him think everything’s fine, then waiting until you can screw him over.”

Yeah, right.

“But I’ve been thinking about what happened up there that day.” That made him look over, and at that point Jimmy had stopped walking. Gary took another step before realizing he was alone in the middle of the sidewalk. And so he stopped as well, letting the bicycle settle against his hip. He still couldn’t bring himself to turn around completely.

“You kept backing up,” Jimmy said. Gary stared at the front tire. “I told you to back off, and you kept coming closer. You were practically daring me to do something.” Jimmy’s voice was quieter now. “And then when I reached for you, you didn’t hit me, and uh…”

It didn’t make sense. Why was he bringing this up? Didn’t he have better things to worry about, better people to worry about? Even if he wasn’t trying to sniff Pete’s ass, he had that girl Zoe or his other concubines. Maybe he should’ve just beaten Gary instead of bringing up this topic; Gary’s breathing became shallow again.

“And so I’ve gone over it a bunch of times, uh,” Jimmy said. “And I don’t know what the hell you were thinking. Maybe you wanted me to fall? Maybe you wanted me to go over with you, I dunno, maybe you just weren’t thinking at all, with your head and everything. But all that said…”

Jimmy hesitated before finally asking, “Were you trying to fall, Gary?”

For a moment Gary was back there. Oh, the bell tower, the rainy gray sky, and his boots slipping against the stone. That and the sickening sensation of empty air where the ground was supposed to be. One thing he knew in that instant was that if he stopped fighting, there would be nothing left to manage, nothing left to fix, and nothing left to explain. It would make everything so much easier…

His stomach turned. “Shut up,” Gary whispered.

Jimmy frowned. “Hey, I’m just asking.”

“I said shut the fuck up.”

Gary turned around so quickly that the bicycle nearly slipped from his grasp. He felt as if his face was blotched and wet, his eyes red-rimmed, and his expression was contorted by something. Desperation, maybe, anger, possibly. What was he even doing anymore?

“Don’t stand there pretending you understand me,” he said. His voice shook badly enough that he had to stop and swallow. “Y-you don’t. You don’t know what was in my head. You don’t fucking know what happened. You don’t— you never know anything.”

Jimmy didn’t move. “You’re right,” he said after a moment. “I, uh, I don’t. But I know you almost died. And I know I almost died with you.”

Gary opened his mouth, searching for something clever enough to push him away again. Yet nothing came. Given the circumstances, there was no joke, no insult, and no elaborate explanation that could turn the conversation into something he controlled. Even though there was no one else nearby, he felt surrounded. For a moment, Gary thought he might be sick.

Jimmy sighed and rubbed the back of his neck. “Look… I don’t hate you, Gary.”

How could he even say something like that? How cynical did one have to be… to be in such denial of things around them? Jimmy didn’t hate Gary, sure, he just beat the crap out of him for the love of the game. He punched and kicked everyone because he thought it was all fun and games, oh wow! And people believed this charade of his. Gary gave a short, bitter laugh. It came out dry and ugly, with none of his usual theatrical amusement behind it.

“Right. Sure you don’t.”

“I’m serious.” Jimmy let his hand fall. “Yeah, of course I was pissed at you when you turned the school against me. Or Russell, or half of the clique leaders… oh yeah, I might’ve hated you when you tried to drag me through a fucking skylight.”

Gary’s eyes flickered.

“But right now?” Jimmy continued. “After seeing you like this? I-I’m not this evil mastermind, man. I don’t even have the energy for it. School’s over, great, and we survived it. If barely.” He offered Gary a faint, crooked smile. “Hell, I got the souvenirs to prove it.”

Before Gary could work out what he meant, Jimmy dropped his skateboard onto the grass and reached for the hem of his canvas jacket. He shrugged it off, tossed it over the bicycle’s handlebars, and then caught the bottom of his white undershirt.

“Hopkins, what the hell are you—”

Jimmy pulled the shirt over his head. This movement exposed the broad, uneven planes of his back beneath the streetlamp. His shoulders seemed to be still bruised in places, and three thick, jagged scars ran across the skin between his shoulder blades, pale pink against the lingering discoloration. They crossed one another at awkward-ish angles before disappearing toward his spine. This was all, of course, thanks to that fall.

Gary stopped speaking. He had seen the scars before, of course… his scars, that is. At the hospital, in the confusion after the fall, there had been some blood and torn clothing here and there, but nothing too unusual for him. Jimmy’s, though, well, he never thought about it; he actually thought the bastard had gotten away unharmed, which at first glance he mostly did.

But now, there was nowhere else to look. Jimmy turned his head slightly. “Pretty ugly, huh?”

Gary’s gaze followed the scars down the center of his back. He remembered the sound of glass breaking beneath them, and the tearing sensation in his own skin. It was one of the strangest feelings he had ever felt, seeing the world tipped sideways and the ground vanishing. Hell, he remembered waking in the hospital and discovering that his body hurt in places he hadn’t known could hurt.

That, combined with his other injuries beneath the hoodie, from the ribs to the tender skin around his eye, oh, all that pain had become so familiar. When he tried hard enough, he could barely notice it. But he was under so much stress that seeing Jimmy’s scars made his issues feel, for the lack of a better word, unfamiliar again. It made them visible.

Jimmy pulled the shirt back down, covering the marks. “I’m just saying, you’re not the only one who got messed up.”

But Gary looked away before Jimmy could catch the expression on his face. “Put your fucking clothes on properly,” he muttered. “Nobody needs to see your disgusting, hairy back.”

“It’s not hairy, dipshit.” Jimmy dragged the undershirt over his head and shrugged back into his jacket. Gary kept his eyes on the pavement, pretending the concrete was suddenly fascinating. (Not many knew this, but he’d always been interested in geology.) And the brief glimpse of Jimmy’s body had been, erm, distracting, certainly, but that wasn’t what had left him feeling unsteady. It was the scars. The knowledge that Jimmy carried them too, that the fall had not simply happened to Gary and then disappeared into the past, it was not something either of them could forget.

Jimmy picked up his skateboard. “So,” he said, as though they had merely paused to discuss the weather, “what were you actually doing at Kirby’s house?”

Gary stiffened. “Nothing. Just talking.”

Jimmy raised an eyebrow. “Right. You were having a friendly late-night chat with a guy you were never close to? How cute.”

“Fuck you.”

“Well, I was there to buy some grass to share with Zoe,” Jimmy said, tapping the front pocket of his jacket. “I heard from a kind friend that Kirby gets good stuff from the docks. And unless you’re some sort of affair of his, which… heh… I don’t think you are, there’s only one reason you’d be knocking on his door at midnight.”

Gary’s eyes narrowed. “Oh wow. You’re a drug addict, James. I would’ve never guessed how far you have fallen.”

“And you’re a hypocrite.” Jimmy’s tone remained irritatingly level. “You were buying stuff, man. I don’t care what it was, I’m not gonna judge, but you gotta stop lying your ass out of every situation.”

Gary opened his mouth. He could have called Jimmy stupid, accused him of inventing things, told him to mind his own business. That would’ve gone well, too. But instead, the words stalled somewhere behind his teeth. He felt as if the Vicodin was finally beginning to take effect. The headache had softened a bit into a more dull-ish pressure, and the pain in his ribs had blurred a bit around the edges. But this relief did not restore him. It only loosened the last threads holding him together.

Jimmy stood beneath the streetlamp. He smelled a lot like cheap tobacco and saltwater, which Gary found repulsive at best and disgusting at worst. His jacket was now hanging from his shoulders and his jeans sagging in comical ways, like they always did. He looked exhausted, also underdressed. And unfortunately, alive.

Gary looked at him and thought of the scars. Then he thought of his own again, and something in his chest gave way.

“You…” he began.

The word came out so quietly that Jimmy almost missed it. “What?”

He swallowed, and his fingers tightened around the bicycle’s handlebars again. Gary knew this was the right time to strike, the right time to say something cruel… tell Jimmy that he had no idea what he was talking about, and had no right to stand there acting like some wounded saint after everything he had done. He was no saint. So why couldn’t he just say it…?

Instead, what came out was, “You came to that school.”

Jimmy frowned. “Well, I mean, didn’t we all…?”

“You came from nowhere,” Gary continued. His voice trembled, though he tried to disguise it with a sneer. “A stupid, illiterate, bald piece of trash from a broken home.”

“Hey! I’m not bald—”

“And you took everything.”

Jimmy went quiet. Gary stared at the ground, and his vision began to swim. No, not again. He blinked hard, but the tears had already gathered, blurring the streetlamp into a pale smear.

“You took my allies. Then you took the school. A-and when that wasn’t enough you took…” He drew in a breath that caught halfway down. “You took Pete away from me.”

His mouth twisted as though the sentence had left a bad taste behind. Jimmy shifted his weight a bit, quite uncomfortable with wherever this was heading. “You know Pete’s not something you can take.”

Gary gave a small, broken sound that might have been a laugh, if it hadn’t collapsed before reaching the end. “Oh, but everyone likes you. Everyone thinks you’re a hero.” His voice grew louder, then faltered. “Nobody gives a fuck about me.”

He pressed the heel of one hand against his eye, trying to stop what was happening. Yet it only made the tears spill faster. His breathing became uneven, so shallow and hurried, and he bent forward over the bicycle as though the handlebars were the only thing keeping him upright.

“My dad hates me,” he said. “My mom ran away, and I’m—”

A joke? A freak? A low-life? The thing everyone wanted to get rid of? The words seemed to catch on something inside him. Any of those would have been easier than the truth, honestly, which was that he didn’t know what he was anymore. A sob escaped him before he could swallow it.

Gary froze at the sound. His face tightened with shame, and he tried to straighten up, tried to pull himself back into some recognizable shape. But at that point, his body had already begun betraying him. His shoulders shook, his breath hitched and broke apart, and finally, he gripped the bicycle harder, lowering his head until his forehead nearly touched his arm.

“You ruined my life,” he whispered; Jimmy said nothing. “You took everything.” Gary’s voice broke completely. “And I have nothing left.”

Although he’d half-expected his crying to arrive in one outburst, as it occasionally did, it instead came in waves he couldn’t control. He kept trying to stop, each time managing only a few seconds before another sob forced its way through. And then his ribs began to hurt again, and the pain made him gasp, which only made the next wave worse.

“Nothing,” he repeated, though by now the word had become almost inaudible.

Gary kept his head down, with one hand still locked around the bicycle’s handlebar. His shoulders shook beneath the hoodie, and every time he tried to draw a proper breath, it caught somewhere in his chest and came back out as a ragged little gasp. He wiped at his face with the sleeve of his other arm, but the tears kept coming, so warm against skin that already felt hot and swollen.

A puzzled Jimmy stared at him for maybe ten or fifteen seconds. Then he stepped closer, slow enough that Gary could have moved away if he’d wanted to. Gary noticed the movement only when Jimmy’s hand came to rest between his shoulder blades. He flinched, but the hand stayed there.

Jimmy’s palm was quite warm through the hoodie, and his fingers spread loosely against the fabric. Whatever he was trying to do, he was giving Gary something solid to lean against while the worst of the crying worked its way through him. Gary’s grip on the bicycle loosened, and he let his forehead sink against his arm.

After a while, Jimmy finally said, very quietly, “You don’t have to talk.” That was all, apparently.

Gary squeezed his eyes shut. As another sob shook through him, smaller this time, he let it happen. He didn’t have the energy to hide it anymore.

Earlier that evening, by the time Pete finally wheeled the scooter back toward Dragon’s Wing Comics, the sun had already begun to sink behind the buildings in Bullworth Town. He had spent the entire afternoon hiding out in his bedroom doing next to nothing (aside from his very exciting drawings), staring at the ceiling and feeling vaguely guilty about keeping that machine — even if Melvin had insisted he hold onto it. Hell, it hadn’t even been six hours, but keeping a cherry-red motorized scooter concealed in his garage had been far more stressful than it was worth.

Turns out Melvin hadn’t needed to wait on the mail courier after all; after Pete left, he’d managed to salvage a compatible rear freewheel from an old, rusted ten-speed frame tucked behind the comic shop’s boiler. If only he had such luck in the rest of his life…

Pulling the little red Honda into the alley behind the comic shop, he found Melvin sitting on an overturned milk crate with a screwdriver in one hand, and a stack of graph paper balanced on his knee. He looked up as Pete approached.

“Greetings, fellow warrior… I see you have returned.”

“I said I would.”

“Well, most people don’t.”

Pete parked the scooter against the wall and removed the enormous silver helmet. His hair was flattened on one side from wearing it, and he was beginning to understand why Melvin had seemed so offended when he’d suggested that the thing made him look ridiculous. (Even though it did.)

“Your helmet,” Pete said, holding it out.

Melvin took it carefully. “No cracks?”

“Not unless you count the ones in my dignity…”

Melvin considered this. “Pftt. I think neither of us have dignity anymore.”

Pete wasn’t sure that was true, but he let it go. Then he noticed the bicycle. It was leaning against the workbench, and for the first time since he’d found it in the garage, it looked… fine, actually! The crooked handlebar had been straightened, the tires had been pumped, and even the chain had been cleaned and properly seated. Crouched beside it, Pete turned the pedals with one hand. They moved quite smoothly.

“Oh wow. You fixed it.” Pete stood and looked at him. “Thank you, really.”

Melvin adjusted his glasses. “You’re welcome.”

It was probably the closest thing to gratitude Pete was ever going to get from him, and Pete was perfectly happy with that. He had expected the bicycle to remain a barely functional emergency vehicle, assuming it worked for long. Instead, Melvin had somehow transformed it into something that might actually get him home without throwing him into traffic. It was amazing!

Pete ran a hand over the handlebars. “Seriously. Thanks.”

“Oh, it was nothing.” Melvin gave a small, almost embarrassed shrug. “I like doing these things.”

If only it ended there, though; Melvin, naturally, wasn’t finished with him.

He took Pete for a ride around town on the scooter, ostensibly to show him a few “areas of interest,” which turned out to mean a fairly elaborate reconnaissance tour of the Greasers’ territory. Why the Greasers? He had no clue. They spent the better part of an hour circling their usual hangouts in New Coventry, watching who came and went, and stopping whenever Melvin decided something looked suspicious. Pete had no idea what any of it was supposed to accomplish, but he went along with it. After all, he clearly owed Melvin one.

At least he learned something: Peanut was apparently an excellent chess player, although this was a discovery Melvin seemed to regard as far more important than Pete did. If any of this would be useful, though, he’d probably not know anytime soon. But if things kept at this rate, he wouldn’t be surprised if Melvin managed to oust Earnest from commanding the Nerds. Maybe that wouldn’t be so bad. If God made the world in six days and rested on the seventh, it would be okay if anything from the eighth day onward was made by Melvin.

By the time Pete finally got home, he was quite exhausted. He parked the newly repaired bicycle in the garage, ate something small, and went upstairs. He changed, brushed his teeth, other normal, boring stuff, and climbed into bed expecting to fall asleep almost immediately. But he didn’t.

For some reason, his mind wouldn’t settle. He would close his eyes and find himself thinking about the same things, over and over again. Sometimes he wasn’t even sure whether he was dreaming or remembering a dream. The same images seemed to circle endlessly through his head: some lights (carnival lights?), Gary disappearing into the crowd, the black plastic bag from the other nightmare, Jimmy asking questions, Gary asking questions, God. Pete turned over and tried to think about something else.

A few minutes later, he was thinking about these things again. It took some time for him to get to sleep, and he woke up several times during the night with the odd feeling that his dreams had continued without him. And every time he woke up, he felt less rested than he had when he’d gone to bed, so lethargic and, of course, very irritated with his own brain for refusing to leave anything alone.

And at five-forty-five in the morning, Pete woke up to the sound of wood rattling against a doorframe.

He bolted upright in bed, with his heart hammering against his ribs, and his breath catching in his throat. For a confused second, he thought he was still trapped in a dream, but there was seemingly none. A tinny buzz in his ears eventually dragged him back into reality.

His fingers found the thin white cords of his headphones, and he pulled one of the earbuds loose. His old iPod lay face-down beside his hip, and from what he could see, it appeared its battery icon was reduced to a desperate sliver of red. What a bummer. Faintly, through the plastic speakers, came the raspy organ chords of Electro-Shock Blues by Eels. Pete frowned and rubbed the sleep from his eyes. He couldn’t even remember what he’d put on before falling asleep. Jamiroquai, probably. But how had the playlist managed to wander all the way into depressing nineties indie rock before sunrise?

Thump. Thump.

Pete stopped rubbing his eyes. The sound came again, sharper this time, vibrating through the floorboards. He began to realize it wasn’t coming from the window, but rather, from his bedroom door.

Huh. Pete froze, staring at the brass knob as a sudden chill shot through him. Someone was inside the house. Had his dad already unlocked the back door? He did some stuff in the early morning from time to time, mostly related to his library inventory. Or worse… had Pete forgotten to throw the deadbolt after sneaking back in last night?

Swallowing hard, he pushed the blankets aside and crept across the cold floorboards, as his heart hammered against his ribs. Pete almost never locked his door, so he wondered why whoever knocked on it didn’t just, well, come in. His hand closed around the knob. Unsurprisingly, it turned easily. Pete pulled the door open, already beginning to say Mom, and stopped when he realized that it was Gary who was standing in the hallway.

The pale morning light from the staircase window caught him from behind, leaving most of his face in shadow. He looked as though he’d spent the night being dragged through a swamp, or worse. His hair was tangled, maybe even damp, hanging over his forehead, and his face had gone almost unnaturally pale, with deep hollows beneath his eyes. His eyelids were red-rimmed and swollen, as if he hadn’t slept in a while, and the hoodie he was wearing smelled a lot like grass. Crushed grass, to be specific. Maybe even a little bit of tobacco smoke?

It was all so unusual. How did he even get inside? Was this even Gary and not some sort of hallucination? He simply stood there with his arms hanging loosely at his sides, and for a few seconds neither of them moved. After what might’ve been twenty or thirty seconds or so, Gary swallowed. When he finally looked up at Pete, there was something almost embarrassed in his expression.

“Pete,” he muttered, with his voice hoarse and quiet, “Uh… I know I’m early but, um, do you mind if I use your shower?”
