Chapter Summary

Gary pays Pete another visit of his own.

Chapter Notes

This took way longer than necessary. It turns out trying to work on a story an hour before you go to sleep is a bad idea...

It might also be worth noting that, barring any other developments in writing, the last chapter should be the final one. (They grow up so fast!) I tend to have a poor idea of how long a story will last for, but I'm far more accurate when it comes to guessing when it will end - but thank you all in advance for keeping up with it!

Chapter Text

Pete stood rooted to the threshold of his bedroom, with his bare toes curling against the wood as he stared at the figure in front of him. For five or six seconds, his brain seemed unwilling to make sense of what he was seeing. He had spent the last eight hours assuming Gary had simply vanished into the night air somewhere, disappearing back into whatever shadowy corner of town he retreated to whenever things stopped going his way. He’d been doing this a lot.

And yet here he was! How original.

Gary stood on the runner rug outside Pete’s door, with water (or perhaps just dew?) dripping from the frayed hem of his dark hoodie onto the floorboards. His hair was disordered, his face pale beneath the dim light, and there was something so exhausted about the way he stood that, for one moment, Pete wondered whether he was actually looking at him or some particularly unpleasant hallucination. It would’ve been a better explanation than whatever the hell was going on in here.

“You’re… here,” Pete whispered.

It was an obvious thing to say, and he knew it the moment the words left his mouth. It wasn’t like Gary lived here, but uh… still. His hand was still clamped around the brass doorknob.

Though he expected Gary to make some comment about Pete’s hair sticking up in three different directions from the pillow, he didn’t do that. He simply shifted his weight and hunched his shoulders slightly against the draft coming down from the staircase. His hands were buried deep in the pockets of his hoodie, but Pete could see his fingers trembling through the fabric.

“Oh, brilliant deduction, Petey,” Gary muttered. His voice was a bit cracked and rough, as though he’d spent the night shouting himself hoarse. “Now… are you gonna let me stand in your hallway until your old man wakes up, or what?”

Pete blinked. The mention of Dad cut through the last of the sleep fog. His father was probably still asleep, but he was an early riser, especially on library inventory days, so it was only a matter of time before he woke up. If he came out of the bedroom and found the one and only Gary Smith standing outside their bathroom looking like he’d been sleeping under a bridge, there was a fair chance the roof of the house would come off.

“Right. Okay, um, yeah,” Pete whispered, stepping into the hallway and gesturing toward the bathroom a few feet away. “But shh, keep your voice down. Seriously. The floor squeaks in some places…”

Gary didn’t need to be told twice. He crossed the runner, letting his sneakers make a faint squelching noise that made Pete wince, and slipped into the bathroom. Pete followed immediately, pulling the door shut behind them until the brass latch clicked into place.

The bathroom was fairly small, tiled in pale yellow porcelain that held the cold, and the small window above the radiator was somewhat clouded with early morning moisture. The room also smelled faintly of his mother’s floral hand soap and freshly washed towels. It was such a good smell, reassuring even… and somehow this made Gary’s presence in the middle of the room seem even stranger.

Pete stayed with his back against the closed door, folding his arms across his chest. Now that they were under the bathroom light, Gary looked worse than he’d thought. Maybe it was just his vision, but his face wasn’t merely pale; it had a grayish, unhealthy cast to it, and he thought he saw a yellow-green bruise along his cheekbone standing out against his skin. His eyelids looked swollen and red, and there were faint tracks of dried tears cutting through the grime along his cheeks.

Pete stared at him. Oh. He had been crying, he thought. And hard. The realization made something twist uneasily in Pete’s stomach. Gary almost never cried. At least, not in any way Pete had actually witnessed. In the ten years he’d known him, Pete could probably count the number of times he’d seen Gary lose control of his face on one hand, and most of those memories ended with someone getting punched, something being thrown, or Gary deciding that whatever had upset him was everybody else’s fault.

Sometimes all three, too, but this was different.

“Gary,” Pete whispered. The questions came all at once, tumbling over each other before he could decide which one to ask first. “How did you even get in? I-I’m pretty sure the front deadbolt was locked.”

Gary turned away from him and reached for the shower knobs. The pipes inside the wall groaned a bit, followed by a sudden hiss as hot water began rattling against the fiberglass tub. “Basement bulkhead,” he muttered.

Pete stared. “What?”

“Yeah, the padlock’s rusted through on the left hinge. I just jimmied it with a pocketknife.” Gary paused, adjusting the temperature with one hand. “Told you that thing was a hazard years ago, but apparently none of you morons could fix it.”

“You broke into my basement?”

“I entered your basement,” Gary corrected. His familiar dismissive drawl was there, but only barely. There was no real bite behind it. “Semantics, Pete. I know what that is, unlike you.”

Pete stared at the back of his head for another second. “And where were you?”

Unsurprisingly, Gary said nothing.

“You disappeared at the carnival,” Pete continued, his irritation finally beginning to catch up with the shock. “A-and then you let go of my— ran into the crowd, and left me standing there like an idiot in front of Jimmy and Zoe. Then you took off on the bike, left me standing there alone, and vanished for eight hours. What was I supposed to think?”

He paused, wrinkling his nose.

“And jeez, why do you smell like that?”

Gary glanced over his shoulder. “Like what?”

Pete hesitated. What was it like? Crabgrass, mud, cigarette smoke, maybe a mix of these, but he was also pretty sure he smelled cheap weed on him. Not that he’d done it, erm, of course, but he knew that smell. He’d encountered it often enough around Bullworth, usually drifting off some of Jimmy’s Townie friends when they showed up next to him, though every once in a while one of the Jocks or even a couple of the Nerds would come to school smelling the same way. Pete had never had much interest in finding out what they were smoking, but the smell sometimes tended to cling to clothes and hair in a way that was difficult to mistake.

“You smell like you’ve been rolling around in a ditch,” Pete said.

Gary looked back toward the shower. “Long night.”

“What does that mean, where the hell were you?”

“I was out,” Gary snapped, grabbing the edge of the vinyl shower curtain and yanking it along its plastic rings with a harsh screech. “Come on, I took care of some stuff. I don’t have to give you a play-by-play of my social calendar, femme-boy. You’re not my parole officer, and you’re definitely not my mother.”

“Well, somebody has to be, apparently!” Pete hissed, trying to keep his voice down even as his chest heaved. “Because you keep showing up at my house in the middle of the night, dragging me into things, acting like we’re fine, and then the second Jimmy walks into the room, you run away like a coward!”

Gary stiffened. He stood beside the tub with one hand resting against the shower curtain, and his back still turned to Pete. For a moment he said nothing.

“Turn around,” he said quietly.

“What?”

“Turn around, Petey. I’m getting in.”

Pete’s face burned. He instinctively spun on his heel, facing the closed bathroom door and the wooden towel rack. He had seen Gary in the dorms more times than he could count, but there was something rather, erm, awkward about standing two feet away while Gary stripped down to use his family’s shower.

“I’m turned,” Pete muttered through his teeth. “I’m not looking, I promise.”

Behind him came the squeak of wet rubber soles stepping over the edge of the tub. Pete waited for the sound of clothes hitting the tile floor, or maybe a shoe being kicked aside first, but curiously, nothing came, and Pete frowned at the door. He heard another squeak, followed by the hiss of water striking something that seemed much heavier than bare skin. But he decided he didn’t want to know.

“I’m tired of it, Gary,” Pete said, raising his voice enough to carry over the pipes. “I’m really, really tired of it. You just… just come around whenever you feel like it, you act like we’re best friends again, you drag me to all kinds of places and eat stupid stuff… a-and then you just leave. You vanish into thin air, and then I’m the one left standing there trying to explain to Jimmy why I’m hanging out with the guy who tried to kill him.”

Inside the shower, Gary lowered his head. The water was hot enough to sting against the cuts across his knuckles. And unbeknownst to Pete, Gary hadn’t taken a single thing off. His hoodie grew heavier by the second, clinging to his shoulders and arms, but he didn’t move, or at least tried his best not to. The noise filled his ears, yet Pete’s voice came through it clearly enough.

“And what did you expect, anyway…?” Pete continued, as the frustration that had been building since the carnival finally spilled out. “I wanted things to be normal, I wanted all of us to be friends. I thought maybe—maybe if you stopped planning everything, and Jimmy stopped fighting everything, we could just hang out. Like normal people.”

He swallowed.

“But you two are so obsessed with each other that neither of you even sees me anymore. Or when one of you does… then either it has to be with other people, or you just abandon me when I stop being important to you.”

Again, Gary said nothing.

Pete stared at the bathroom door. “Look, Jimmy told me about the whole roof story,” he said, quieter now. “He asked me if you were trying to fall on purpose.” His voice caught slightly. “Because, um, you didn’t even try to punch him, Gary. You just pulled him over with you—”

The shower curtain suddenly rattled.

“Oh, of course!” Gary shoved the vinyl aside so violently that the rings clattered along the rod. “Jimmy told you. Jimmy explained it all, didn’t he?” His voice cracked as it rose. “Because Saint Jimmy knows everything. Ah, yes, I forgot Jimmy’s the expert on everybody’s fucking brain now.”

Pete flinched but kept his back turned. “Gary, shut up. My dad—”

“Jimmy this, Jimmy that!” Gary shouted over him. “Jesus, every time you open your mouth, his name comes out. I can’t go five fucking minutes without hearing about Hopkins. Oh, Jimmy’s so brave. Oh, Jimmy’s so tough. Jimmy beat the Preps, Jimmy saved the Nerds, Jimmy did this, Jimmy did that. Well, fuck him! He can suck my dick for all I care.”

The shower curtain slapped against the side of the tub. “And you stand there looking at me like I’m some pathetic charity case! Oh, yes, isn’t that great?”

Pete’s hands tightened around his pajama sleeves.

“Like you’re doing me some huge favor because you let a shitass like me sleep on your floor. You think you’re so noble, Pete? You think handing me a bowl of canned soup once when I was in the school basement makes you some kind of saint? Do you think I’m just some Make-A-Wish kid you need to make happy?”

By that point, Gary’s voice had gone strangely uneven. Pete finally turned his head a fraction, then stopped himself.

“You’re just as selfish as everybody else,” Gary said. “You only care about feeling like the good guy.”

Pete’s jaw clenched so hard his molars ached. How could he say all of this, after everything? He’d spent days worrying about Gary, making excuses for him, wondering where he was, whether he was safe, and whether he would disappear again. Hell, he’d stood up for him to Jimmy, he’d let him sleep in his house, and he’d given him food, clothes, space, practically anything he asked for. He did it because Gary was his friend. And yet this was how he was being treated.

The injustice of it, just having his concern thrown back in his face like garbage, seemed enough to snap the last thread of Pete’s patience. Forgetting his modesty, and forgetting that he had sworn he wouldn’t look, Pete whirled around to face him.

“Don’t you dare—”

He stopped.

Gary was standing in the middle of the bathroom, soaking wet from head to toe, and noticeably still fully dressed. His hoodie hung heavily from his shoulders, his jeans were plastered to his legs, and water streamed from his hair and sleeves in steady rivulets, gathering around his sneakers and spreading across the linoleum.

Pete stared at him. “You’re… oh— you have your clothes on.”

“Of course I have my clothes on, you idiot!”

Gary took a furious step toward him. His face was flushed beneath the pallor, his hair stuck flat against his forehead, and his upper lip trembled with his own effort of keeping his voice from breaking.

“You think I care about my clothes?” he shouted. “You think I care about anything right now? Look at me, Pete! Look at what you did!” He jabbed a shaking finger toward the right side of his face, toward the pale scar running from his eyebrow down across his cheek. “I have this because of you!”

For a second his anger faltered, and Pete could only stare at the scar, this familiar line he’d seen on Gary’s face for six years. He never thought things would come to this. He didn’t need Gary to point it out; if anything, Pete had memorized every millimeter of that scar a long time ago.

He remembered Gary at ten years old, practically vibrating out of his skin during an otherwise fairly calm August afternoon, where the world couldn’t spin fast enough for both of them. Gary had set up a row of dead glass insulators on the rusty catwalk of an abandoned railcar, daring Pete to knock them off. Prove you’re not a sissy, Petey. Throw it harder. Harder! And when Pete had finally found his nerve, winding up with a fist-sized chunk of railway ballast, Gary had abruptly lunged forward across the tracks — not to dodge, that is, but to snatch the target away mid-throw just to prove Pete was too slow. And then it hit him.

God. Pete had watched Gary drop like a puppet into the gravel, blood instantly sheeting down the right side of his face, so bright and so hot. But Gary hadn’t screamed in pain. Even then, with blood pouring between his fingers, he had other concerns on his mind. Don’t let my dad see, Pete, don’t let him see. He’ll kill me. He’ll fucking kill me. Gary had whimpered, clawing at Pete’s shirt.

“You know that was an accident,” Pete said. His voice was fairly quiet. “You know damn well it was.”

“You threw the fucking rock!”

“Because you told me to!” Pete’s voice came back louder than he intended, echoing off the yellow tile. “You were jumping around like a maniac, daring me to throw, and you stepped right in front of me! I didn’t know you were going to move!”

He swallowed hard; he really didn’t want to recite the logistics of a day they had both bled through. But the memory was right there between them, so what else could he do?

“You think I don’t remember?” Pete’s voice dropped. “I was ten. I thought I’d put your eye out, man. I remember I stole iodine from my mom’s cabinet and sat in the dirt behind your garage for, I don’t know, hours? Just pressing gauze against your face, until my hands were shaking so bad I-I dropped the tape.”

Gary’s mouth twisted, but he didn’t immediately answer.

“A-and you sat there shaking so bad, and I had no idea what to do,” Pete whispered, holding Gary’s gaze. “I hated all that. I threw up on the walk home because I thought I’d ruined your life. I-I thought I’d blinded you. You don’t know how bad I felt then.”

Gary stared at him again. His fists were still clenched, but his fingers had begun to loosen a bit inside the wet fabric of his pockets. There was definitely some anger there, but something underneath it had shifted.

“I am not your toy, Gary,” Pete said. “I’m not a prop in whatever war you’re always fighting with the school or Jimmy or whoever. I—look, I was your friend before any of this. Before Bullworth, before all of it.” His voice finally broke on the last word, but he didn’t look away. “And you’ve been punishing me for it ever since.”

He knew Pete was right. That was the part he couldn’t stand. It was weird, but even at his worst moments, or after everything he’d done at Bullworth, Pete had kept coming back. And Gary had never quite known what to do with that.

“And I know you suck,” Pete continued, as his voice dropped into more of a murmur. “I know you’re a prick. I know you’re depressed, or off your meds, or whatever the hell is going on in your head that makes you act like a monster. But I kept reaching out and I…” His hands were shaking now. “Because I thought…” Pete stopped and swallowed. “I thought there was still something left of that kid in the woods.”

Gary’s eyes flickered away. Pete stepped closer, as his now wet socks squished against the floor. He was barely a foot away now, just close enough for stray drops from Gary’s hair to strike his cheek. He didn’t wipe them away.

“Do you have any idea how quiet the dorm was?” Pete asked. His voice cracked, and tears finally spilled over. “When you left and people didn’t know where you were? E-Everyone thought it was peaceful, that they’d have to worry about one less psycho in the dorms. But um, it wasn’t really that way, it just felt more empty. You know?”

He looked down at Gary’s soaked hoodie, then back at his face.

“I hated sitting in that room alone. I kept looking at your empty bed and wondering where you were. And I didn’t know if you were freezing to death in some sewer somewhere… and I tried to go after you and you would just keep disappearing…”

Pete tried to reach out. His fingers brushed the soaked fabric of Gary’s sleeve before closing around it.

“Do you even care about me?”

Gary looked down at Pete’s hand, then back at his face.

“Do you care how I feel at all?” Pete asked, crying openly now. “Are we even friends, Gary? Or are you just using me? H-how do I know you’re not keeping me around until you find some better angle to screw Jimmy over?”

Oh no, not this. Gary’s thoughts began colliding with one another, and it was as if each one arrived faster than he could follow it. He saw Pete’s red, tear-streaked face, the agonizing vulnerability in his eyes, the way his fingers were clutching at Gary’s wet arm. It was as though he were begging for a lifeline, but there was none. It was incredible to think that this weak, pathetic, soft-hearted boy, oh, was the only person on the face of the earth who had ever actually cared for him, loved him, whatever those words meant at this point.

It was too much, this was all too much. The walls were closing in, the air was gone, his brain was short-circuiting under the weight of it all. It was bad enough with Jimmy, but with Pete? He couldn’t explain where he’d been, and now here he was, pretending his brain would help him deflect from these concerns again. But once again, he was cornered. He couldn’t explain why he’d come here at five in the morning instead of going anywhere else.

God, he couldn’t even explain why he’d come to Pete at all. And he had no good answer to give.

“SHUT UP!”

The scream tore out of Gary before he could stop it, and he shoved Pete backward. Pete stumbled, catching himself against the edge of the vanity with a gasp. The sound of his elbow hitting the wood seemed impossibly loud in the little bathroom. And then everything went quiet.

Gary froze. As he stood there for a second, he stared at Pete as though he had only just realized where he was. His face drained of color, and his chest rose and fell in quick, ragged breaths. The house, Pete’s parents. How could he have forgotten that!? He looked toward the closed bathroom door. It was as if he could suddenly hear every little sound beyond it.

He listened for footsteps, and to his relief, heard none. But still, his breathing wouldn’t slow. What had he done? He’d shouted, he’d pushed Pete, his clothes were awfully soaked through, there was water all over the floor, God, no, none of this was like him. If Pete’s parents walked in now, Gary wouldn’t even know what he was supposed to tell them.

Eventually, he lowered his head. As his hands closed around the fabric of his hoodie, his fingers trembled as he stared at the tiles. The anger had vanished so quickly that Pete almost couldn’t remember what Gary’s face had looked like a moment earlier. But he looked genuinely embarrassed, maybe even overwhelmed by it.

Pete stayed by the vanity and rubbed the sore spot where he’d hit the edge. That hurt a bit. His chest still hurt, and there was a lingering sting behind his eyes, but the anger that had been burning through him was gone. What was left was mostly exhaustion. Pete wasn’t suddenly going to decide everything was fine… but at this point, he just wasn’t going to get anything he wanted from Gary now. He was in no position to demand anything anymore.

And, more importantly, Pete really didn’t want either of his parents walking in and finding them like this.

He glanced toward the door. Apparently, nobody had heard them or at least, nobody had come running. Not sure how long that would last, Pete let out a slow breath and wiped the remaining tears from his cheeks with the back of his hand.

“Okay,” he whispered, though mostly to himself. He crossed the bathroom to the laundry basket and rummaged through the clean clothes his mother had folded on the shelf the day before. After a moment he pulled out a pair of dark blue flannel pajama pants and a fairly bland gray shirt.

He carried them over and placed them carefully on the closed toilet lid, away from the puddle spreading across the floor.

“The water’s getting cold,” Pete said. “Take those off. These should be clean enough for you.”

There was no response from Gary. He wasn’t even looking at him. Pete looked at Gary’s hunched shoulders, then added quietly, “You can keep the door locked. I’ll make sure nobody comes in.”

Gary’s fingers tightened around his hoodie. Pete waited a moment longer, but there was still no answer. Maybe he shouldn’t have waited in the first place. Instead, he reached past him and turned the shower knobs. The water sputtered, diminished to a thin stream, and finally stopped. The sudden quiet felt quite strange.

Pete stepped toward the door. Could he just say something else now? Maybe apologize for shouting, or ask Gary if he was okay, or maybe try once more to get an answer to the question he’d just asked. Ultimately, he decided against all three. He opened the door and slipped into the hallway, pulling it closed behind him as quietly as he could.

In his bedroom, Pete lay flat on his side, with his face buried deep into his pillow, and his knees pulled up toward his chest. He didn’t reach for his iPod; he might as well have just let it keep playing. As he lay there in the morning light, staring blankly at the walls of his room, he tried his best to not listen to the muffled sounds of the pipes rattling as the bathroom drained.

His eyes burned, his head pounded with a headache, and the cuffs of his pajama sleeves felt cold against his wrists. He felt completely emptied out, as though he’d spent the last hour scraping every thought and feeling out of himself and leaving the mess on the bathroom floor. It was not hard to see why; he had said everything he’d been carrying around for months. He’d admitted how much he missed Gary, sure, and how much he’d worried about him, or how badly he’d wanted their friendship back, and Gary had responded by screaming in his face and shoving him around.

Stupid. You’re so fucking stupid, Pete. Why do you never learn? Pete thought, squeezing his eyes shut as fresh tears prickled beneath his lashes.

He hated himself for even thinking about it. It sounded ridiculous the moment it was in his head, admittedly. But that didn’t make it hurt any less. He pressed his face deeper into the pillow and tried to breathe quietly, determined that if Gary came back into the room, he wasn’t going to let him see him crying again. And so he tried to fall back to sleep.

Ten minutes passed, and then fifteen. Eventually, the creak of the bedroom door broke the silence.

Pete didn’t move an inch. He kept his back to the room, pulling the quilt a little higher over his shoulders and forcing his breathing into a measured enough rhythm. Oh, he was done. He really, really didn’t have the stamina for whatever fresh round of poison Gary felt like dishing out.

Now that he was here, Gary was wearing the blue flannel pajama pants Pete had left for him, which dragged slightly around his ankles, but was good enough. The grey shirt was clearly a disaster, though. Pete had bought it two years ago; the sleeves were pinched tight around Gary’s forearms, the hem stopped two inches above his waistline, and the fabric pulled so taut across his shoulders that Gary looked like a sausage.

“Can’t believe this guy wears clothes for a toddler,” he muttered, something like that, before walking over to Pete’s wooden dresser. He pulled open the top drawer and began rummaging through Pete’s collection of shirts. Surely he had something decent to wear here, right? It was after a moment that Gary pulled out an old, dark navy long-sleeve tee — fortunately, this one would fit. It looked worn, soft, and long enough to actually cover a normal human torso. Whew! And so Gary peeled off the tight grey shirt, tossing it onto the desk chair.

As he stood there bare-chested in the dim light, he caught sight of his reflection in the small mirror mounted on Pete’s wall. Gary froze, and stared at his own face for a second… first at the vaguely yellowish bruise fading on his cheek, then at the pale line of the scar through his eyebrow that Pete had just screamed about, then, slowly, he turned his torso, looking back over his left shoulder into the glass. The morning light from the window caught the uneven skin of his back.

Running diagonally across his shoulder blades and down toward his ribs were two thick, jagged, raised lines of pale pink scar tissue. He could very well say they were identical in texture and color to the ones Jimmy had shown him beneath the streetlamp just hours earlier. Apparently the glass from the skylight hadn’t played favorites…

Gary rolled his eyes at his own reflection, as a scoff escaped his nose, and quickly yanked the dark navy sweater over his head, hiding the marks beneath the worn wool. Much better. Eventually he turned around, and his eyes landed on Pete’s curled-up form in the bed. There he was. His shoulders were completely still, his face buried in the pillow, and his blankets pulled up to his armpits.

One could think he was asleep, but that just wasn’t true. Gary watched him for a long moment. Whatever fury had been keeping him upright in the bathroom had drained out with the shower water, leaving behind a weird-ish, prickly silence that settled in his ribs. He hated it. Guilt was an amateur emotion, the kind of thing weak people used to torture themselves. If anything, it was useless, didn’t fix anything, and only made him feel like an idiot.

Yet as he looked at the trembling curve of Pete’s back, Gary couldn’t really make himself leave. He crossed the room and sat down on the edge of the mattress. Unsurprisingly, Pete didn’t budge. He kept his eyes shut tight, clearly waiting for Gary to take the hint, climb onto the spare mattress on the floor, and pass out. That was so simple, it really was all there was left for him to do.

Gary tilted his head, eyeing the line of Pete’s jaw. A tiny smirk twitched at the corner of his mouth. He had an idea! Slowly, he reached out and poked Pete hard in the center of his ribcage.

Pete flinched, as his shoulder hitched, but kept his face in the pillow. “Stop,” he muttered, voice muffled and flat. Of course, Gary didn’t stop. He poked him again, this time higher up, right under the arm.

“Gary, don’t.”

Having some fun, Gary poked him a third time, finding a soft, and ridiculously ticklish, spot along Pete’s ribs and digging his knuckles in. Turns out having practiced this did make things easier.

“Gary!” Pete gasped, twisting away as an involuntary squeak tore out of him. “Quit it!”

“Thought you were asleep, Petey,” Gary drawled, leaning over him and digging his fingers into Pete’s side. “You see, sleeping beauty, dead people don’t squirm.”

“Stop— dude, get off!” Pete shoved his face into the mattress, fighting the helpless wheeze bubbling up in his chest. He tried to roll onto his stomach to guard himself, but Gary caught him by the shoulder, pinning him down and working his fingers into Pete’s waist until Pete was completely losing it, gasping and kicking at the covers. He was definitely not prepared for this. “I can’t—stop, you bastard, I can’t breathe!”

“Say uncle,” Gary said, grinning now, actually laughing under his breath.

“No, screw you!”

“Oh, say it, or I’m going for your collarbones next.”

“Uncle! Fine— uncle, uncle, get the hell off me!” Pete choked out, flailing until his palm caught Gary’s forearm and shoved him back with everything he had left.

Gary let go, tumbling back against the headboard with a chuckle, and shaking his hair out of his eyes. Who could’ve thought these moments could be so… erm… exciting? Pete collapsed on his side, face bright red, and chest heaving as he pulled air back into his lungs. He pressed the heels of his hands against his ribs, glaring at Gary through a mess of brown hair, but the fight was completely out of him.

Either way, he had Pete’s attention now. Gary sat there for a second, catching his breath. What would he say now, exactly? It’s not like he could just go into a normal conversation with him after what happened earlier. Slowly, his grin faded. He drew his knees up, rested his wrists on his shins, and looked down at the blue flannel of his pants.

He picked at a loose thread near the hem, cleared his throat, and stared at the floor. “I was…” Gary started, the words coming out rough and awkward. He paused for a moment, with his jaw clenching as he forced himself to spit it out. “I was being a prick. Uh, earlier.”

Pete blinked, lowering his hands from his chest. He looked at Gary, caught entirely off guard by whatever he was saying. After all, Gary admitting fault was roughly as common as a solar eclipse. Maybe even rarer. “Yeah,” he murmured, still winded. “I mean, yeah, you were.”

Gary kept his eyes on the floor, eventually pulling the cuffs down over his knuckles until only his fingertips showed. His teeth clicked together, and honestly, he’d rather chew on broken glass than say whatever was backing up in his throat. But it came anyway.

“…I’m sorry, alright?” he muttered. Pete didn’t answer right away, which only seemed to make it worse. Gary shifted a bit, as his shoulders hitched up beneath the wool.

“I was being a shitass,” Gary spat out. “A paranoid, miserable prick. Yeah yeah, laugh all you want. I mean… I come here, I bang on your door at sunrise, drag blood and dirt and shit into your bathroom, scream in your face like a lunatic… it’s pathetic. I’m not like that, I know I’m not like that, right? You should’ve just left me on the porch.” He dragged a hand through his hair, tugging hard at the roots. “You said I always drag you into my shit, but I don’t know what’s wrong with my fucking head half the time, I just—”

“Gary, shut up.”

Gary froze, his hand still tangled in his hair.

“Stop beating yourself up,” Pete said. He was propped up on one elbow now, sounding far more tired than angry. “Seriously, quit it. I wasn’t even mad about the yelling, I just… didn’t want my dad coming in here with a golf club.”

This was half-true, though he would never say that to his face. If there was one thing Pete didn’t like, it was having his friends drag themselves down. It was bad enough with Jimmy during that time he got expelled, and it was much worse seeing it with Gary.

Gary let out a short puff of air, as his hand dropped back to his lap. “Yeah. Uh, well.”

The room fell quiet again. By that point, grey morning light was beginning to bleed around the edges of the window shade, washing out the warm yellow of the bedside lamp.

Pete looked at his own hands resting against the quilt. He knew he should let it go. He was running on two hours of broken sleep, his eyes felt full of sand, and pushing Gary even further would end up badly for him. But there was one thought that had been rattling around in his chest all week… or longer, actually, and he was too exhausted to filter it out.

“You know…” Pete started quietly, picking at a loose knot in the blanket. “I still don’t really get why it had to go down like this.”

Gary didn’t move. “Like what?”

“Any of it.” Pete looked up, catching Gary’s profile against the wall. “I really— I mean, look, before everything went completely off the rails. If you, me, and Jimmy had just… actually hung out. Just the three of us, y’know, we could’ve had fun, Gary. Real fun, actual fun, or um, at least we wouldn’t all be sitting around this summer being so goddamn miserable.”

Gary’s head snapped toward him. “Hopkins? Are you seriously doing this crap again? You think Jimmy-boy wanted to be friends? He showed up looking for a fight, Pete. Why are you so in denial about this? Come on, he was gunning for me from day one.”

“He wasn’t gunning for anyone!” Pete shot back, and his voice rose just enough to make him glance nervously toward the hallway before dropping it again. “I liked hanging out with you. I wanted you around. I still do, but—hey, Jimmy didn’t care about your throne, or the school, or whatever war you thought you were fighting. He didn’t care about you at all until you made him care. That’s what I’ve been trying to say, all this time.”

Gary opened his mouth to snap something vicious, but the argument died behind his teeth. He stared at Pete for a little moment. Between Pete’s exhausted (but honestly, kind of scary!) glare and the unfortunately humiliating memory of Jimmy’s hand on his back just a few hours ago, Gary had run completely out of ammunition. This was all getting so repetitive, but he looked cornered, maybe even smaller somehow in Pete’s father’s clothes, and his fingers curled tight into the flannel of his borrowed pants.

Letting out a slow breath, Pete rubbed his thumb across the bridge of his nose. “I think the three of us…” he hesitated, the words feeling way too ridiculous to say out loud. Ah, of course they were. “We need to, uh, bury the hatchet. For real-real.”

Gary blinked at him, completely unmoving, as if waiting for the punchline to hit. It would’ve been easier than trying to understand the idea he was insinuating. This had to be a joke of some sort, and a terrible one at it. He’d always been a bad joker. He wouldn’t stand an hour if someone put him on on of those fancy stand-up comedy shows, surely.

But Pete pushed ahead before Gary could snap at him. “Not some big, formal thing, okay? Just… one night. W-we wait until it gets dark so nobody’s watching, we, I don’t know, grab some burgers or street food in town, and we find somewhere to chill. You know Jimmy’s got that beat-up beach house out by the lighthouse… it’s quiet, nobody’s out there, and so we can just, well, you know. Sit around, eat, talk…?”

Gary’s mouth fell open slightly, and his expression was somewhere between sheer disgust and mild nausea. It just— it couldn’t make sense. They would bake cookies, have tea, act like nothing happened?

“I promise nothing’s gonna happen to you,” Pete added quickly, leaning forward. “I swear, Gary. I’ll be right there the whole time. It’ll be fine, really. And if it gets weird, we just walk out. That’s it.”

And Gary’s brain seemed to stall out entirely the more Pete went on. Great. Here Gary was, thinking The Head Boy could solve all his problems, with his half-baked and lackadaisical solutions. When he inevitably returned to school, he’d also have to deal with Pete’s new ridiculous attire, which at worst would make him look like the school hobo, and at best would make him look like that vice principal from Ned’s Declassified. But maybe he had no other choice at this point, he’d tried out all possible avenues.

He stared at Pete in dead silence, and his brow furrowed so hard it nearly pulled the scar above his eye tight. For five seconds, nothing happened. Then, with a slow, almost theatrical deliberateness, Gary planted his face into both hands, as his fingers dug into his temples, thinking he could massage the conversation out of his skull. He could not.

A muffled, agonized groan tore out from behind his palms. This was a trap, this had to be one. He knew that Jimmy would laugh in his face, that they would fight, that he would be humiliated all over again. This was a trap. Right? Right!? Was it…!?

“I hate you,” Gary groaned into his hands. “I genuinely, truly hate your guts.”

Pete looked at him through a tangle of brown hair. A faint, tired smile finally cracked across his face. “So you agree?”

“Fuck you, Petey.” Gary dragged his hands down, and glared at him through his fingers with a look of defeat. God, help me. What the fuck have I gotten myself into?

Letting out a quiet laugh, Pete finally dropped his head back onto the pillows.

“Deal.”
