It was strange, but the taxidermy moose head mounted just above the stone fireplace almost looked impressive. At least, it did until you noticed one of its glass eyes had been glued in just a little crooked. After that, it was impossible to see anything else.

Dolph had spent what felt like the better part of an hour staring at that left eye. The poor thing looked like it’d forgotten why it’d walked into the room. “Poor guy,” Dolph thought.

Luckily, the moose wasn’t much of a problem. The cabin was warm enough to make Dolph feel pleasantly heavy, and Mr. Black’s private stash had done the rest. His thoughts drifted somewhere far away from the expensive log cabin. In his head, he was floating over Springfield, watching the glow of the tire fire and the blue lights of Chief Wiggum’s cruiser spin lazily in the dark.

A sharp clink of silver against porcelain shattered his trance.

Dolph blinked, as the fog in his brain cleared just enough for his vision to snap back into focus. He rubbed at one eye, pushing his dark fringe out of his face as he looked across the mahogany coffee table.

“Hey,” he mumbled, trying to concentrate, but also trying very hard to sound sober. “Pass… pass the truffles, Jimbo.”

Jimbo just snorted. He was sprawled across a deep leather armchair, and his boots were resting on a stack of pristine corporate retreat brochures. An unlabeled green bottle hung loosely from one hand. Dolph didn’t know what it was, but whatever was inside, it probably cost more than anything Jimbo had ever accidentally broken.

“Truffles?” Jimbo replied, squinting at a small jar with a fancy, gold-embossed label. “Man, I still don’t even know what a truffle is. I thought they were those little chocolate things my mom gets from her boyfriends whenever they screw something up. But… if this stuff comes out of a jar with French writing, it probably costs half a billion, so you bet your ass we’re eating it.”

He rolled the glass jar across the table. It clattered against a plate of semi-hard imported cheeses that smelled horrible, to say the least, but tasted incredibly rich and buttery.

“Amen, Mr. Black,” Dolph muttered, as a lazy, quite lopsided grin spread across his face. He caught the jar, unscrewed the lid with a bit of a struggle, and used a sterling silver oyster fork — which obviously didn’t belong to any of them, they’d never have enough money to buy one — to scoop a small mound of these rich delicacies onto a cracker. “Thank God the bastard’s up there in Utica. Hopefully his stuff keeps him occupied forever. Or at least until we drink every bottle of this stuff…”

“Heh, amen,” Jimbo agreed, raising his bottle in a salute before taking a long, dramatic swig.

He felt the alcohol was different tonight. Usually, when they managed to get drunk back in Springfield, or close enough to it, it was on warm-ish, metallic-tasting cans of Duff they’d swipe from the back of the Kwik-E-Mart (or its generic alternatives), or sometimes whatever sugary drinks they’d find in dumpsters. God, that stuff made your throat burn and your stomach churn like a washing machine. But this wine, though, it was quite smooth. Fruity, and expensive too. It also made the leather chairs they were in feel softer than a pile of mattresses. Not even a pea could ruin that moment. It was fancy.

“We’re living the good life, man,” Jimbo said, leaning his head back and watching the orange light of the fire dance across the timber rafters. “Even if it’s just for once, really. I mean look at this place. It’s got a fireplace, a bear rug, and… uh… a fridge. It can’t get any better than this.”

“Yeah,” Dolph said, taking a bite of the cracker. The salty, buttery flavor lingered on his tongue, making him close his eyes for a moment. “It’s like those houses they show on those late-night infomercials. The ones where like… some guy with a fake tan tries to sell you a timeshare in the Bahamas or something. I’d like that.”

Jimbo leaned forward, his elbows resting on his knees, as his eyes drifted toward the window. Outside, beyond the tidy little clearing that surrounded their executive cabin, lay the dark, sprawling nightmare of Kamp Krusty. They were on the only nice patch of land for miles. Outside the clearing were muddy trails, broken cabins, and hundreds of miserable campers trying to survive another week.

By day, they were the enforcers, that is, the guys who stood by with whips and whistles while the campers assembled knockoff leather wallets for export, or ran through countless obstacle courses. By night, the kids were left to huddle together under scratchy burlap blankets that Krusty had, reportedly, bought surplus from a closed-down military prison.

“You know,” Jimbo said, as his tone dropped, losing some of its more cocky edge. “Everyone else at this camp is totally screwed up.”

Dolph stopped chewing. He stared at his plate, where a smear of rich, red wine sauce was slowly congealing next to some cracker crumbs. The light from the fireplace flickered across his face, highlighting the dark circles under his eyes.

“Yeah,” Dolph said. It was a soft, rather shy response. He didn’t look up, and his voice was barely louder than the crackle of the burning birch.

A heavy tension settled over the room. Of course, they both knew what they were doing. They weren’t just camp counselors; they were the muscle of this entire operation! After all, they were the ones who confiscated care packages and replaced homemade cookies with Krusty Brand Imitation Gruel™ (now with 10% more shredded newspaper for fiber!), among other things. Hell, they were the ones who kept the kids from climbing the barbed-wire fences. In the harsh sunlight, when they were angry, hungry, and acting out the roles Springfield had written for them, it was easy to be mean. It was second nature. But here, with full stomachs and warm feet, it was just as easy for the guilt to find a way to creep in through the cracks.

Sensing that the conversation was sliding into territory neither of them had the vocabulary to survive, Jimbo cleared his throat and gestured toward the cast-iron skillet resting on the trivet near the hearth.

“Y’know, I still can’t believe we didn’t burn the place down cooking those steaks,” Jimbo said, throwing a grin to break the ice. “I mean, seriously. We actually cooked them. With the butter and the garlic and that green stuff. What was the green stuff?”

“Rosemary?”

“Ah, yeah, I think it’s rosemary. Smells so good, uh, yeah.”

“Hm, yeah,” Dolph laughed, the tension easing out of his shoulders. “I never thought we’d be decent cooks. Usually, my dinner is just microwaving a burrito until the edges turn into plastic, and the middle is still a block of ice, or well, just eating cold pork and beans straight out of the can with whatever clean piece of plastic I can find.”

“Heh, my cooking specialty is what I call the, um ‘radiator pizza,’” Jimbo said. “I need a better name for it. Anyway, you take a piece of white bread, squeeze some ketchup on it, put a slice of American cheese on top, and you throw it in the toaster oven until the cheese bubbles. Bam! If you’re lucky, you find some pepperoni under the couch cushions. But this stuff, though…” He gestured at the skillet. “This is like something off Lifestyles of the Rich and Famous or some shit.”

“Oh yeah,” Dolph grinned, tugging at the edge of his hood. “I’m sure any second now Robin Leach is gonna pop out from behind the curtain talking about his own, uh, champagne wishes and caviar dreams.”

Jimbo snorted. “Instead we got Mr. Black’s leftovers.”

Eventually, they got back to eating. They tore off chunks of a crusty baguette, using them to swipe up the remaining garlic butter and steak juices from the skillet. It was the closest thing either of them had ever had to a normal dinner. Back home, dinner used to be either a battlefield or some sort of silent affair in front of the TV, which most of the time only showed static. Here, for a few hours, they had a sanctuary.

After another half-hour, the air inside the cabin began to feel thick and stuffy. Between the fire, the heavy meal, and the wine, they were growing sleepy, even if neither of them felt quite ready for bed. The warmth was almost too intense, making their skin feel hot and itchy.

“Hey, let’s go check on Kearney,” Jimbo suggested, tossing his empty wine bottle into a trash can, where it landed with a loud, hollow clatter. “He’s been down at the lake for like, hours now.”

“Is he still trying to catch something?” Dolph asked, wiping his fingers on a linen napkin that was embroidered with the Camp Krusty® logo.

“Yeah. Said he wanted to catch a giant bass, show those little camp twerps how a real man gets his dinner. What a romantic…” Jimbo stood up, swaying slightly as his brain tried to catch up with his feet. He grabbed his purple knit beanie from the coffee table and pulled it down over his greasy, unwashed hair. He also slipped on his worn green vest over his black t-shirt, checking his pockets for his lighter.

Dolph stood up too, feeling the full weight of the wine hit his knees. Jesus. His head spun for a second, and he had to place a hand on the back of the leather chair to steady himself. He grabbed his oversized dark hoodie, and pulled the drawstring tight, leaving only a small window for his face.

They unlocked the timber door and pushed it open. The warm, if, erm, decadent atmosphere of the cabin was instantly ripped away, replaced by a much more aggressive gust of wind. It wasn’t winter, exactly; it was the dead of summer, but some places in Springfield were notorious for their freezing night drafts. As the wind howled through the valley, the surrounding pines also groaned and swayed, with their dark needles rustling like dry paper.

And the sky was pitch black, devoid of stars, and choked only by a thick blanket of low-hanging clouds.

“Man, it’s brutal out here,” Dolph muttered, shoving his hands deep into his hoodie pockets and hunching his shoulders against the wind. The cold air hit his face like a slap, instantly clearing some of the wine-induced fog from his brain, though it replaced it with a dull ache behind his temples.

“Ah, builds character. That’s what the brochure said, right?” Jimbo joked, though he was shivering too as they stepped off the porch and onto the muddy path.

They walked down the winding trail toward the water, as the beams of their cheap flashlights cut weak, yellow arcs through the darkness. The woods around them felt very vast and unfriendly, at least to Dolph. And in the distance, the silent, dark-ish shapes of the campers’ cabins looked like nothing more than abandoned chicken coops. There were no lights, no warmth, and seemingly no sounds other than the wind. The fact Mr. Black cut the generator at eight sharp to save on diesel didn’t help their case; Dolph wasn’t quite sure when Kearney had turned off theirs.

The two made their way down to the shoreline. The lake was a vast, black void, and its surface whipped into choppy, white-capped waves by the gale. The sound of the water lapping aggressively against the shore was loud, almost deafening. And at the end of the half-rotten wooden dock sat Kearney.

He was a strange sight. He was wearing his trademark white shirt, no surprise here, but he had also draped a ragged, oversized red flannel shirt over his shoulders like a cape to keep the wind off. He was sitting on an upturned, rusted five-gallon bucket, and holding a fishing rod loosely in his dirt-caked hands. A single, battery-powered lantern also sat beside him, casting a white light over his shaved head and the metal tackle box at his feet.

To their relief, Kearney had actually already eaten. Earlier in the evening, while Jimbo and Dolph were still debating how to light the fancy stove, Kearney had taken a massive, pre-cooked ham from the storage, hacked off a chunk the size of a football with his pocketknife, and eaten it cold. He had washed it down with two or three cans of cheap beer he’d found in the camp kitchen’s pantry, and declared himself satisfied. He was an odd guy.

“Any bites, Ahab?” Jimbo called out, with his voice competing with the wind as they walked out onto the creaking planks of the dock. The wood groaned under their weight, and the dock swayed slightly with the motion of the choppy waves.

Kearney didn’t look back. “Keep it down. You’ll spook the fish.”

“Like there’s any fish in this dump anyway,” Dolph said, stepping up beside him. He looked down into the black water. He couldn’t see anything past the reflection of the lantern light. “You get anything?”

Kearney grunted; it was a low, rumbling sound. He slowly reeled in his line. The plastic bobber broke the surface with a splash, and to no surprise, it was empty. “Nah, not even a nibble. I think the fish in this lake are too depressed to bite. Either that or they’re all sleeping. Or dead. Maybe both, I dunno.”

“Yeah,” Jimbo said, shrugging. He leaned against the wooden railing of the dock, which creaked alarmingly. He pulled out a crumpled pack of Laramie cigarettes from his pocket, lit one after three failed attempts against the wind, and took a long drag. The tip glowed a bright, angry orange in the dark. “It’s pretty dead out here.”

Their enthusiasm, which had been buoyed by the warmth and the fancy food in the cabin, was quickly watering down. The cold wind was biting through their thin jackets, and the sheer emptiness of the lake at night was starting to feel more oppressive than anything. The wind also seemed to carry the faint, distant sounds of the camp, and ironically, it was a stark reminder of the reality they were ignoring.

Dolph wrapped his arms around himself, as his fingers dug into the fabric of his sleeves. He looked out across the lake, toward the far shore where the dark silhouette of the mountains rose against a starless sky.

“Still,” Dolph said quietly, his voice barely carrying over the rustle of the wind. “It’s good to, um, spend time with you guys. You know… out of some of the town’s chaos.”

Jimbo looked at him, as the cherry of his cigarette glowed bright red in the dark. He took another drag. “Yeah. Springfield’s a toilet. Always someone breathing down your neck. The cops, the principal… those old ladies who yell at you just for standing on the sidewalk. Well, at least out here, nobody’s telling us what to do.”

“And the chaos at home,” Dolph added, his voice dropping even lower.

Jimbo’s eyes softened, albeit just a fraction. He knew what Dolph meant. Jimbo’s own home was a revolving door of his mother’s boyfriends, empty pizza boxes, and also several screaming matches that lasted until three in the morning. Half the time, he slept on the couch because some guy he’d never met was passed out in his bed, or sometimes because his mom was too drunk to realize he was even home. He’d learned early on that the easiest way to deal with home was to simply not be there. That was why they spent so much time wandering the streets, throwing rocks at train cars, and stealing from the comic book shop.

Well, at least the comic book shop part was fun.

“Yeah,” Jimbo said, exhaling a cloud of gray smoke that was instantly torn away by the wind. “I can relate to that, a lot.”

Kearney didn’t say anything either, but he spat into the lake, as his shoulders tensed slightly under his flannel shirt. His life wasn’t any easier, unfortunately. He was already a father, trying to raise a toddler while still being a kid himself, living in a cramped, run-down apartment with parents who barely acknowledged his existence unless they wanted him to pay for the cable bill. They were three boys who had been forced to grow up too fast, yet were still treated like garbage by everyone in authority. Here, they were the authority, which seemed exciting at first, but it felt hollow. It was just a different kind of trap.

And so Dolph closed his eyes.

The wind whipped his hair across his forehead. In the darkness behind his eyelids, as the cold smell of the lake began to fade, it was replaced by a more desperate warmth. He tried to imagine something else.

He imagined a house. A real house, like the ones in the nice part of Springfield, where the lawns were a ridiculous shade of vomit green, and the sprinklers made clicking noises in the afternoon. He imagined a dining room table made of real wood, not one of those folding card tables, and he imagined a mother who was actually smiling, holding a plate of fresh cookies just for him… and a father who didn’t smell like beer, who actually wanted to hear about his day…

So he imagined a birthday. He had never really had a real birthday party, to be fair; usually, it was just a half-eaten cake his mom bought on clearance from the grocery store, or nothing at all. But in his mind, he saw a cake. It was chocolate, with thick, messy frosting, and it had eleven, maybe twelve, bright, burning candles on it. His friends were there, Jimbo and Kearney, and they were laughing and having fun. They were just kids.

In his mind, Dolph took a deep breath. He leaned forward and blew. He blew out the imaginary candles, as he made a silent, if rather desperate, wish. Just let things be different. God, let us be normal.

But as the last imaginary flame flickered out in his mind, the reality of the lake wind hit him hard, stinging his cheeks. He opened his eyes. The black water of the lake was still there, so choppy and cold, and the rusty dock still creaked beneath his sneakers. Jimbo was still smoking, looking tired and old beyond his years, and Kearney was still staring blankly at a fishing line that was, honestly, never going to catch anything.

Dolph’s brow furrowed. Nothing had changed. A heavy frown settled onto his face, pulling the corners of his mouth down. The brief illusion of escape he’d been in had evaporated, leaving behind only a hollow unhappiness that felt, to him, much heavier than the wind. He hated how quickly this fantasy disappeared, and how easily the cold rushed back to fill the empty space.

“This sucks,” Kearney suddenly said, pulling his line completely out of the water with a sharp jerk of his rod. “I’m freezing my balls off, and these fish are clearly smarter than us. I’m going back.”

“Yeah,” Jimbo agreed, tossing his cigarette butt into the water. It hissed briefly before disappearing into the dark waves. “Let’s get out of here. The wind is giving me a headache, and uh, I think the wine is wearing off.”

They turned around and walked back down the dock, as their boots clattered loudly on the wood. The walk back to the cabin sure felt much longer.

When they opened the heavy wooden door, the cabin felt almost tropical compared to the wind outside. At that point, the fire had burned down to glowing embers, and the room still smelled faintly of garlic butter, smoke, and wine. Nobody complained as they stripped off their damp coats and hoodies and threw them over the nearest chairs.

“I’m taking the bed,” Kearney announced, not even waiting for an argument. He tossed his fishing rod onto the floor and headed into the small, carpeted back room of the lodge. The bed in there was actually Mr. Black’s personal king-size mattress, complete with goose-down pillows and a rather thick, velvet duvet. As Kearney was the biggest, and he had eaten his weight in cold ham, neither Jimbo nor Dolph was about to challenge him for it.

“Yeah, yeah, whatever,” Jimbo called after him. “Just don’t drool on the fancy sheets, shitass.”

A grunt from the back room was Kearney’s only reply, followed by the creak of the mattress as he threw his bulk onto it. Within minutes, the deep sound of his snoring began to echo through the cabin. And so, Jimbo and Dolph were left in the main room.

The cabin had two large, plush leather couches positioned on either side of the fireplace. They weren’t beds, unfortunately, but they were incredibly comfortable — certainly better than the rusty cots they had back at their own counselor quarters, and infinitely better than the damp ground the campers were sleeping on. Maybe this was how rich people slept.

Dolph walked over to the left couch. He pulled a thick, hand-woven wool blanket off the back of a rocking chair and spread it out. He lay down, his body sinking into the soft leather. The wine made his limbs feel like lead, but he would have been exhausted either way. And Jimbo took the other couch, throwing himself down with a sigh. He pulled a heavy plaid blanket up to his chin.

“Hey, Dolph?” Jimbo said quietly, as his voice echoed softly in the room. The high ceiling made his voice sound smaller than usual.

“Yeah?”

“We supposed to do that ‘trench digging’ activity with the kids at six tomorrow? I think it’s on that checklist on the wall.”

They were, in fact. Dolph stared at the ceiling; the shadows of the embers were dancing like ghosts, and it was vaguely fascinating. “Yeah. Section… uh… four, right after morning roll call.”

“I mean… Black’s not even here,” Jimbo muttered, shifting beneath his stolen wool blanket. “We don’t even know when the guy is coming back, if he ever does. So um, we could just sleep in until noon, skip the trench, and let those little runts eat dry oats out of the trough.”

Dolph sighed, although most of the sound was muffled by his pillow. “Yeah, we could. But I mean, if we don’t keep them doing something, they’re gonna realize we’re completely outnumbered. You saw the look on that kid Simpson’s face today during the wallet-stamping. He was staring at the perimeter fence the whole time. Just think about it…”

“True,” Jimbo admitted, as his eyelids grew heavy. “If we let up for even half a day, those little maniacs are gonna riot and burn the place down. We gotta maintain some kind of discipline, y’know, just to keep the lid on the pot. Otherwise, we’re the ones who’ll end up locked in the cabin.”

“So we still gotta get up,” Dolph said.

“Yeah, at six. I hope you’re prepared to yell at them a lot to, well, make sure they think we still care.”

“I am, yeah.”

“But it’s gonna suck.”

“Yeah,” Dolph agreed.

There was a long pause. Outside, the wind sighed against the windows, making the glass tremble every now and then. Inside, though, the fire was little more than a bed of orange coals, quietly giving off the last of its warmth.

“But tonight was cool,” Jimbo added, his voice growing faint as sleep began to take over. “The food… and all that stuff.”

Dolph let out a slow sigh, as his shoulders finally relaxed into the cushions. It felt as though he’d been carrying something heavy all his life, and for one evening he’d been allowed to set it down. He pulled the blanket a little higher over his shoulder.

“Yeah,” he said quietly. “It was.”

He closed his eyes again, but this time, he didn’t try to imagine anything else. As Dolph let himself sink into the warmth of the couch, listening to the wind outside, he tried to pretend for a little while that he was safe.
